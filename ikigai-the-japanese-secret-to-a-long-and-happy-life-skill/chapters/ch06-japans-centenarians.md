# Chapter VI: Lessons from Japan’s Centenarians

## Core Idea

This chapter reports the authors' visit to Ogimi and their interviews with residents, focusing on the social setting in which long life is lived. The town's residents participate in neighborhood groups, volunteer work, festivals, games, gardens, and regular celebrations; many remain useful and connected rather than withdrawing from community life. The authors notice that food is only one part of the picture: smiling, shared activity, traditions, and a sense of belonging are repeatedly emphasized. The practical lesson is to make social participation and usefulness ordinary features of the week.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Community as family**: Build recurring social structures that provide care, activity, and belonging.
  - **When to use**: When health or purpose is being treated as a private project.
  - **How**: Join or form groups with shared activities, contribution, and regular contact.
- **Celebrate each day together**: Use play, birthdays, festivals, and shared rituals to keep life socially vivid.
  - **When to use**: When days have become only obligations and maintenance.
  - **How**: Put celebration and low-impact activity on the calendar.

## Key Concepts

- **Ogimi**: The Okinawan village the authors call the Village of Longevity.
- **Yuimaaru**: Teamwork and mutual help.
- **Moai**: A close support group.
- **Gateball**: A low-impact group game played by older residents.
- **Volunteer usefulness**: Contributing time and labor without reducing value to money.

## Mental Models

- Design belonging through repeated contact, not occasional social rescue.
- Ask “where can I be useful?” alongside “what can I receive?”
- Use play and celebration as health-supporting social practices, not frivolous extras.

## Anti-patterns

- **Isolation disguised as independence**: The chapter treats close ties as part of a healthy life.
- **Making usefulness equal paid work**: Ogimi's volunteering shows contribution can take other forms.
- **Waiting for a special event to connect**: Community in the chapter is woven into ordinary routines.

## Worked Example

The authors attend a neighborhood birthday gathering, play gateball, and observe associations that organize culture, social activities, and longevity. The youngest member of one group is already in advanced old age, yet the group behaves as a lively social unit rather than a care facility. This shows how activity, ritual, humor, and belonging reinforce one another. The transferable practice is a recurring group calendar with roles and shared enjoyment.

## Key Takeaways

1. Put recurring social contact on the calendar.
2. Contribute through volunteering, care, teaching, gardening, or local activity.
3. Build celebration and play into ordinary life.
4. Treat belonging as infrastructure for purpose and resilience.
