# Ikigai — Glossary

**Antifragility** — A condition in which a system becomes stronger after manageable shocks, beyond merely resisting or surviving them (Ch 9).

**Blue Zones** — Geographic regions discussed as having unusually high longevity, used in the book to compare diet, movement, purpose, and social ties (Ch 1, Ch 5).

**Flow** — Deep absorption in an appropriately challenging activity, marked by focused attention, feedback, altered time sense, and reduced distraction (Ch 4).

**Hara hachi bu** — An Okinawan eating reminder to stop when beginning to feel full, roughly treating the stomach as 80 percent full (Ch 1, Ch 7).

**Ichi-go ichi-e** — The idea that a particular encounter exists only once and should be appreciated in the present (Ch 9).

**Ikigai** — A reason for being or reason to get up, which may be found in work, craft, relationships, service, or ordinary daily activity (Prologue, Ch 1, Ch 3).

**Logotherapy** — Viktor Frankl’s meaning-centered approach, presented as helping people discover purpose in life (Prologue, Ch 3).

**Microflow** — A small state of engagement created by adding care, order, or a modest challenge to a routine task (Ch 4).

**Moai** — A close-knit group in Okinawa that provides companionship, shared activity, and mutual support (Ch 1, Ch 6).

**Morita therapy** — A Japanese therapeutic approach discussed through stages of rest, observation, simple tasks, and purposeful activity (Ch 3).

**Naikan** — A reflective practice centered on considering what one has received, what one has given, and what difficulties one has caused others (Ch 3).

**Radio taiso** — A short group exercise routine used in Japan as an example of accessible daily movement (Ch 8).

**Resilience** — The ability to withstand difficulty and recover without being ruled by stress, fear, or resentment (Ch 9).

**Ritual over goals** — The preference for clear repeatable processes that make a large purpose actionable in the present (Ch 4).

**Wabi-sabi** — Appreciation of the beauty of the imperfect, incomplete, fleeting, and changing (Ch 9).

**Yuimaaru** — Okinawan teamwork and mutual-help spirit described in the prologue (Prologue).
