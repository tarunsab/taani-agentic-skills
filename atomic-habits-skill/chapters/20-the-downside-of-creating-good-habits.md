# Chapter 20 — The Downside of Creating Good Habits

## Core lesson

Automaticity saves effort but can hide small errors and encourage unexamined repetition. A behavior can become easy to perform while quietly drifting away from the quality or outcome that originally mattered. Mastery requires habits plus deliberate practice, because repetition supplies consistency while focused correction improves the underlying skill. Reflection and review keep performance conscious and allow the identity or system to evolve. The aim is selective automaticity: automate the routine parts, but preserve deliberate attention where quality, judgment, or growth matters.

## Use it

Schedule periodic reviews: inspect results, identify drift, test a correction, and decide whether the habit still serves the larger aim. Compare consistency with quality, because completing the behavior more often does not prove that the method remains effective. Keep the routine automatic where it helps, but deliberately examine the parts where quality matters or where small errors compound. Use feedback from the task, another person, or a measurable outcome to prevent self-assessment from becoming purely subjective. Update the identity and system when the person has genuinely outgrown the old version.

## Playbook

Begin by separating the behavior's repeatable shell from the skill elements that require attention. Keep the shell easy to cue and execute, then reserve focused practice for the current edge of ability. Use reflection to identify one recurring error and one adjustment rather than generating a broad list of flaws. Review the system at a deliberate interval so that habit strength does not become an excuse to stop thinking. If the identity no longer fits the user's growth, revise it without discarding the useful evidence accumulated through the habit.

1. Separate consistency from quality.
2. Use feedback to find recurring errors.
3. Practice at the edge of ability with focused correction.
4. Revisit identity assumptions after growth or a change in context.

## Pitfall

Clinging to an identity because it once helped can make a useful history feel like a permanent boundary. Measuring repetition while ignoring whether the behavior remains effective rewards automaticity instead of mastery. Reflection can also become an occasional emotional review that never leads to a concrete correction. Keep the review tied to evidence, choose a specific adjustment, and return to practice. Good habits should reduce needless effort while leaving enough attention for learning and change.
