# Chapter 4 — The Man Who Didn’t Look Right

## Core lesson

Practice teaches the brain to recognize cues and act with less conscious attention. This automaticity saves mental energy, but it also makes a routine feel as if it is happening by itself. Once the pattern is familiar, the person may notice the result without noticing the sequence that produced it. Automaticity is useful for execution, but it becomes a liability when it hides a behavior that needs to change. Change begins by making the current pattern visible before deciding whether it is helpful, harmful, or neutral. Awareness creates an intervention point; it does not require self-criticism to be effective.

## Tools

- **Habits Scorecard:** list the ordinary sequence of actions in a day and mark each as helpful, harmful, or neutral relative to the present aim.
- **Pointing-and-Calling:** name the action and its consequence out loud or in writing before doing it. Verbalizing the choice interrupts autopilot.

The Scorecard is most useful when it records what actually happens rather than what the person wishes happened. “Check messages” or “put off the task” is more actionable than a judgment such as “be unproductive.” Pointing-and-Calling adds a small pause between cue and response by turning an automatic action into a stated choice. The purpose is not to narrate every movement forever, but to make a consequential pattern observable long enough to redesign it. Use the least burdensome awareness method that reveals the first point where the routine can change.

## Use it

Start with observation rather than immediate self-criticism. Record the sequence close to the moment it occurs, because retrospective accounts often compress several cues into one vague explanation. Look for the first cue and the decisive moment in the chain; that is often easier to change than the final behavior. Also note what the behavior seems to promise and what it actually delivers afterward. A neutral description gives the user more options than a character judgment does, because it points toward a design problem that can be tested.

## Pitfall

Trying to change an unnamed behavior leaves the intervention too abstract to test. Awareness is not the whole solution, but without it the system is hard to redesign. Another failure is to turn the Scorecard into a daily prosecution, which can make accurate observation less likely. Record the cue, action, and immediate payoff in ordinary language, then choose one link to modify. The goal is enough clarity to act differently, not perfect surveillance of the self.
