# Atomic Habits cheatsheet

## The short version

Design the cue, make the behavior appealing, remove effort, and provide an immediate sense of success. These four levers address different points in the loop, so the right intervention depends on where the behavior is failing. For an unwanted behavior, reverse each step, beginning with the cue that makes it visible and ending with the consequence that makes repetition less attractive. Use the shortest intervention that changes the next decision, then review whether the behavior and outcome actually improve.

## Build a habit

Start with one observable behavior rather than a broad outcome. The minimum version should be small enough to complete when motivation is ordinary or low, while still acting as a credible vote for the desired identity. Give it a reliable cue, make the first step convenient, and add immediate evidence that completion happened. Expand only after repetitions are stable enough to provide useful feedback.

1. **Name the vote:** “I am becoming a person who ___.”
2. **Make it obvious:** write an implementation intention or attach it to an existing routine.
3. **Make it attractive:** pair it with something enjoyable or join a group where it is normal.
4. **Make it easy:** prepare the environment and shrink the starting action to two minutes.
5. **Make it satisfying:** record completion or add a small immediate reward.
6. **Recover:** never let one missed repetition become two when the next opportunity arrives.

## Break a habit

Treat the unwanted behavior as a loop with a cue, an expected benefit, an easy response, and an immediate reward. Remove or hide the cue before asking the user to resist it, then address the underlying need so that a replacement has a chance to compete. Add friction or a commitment device when the response remains too easy, and make the cost of repeating it immediate and credible. If the behavior returns, diagnose the link that failed instead of escalating shame or complexity.

1. Make the cue invisible.
2. Reframe the behavior so its cost is salient and its alternative is attractive.
3. Add friction, delay, or a commitment device.
4. Add an immediate consequence or accountability.

## Failure modes

These failures usually indicate a design problem before they indicate a character problem. A vague goal leaves no behavior to cue, while a large minimum makes the cue predict effort and avoidance. A tracking system can also become a proxy that rewards visible activity without improving the real outcome. Use the list as a quick diagnosis, then change the smallest relevant part of the system and observe another repetition.

- Vague goal with no observable behavior.
- Depending on motivation while leaving cues and friction unchanged.
- Planning and researching instead of repeating the behavior.
- Making the habit too large to survive a difficult day.
- Tracking a proxy that distracts from the real result.
- Treating a lapse as proof of a fixed identity.
- Continuing a habit automatically after it has stopped serving the larger aim.

## Review questions

Review after enough repetitions to reveal a pattern, not after one unusually good or bad day. Ask what happened at the earliest point of failure and whether the reward matched the behavior the user wants to repeat. If the system is consistent but quality or outcomes are stagnant, add deliberate practice or revise the measure. The review should end with one specific adjustment, a reason for it, and the next opportunity to test it.

- Did the cue appear when expected?
- Was the first step small and easy enough?
- What reward was the behavior actually promising?
- Which environmental change would remove the most friction?
- What happened after a missed day?
- Is the habit still aligned with the identity and outcome that matter?
