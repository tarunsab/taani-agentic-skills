# Glossary

These definitions are deliberately concise, but each term should carry enough distinction to prevent a familiar phrase from becoming a vague slogan. Use the glossary to clarify what a concept means before applying it to a personal situation. The definitions describe the role a term plays in the behavior system, not a guarantee that the technique will work in every context. When a term needs a practical decision or example, follow its related chapter note rather than expanding the glossary into an essay.

- **Atomic habit:** A small behavior that belongs to a larger system and compounds through repetition. Its value comes from how reliably it contributes to a pattern, not from how impressive one repetition looks.
- **Behavior loop:** The cue–craving–response–reward sequence that explains how a habit operates. Mapping all four parts helps distinguish what starts a behavior from what reinforces it.
- **Cue:** A signal that predicts an opportunity, outcome, or reward. Cues can be objects, times, places, emotions, people, or actions that precede the response.
- **Craving:** The anticipated change in state that makes a response appealing. It is the desired feeling or result behind the surface behavior, not necessarily the object itself.
- **Response:** The action taken, constrained by ability and friction. A response must be possible in the user's current context before motivation can matter.
- **Reward:** The immediate benefit that teaches the brain whether to repeat the loop. Delayed outcomes may matter, but the immediate reward often determines what is learned first.
- **Outcome change:** Changing the result one wants. An outcome gives direction, but it does not specify the repeated behavior that produces it.
- **Process change:** Changing the routine or system that produces the result. Process changes are closer to daily control than outcomes are.
- **Identity change:** Changing the beliefs and self-concept that guide behavior. Identity can support repetition, but it should remain flexible enough to change with evidence.
- **Identity vote:** A repetition that provides evidence for a desired identity. One vote is not proof, yet a pattern of votes can gradually change self-belief.
- **Implementation intention:** A behavior plan tied to a specific time and place. It replaces a vague intention with a cue that can be noticed and tested.
- **Habit stacking:** Pairing a new behavior with an existing habit. The existing habit serves as the anchor, so it must occur reliably enough to provide repeated cues.
- **Temptation bundling:** Pairing a needed behavior with an enjoyable one. The bundle makes anticipation more attractive, but the reward must not overwhelm or undermine the target behavior.
- **Environment design:** Shaping surroundings so cues and defaults support the desired action. It changes what is noticed and what is easy before willpower is required.
- **Friction:** The effort, steps, or inconvenience required to perform a behavior. Reducing friction can support a desired response, while adding it can weaken a competing one.
- **Law of Least Effort:** The tendency to choose the option requiring less work. The law makes convenience a design lever, not a judgment about commitment.
- **Two-Minute Rule:** Shrinking the start of a new habit to an action that takes about two minutes. The gateway protects initiation; later increases in duration or quality are separate design problems.
- **Commitment device:** A present choice that constrains future behavior. It can automate a beneficial default, add friction, or reduce access to a tempting response.
- **Habit tracker:** A simple visible record of repetitions. Tracking supplies immediate feedback, but the measure should remain a useful proxy for the real behavior or outcome.
- **Never miss twice:** A recovery rule for returning after a lapse. It prevents one disruption from becoming a new pattern without requiring perfect continuity.
- **Goldilocks Rule:** Motivation tends to be strongest when a challenge is just beyond current ability. The useful difficulty zone can shift as skill improves, so the challenge needs periodic adjustment.
- **Reflection and review:** Deliberate examination that keeps automatic performance conscious and adaptable. Review separates useful consistency from repetition that has drifted in quality or purpose.
