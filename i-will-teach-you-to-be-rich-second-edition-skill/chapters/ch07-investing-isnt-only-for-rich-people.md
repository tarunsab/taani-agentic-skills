# Chapter 7: Investing Isn’t Only for Rich People

## Core Idea
Sethi’s central investing claim is that a simple, diversified portfolio can be built with modest amounts and does not require stock-picking expertise. Automatic investing helps because it lowers ongoing expenses and reduces the number of emotional decisions made in response to market noise. Asset allocation—the mix of stocks, bonds, and cash—sets the portfolio’s risk more directly than the search for the “best” individual security. A target-date fund bundles allocation and rebalancing for convenience, while a small set of broad index funds offers more control at the cost of more maintenance. Choose the level of convenience you need, set an asset allocation that fits your time horizon and risk tolerance, keep costs low, and automate contributions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Automatic investing**: Invest a set amount on a recurring schedule so you accumulate through different market conditions. Use this when timing anxiety or indecision is the main barrier.
- **Asset allocation**: Decide the mix of stocks, bonds, and cash before choosing individual funds. Use this when comparing “best stocks” distracts from portfolio risk.
- **Target-date fund**: One diversified fund that changes its allocation over time and rebalances for you. Use this as the book’s 85 Percent Solution when convenience matters.
- **Broad index portfolio**: A small set of low-cost funds covering domestic equities, international equities, real estate, and bonds. Use this when you want more control and can maintain the allocation.
- **Fun-money sleeve**: Limit high-risk ideas to money you can afford to lose, after the core portfolio and emergency reserves work.

## Key Concepts
- **Stock / equity**: Ownership exposure with higher long-term growth potential and higher volatility.
- **Bond**: Lending exposure with generally lower expected return and volatility than stocks.
- **Diversification**: Spreading exposure across asset classes and regions so one bet does not dominate.
- **Expense ratio**: Annual fund cost; low costs preserve more compounding.
- **Dollar-cost averaging**: Investing equal amounts at intervals; useful for ongoing paychecks and behavior, though a lump sum may have higher expected return when invested immediately.
- **Rule of 72**: A rough way to estimate doubling time by dividing 72 by the annual return percentage.
- **Rebalancing**: Restoring the portfolio to its chosen allocation after market movements.

## Mental Models
- Use **convenience versus control** to choose between a target-date fund and a hand-built portfolio.
- Think of asset allocation as the portfolio’s steering wheel; security selection is a smaller detail.
- Use a small fun-money allocation to satisfy curiosity without letting speculation become the financial plan.

## Anti-patterns
- **Picking individual winners as the core plan**: One lucky result does not demonstrate repeatable skill.
- **Treating crypto or alternatives as a diversified portfolio**: A volatile theme is not a substitute for broad exposure.
- **Buying seven funds and splitting money evenly**: Contributions should follow the chosen allocation, not convenience.
- **Waiting for a perfect portfolio**: Buy the first appropriate fund and build over time.

## Worked Example
Suppose $500 per month is available after the 401(k). If a fund has a $1,000 minimum, save for two months, buy it, contribute a smaller amount to keep it active, and direct the remainder toward the next fund. Once all funds are present, multiply the monthly investing total by each target allocation percentage rather than dividing evenly. The example shows how to start without waiting for a large balance while still respecting the chosen allocation. It also separates a temporary minimum-investment constraint from the long-term rule: buy broad exposure according to the plan, then automate and rebalance instead of reacting to headlines.

## Key Takeaways
1. Pick a target-date fund if maintenance is a barrier.
2. If building your own portfolio, decide allocation first and use broad, low-cost funds.
3. Automate contributions and rebalance periodically.
4. Treat speculative investments as optional entertainment, never as the foundation.
