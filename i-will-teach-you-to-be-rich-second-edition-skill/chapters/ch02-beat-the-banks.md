# Chapter 2: Beat the Banks

## Core Idea
Bank accounts are the infrastructure through which income, bills, savings, and investing flow. Sethi argues that banks earn heavily from customer inertia, confusing fee structures, overdrafts, and accounts that make the bank money while delivering little value to the customer. The 30-Second Test asks whether the provider is acting in a way that deserves the relationship; repeated poor service is evidence to move. Checking should serve as the transaction inbox, while savings should be separated into named goals that are reachable but less tempting to raid. Choose low-hassle, low-fee accounts based on how a provider treats customers, then separate checking from goal-based savings to add useful friction.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **30-Second Test**: When a company repeatedly demonstrates bad values or service, believe the pattern and move your business. Use this when inertia keeps you with a fee-heavy provider.
- **Checking as inbox, savings as goals**: Let checking receive and filter money; let savings hold named short- and medium-term goals. Use this when cash is disappearing into undifferentiated spending.
- **Two-bank architecture**: Keep the account used for frequent withdrawals separate from the account used for saving. Use this when easy access makes it too tempting to raid savings.
- **Fee negotiation**: Ask for a fee reversal or no-fee account, request a supervisor if needed, and calmly state that you will move the account if the bank cannot help. Use this when a fee is charged or an account no longer fits.

## Key Concepts
- **Checking account**: Transaction hub for deposits, regular withdrawals, and bills.
- **Savings account**: Low-frequency account for goals such as emergency cash, travel, gifts, or a down payment.
- **Overdraft fee**: A potentially large charge that can erase a year of interest on a small balance.
- **Rate chasing**: Repeatedly switching for tiny yield differences while ignoring fees, service, and system friction.
- **Sub-account / named goal**: A separate label or account that gives a dollar a job.

## Mental Models
- Prefer **total cost and behavior** over the advertised rate. A single fee can outweigh a modest rate advantage.
- Use **friction as a feature**: savings should be reachable, but not as convenient as checking.
- Start with small balances to build durable habits before income and stakes grow.

## Anti-patterns
- **Staying because it is your first bank**: Familiarity is not evidence of value.
- **Choosing one mediocre provider for everything**: Convenience can hide inferior checking, savings, and investing products.
- **Optimizing rates obsessively**: The macro system—fees avoided, contributions automated, investments growing—matters more.
- **Accepting “we do not offer that” as final**: Ask for alternatives or a supervisor before giving up.

## Worked Example
A customer receives an avoidable bank fee. Rather than arguing emotionally, they ask whether the fee can be reversed, state that they have been a customer in good standing, and ask what the bank can do to retain the relationship. If the representative cannot help, the customer asks for a supervisor and repeats the request plainly. The book presents this as a practical response to the bank’s assumption that most people will accept the charge and stay. The method is respectful persistence, not aggression, and it is useful precisely because the conversation costs little compared with years of recurring fees.

## Key Takeaways
1. Keep checking for transactions and savings for named goals.
2. Pick accounts with no unnecessary fees and a provider whose conduct you trust.
3. Negotiate fees; if the pattern remains poor, switch banks.
4. Do not let a small interest-rate difference distract from fees and automation.
