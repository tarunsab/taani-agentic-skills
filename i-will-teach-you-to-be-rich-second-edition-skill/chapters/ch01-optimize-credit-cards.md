# Chapter 1: Optimize Your Credit Cards

## Core Idea
Credit cards are tools: used deliberately, they can build credit, provide protections, and earn rewards; carried balances and missed payments make them expensive debt. Sethi separates the credit report, which records account and payment history, from the score, which compresses that history into a lending signal. He emphasizes payment reliability, low utilization, long account history, and sensible card choice before any reward optimization. He also treats debt payoff as a behavioral and mathematical problem: the highest-APR method saves the most interest, while the snowball can create faster psychological momentum. The first Big Win is to understand the system and make the safe behavior automatic.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Credit score / credit report split**: The report contains account and payment history; the score summarizes lending risk. Use this when preparing for future borrowing or checking for errors.
- **Six Commandments of Credit Cards**: Pay on time; get fees waived; keep utilization low; preserve a long credit history; use more than one source of credit sensibly; and choose cards/rewards that fit your actual spending. The practical priority is never missing a payment and avoiding interest.
- **Negotiation script**: Ask the issuer to waive an annual or late fee or lower the APR, citing your history and competing offers. Use this when the upside is meaningful and the call costs minutes.
- **Debt payoff sequence**: List every balance, rate, and minimum; choose either highest APR first (mathematical efficiency) or lowest balance first (motivational momentum); negotiate the rate; direct extra cash to the selected debt; automate it.

## Key Concepts
- **Credit utilization**: The share of available revolving credit currently used; lower utilization generally supports a stronger score.
- **Payment history**: The largest component of the score in the book’s framing; late payments can be costly.
- **Rewards optimization**: Choose cash back or travel points only when the card is paid in full and the rewards match normal spending.
- **Minimum payment trap**: A variable minimum can stretch repayment for decades and magnify interest.
- **Snowball method**: Pay minimums everywhere and attack the smallest balance first for psychological wins.
- **Highest-APR method**: Pay minimums everywhere and attack the costliest balance first for mathematical savings.

## Mental Models
- Use **offense, not defense**: learn the card’s incentives and claim benefits instead of merely avoiding cards.
- Prefer a five-minute, high-upside negotiation over months of searching for a perfect balance transfer.
- Treat debt as a priority constraint: keep the rest of the system running, but do not invest aggressively while expensive revolving debt grows.

## Anti-patterns
- **Paying only the minimum**: The payment falls as the balance falls, slowing payoff and increasing interest.
- **Closing old cards casually**: Can shorten credit history or reduce available credit; evaluate the effect first.
- **Chasing rewards while carrying a balance**: Interest can overwhelm the rewards.
- **Using a balance transfer as the whole plan**: A lower rate does not fix spending behavior and can add complexity.
- **Borrowing from a 401(k) or home equity to hide card debt**: It may move risk rather than remove it.

## Worked Example
For a $5,000 balance at 14% APR, the book compares a declining minimum payment with fixed payments. Paying roughly $100 per month takes more than six years and costs thousands in interest; doubling the fixed payment cuts payoff to roughly two and a half years and reduces interest substantially. The book makes the same point with smaller purchases: a modest consumer balance can persist for years when the minimum payment is calculated as a percentage of the remaining balance. The exact figures vary with issuer rules, and a balance-transfer offer does not remove the need to stop adding debt. The decision rule is to pay a fixed amount above the minimum and automate it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Pull your credit report, check your score, and correct errors.
2. Automate at least the minimum; ideally pay the statement balance in full.
3. Call to negotiate APRs and fees before accepting them as fixed.
4. If in debt, choose a payoff method quickly and begin with a fixed aggressive payment.
