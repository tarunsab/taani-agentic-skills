# Lesson 6: Work to Learn—Don’t Work for Money

## Choose skills as well as salary

The chapter recommends evaluating work by the abilities it develops, not only by immediate pay or job title. It highlights sales, communication, marketing, accounting, investing, negotiation, and the operation of systems and people. These skills make a person more capable of creating value, managing a business, and recognizing opportunities. The principle does not cancel the need for fair pay; it adds a long-term learning return to the compensation calculation.

## Broad competence

The book favors knowing a little about many connected functions rather than becoming trapped in one narrow specialty. Exposure across accounting, sales, operations, and management helps a person understand how departments interact and where value or failure is created. Specialization can still be valuable, especially when it is chosen deliberately and paired with communication or business fluency. Use the principle to identify the next adjacent capability that increases the user's options.

## Sales and communication

Kiyosaki treats sales as a core life and business skill because value must be communicated before people can choose it. The author's own sales experience is presented as practice in handling rejection and building confidence. For a user who avoids selling, start with truthful communication about a useful product, service, or idea rather than manipulative scripts. The aim is to make the user more effective at asking, explaining, negotiating, and receiving feedback.

## Management skills

The chapter identifies management of cash flow, systems, and people as important capabilities. Cash-flow management connects operations to survival; systems management makes results repeatable; people management coordinates expertise and accountability. These are different problems, so a person who is good at one may still need to learn the others. In career planning, choose projects that provide observable practice in the missing area.

## Application

Before accepting a role, write two lists: what it pays now and what it teaches that compounds later. Name the manager, project, or environment that will provide feedback, and set a review date for whether the learning is actually happening. If a skill is important but absent from the current job, acquire it through a project, course, volunteer responsibility, or carefully chosen role. The result should be greater capability, not simply more activity.
