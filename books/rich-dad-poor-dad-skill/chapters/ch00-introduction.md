# Introduction: Rich Dad Poor Dad

## The two money philosophies

The book is organized around the contrast between the author's educated father, who emphasized school, a secure job, benefits, and a dependable paycheck, and his friend's father, who emphasized financial education, ownership, and making money work through assets. The contrast is a teaching device rather than a complete portrait of every career or household. One path tends to prioritize security through employment; the other prioritizes control through financial knowledge and asset ownership. Use it to surface which assumptions are guiding a decision, not to force a false choice between learning and employment.

## The central choice

The introduction frames financial progress as a choice about what to learn and what to do with money, time, and attention. A person can follow the familiar route without examining its cash-flow consequences, or deliberately learn the language of assets, liabilities, income, and expenses. The book's promise is not that wealth is effortless; it is that the relevant financial skills can be learned. The rest of the skill should therefore be used as a questioning framework, not as a prediction engine.

## How to apply the frame

When a user asks for financial help, first identify which “dad” assumption is present: security through wages, or freedom through ownership and financial intelligence. Then inspect the actual numbers, because a belief about money may not match the user's cash flow. Preserve the useful part of the conventional view—income stability, education, and responsible obligations—while adding the book's emphasis on assets and learning. The productive synthesis is a better-informed choice, not imitation of either character.
