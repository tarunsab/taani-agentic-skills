# Lesson 3: Mind Your Own Business

## Employment is not ownership

The chapter distinguishes a person's profession or job from the business they own. Someone can be an excellent employee, teacher, engineer, or manager while still having no growing asset column outside the employer's business. “Mind your own business” means keep the job if it serves you, but use some of the resulting surplus to acquire assets. The point is to create an ownership track alongside the income track.

## The asset column

The book lists stocks, bonds, income-producing real estate, notes, royalties, and other holdings that have value, produce income, appreciate, or have a ready market. These categories are examples, not endorsements of a particular allocation. The user should understand what produces the return, what can reduce it, how liquid it is, and which costs or taxes apply. An asset column is useful only when it is connected to the user's actual cash flow and responsibilities.

## Keep lifestyle inflation visible

The chapter warns that people can increase earned income without increasing financial independence if every raise is absorbed by larger expenses and liabilities. A useful operating rule is to decide in advance what portion of new income will improve present life and what portion will acquire assets or reduce expensive liabilities. This is not a command to live miserably or to reject every comfort. It is a way to ensure that work gradually buys optionality rather than only a more expensive version of the same dependence.

## Application

Make a two-column inventory: current work and owned assets. For each asset, record what it contributes, what it costs, who manages it, and what would cause you to sell or stop adding to it. Then choose one repeatable contribution or learning project rather than trying to build an entire portfolio at once. The chapter is successful when ownership becomes a deliberate part of the user's identity and monthly plan.
