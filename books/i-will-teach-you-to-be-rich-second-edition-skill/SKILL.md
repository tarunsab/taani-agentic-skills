## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Define the Rich Life first
Use money as a tool for a concrete life you want—not as a scorekeeping exercise. Describe the desired life at street level: the people, experiences, freedoms, purchases, time, and security that matter. “Freedom” and “security” are starting themes, not sufficient goals.

### 2. Prioritize Big Wins over minutiae
The 85 Percent Solution says to act once you have a sound plan, rather than delaying for perfect information. Focus on the few decisions with disproportionate effects: start investing early, eliminate high-interest debt, avoid recurring fees, automate transfers, negotiate income and large purchases, and use low-cost diversified investments. Do not spend hours optimizing a tiny rate difference while ignoring a fee, debt balance, or savings contribution.

### 3. Use the CEO Method when money is tight
The three levers are **Cut costs**, **Earn more**, and **Optimize existing spending**. Cutting costs is useful, but income growth and conscious redirection often create more room than endless couponing. Treat constraints as a prompt to choose the strongest available lever, not as proof that no action is possible.

### 4. Replace invisible money scripts with deliberate choices
An invisible money script is an inherited or socially absorbed belief such as “rent is throwing money away,” “the stock market is gambling,” or “credit cards are always bad.” Name the belief, test it against evidence and your goals, then choose a behavior. The book’s system is behavioral: reduce friction, make good actions defaults, and avoid relying on willpower.

### 5. Conscious Spending Plan
Do not attempt a permanent, guilt-inducing ledger. Allocate income in advance, then spend the remainder deliberately:

| Bucket | Guideline | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| Fixed costs | 50–60% of take-home | Housing, utilities, food, transport, debt payments |
| Investments | ~10% | 401(k), IRA, other long-term investing |
| Savings goals | 5–10% | Emergency fund, travel, gifts, planned purchases |
| Guilt-free spending | 20–35% | Values-aligned discretionary spending |

Treat these as starting ranges, not moral rules. Spend extravagantly on high-value priorities and cut mercilessly on low-value categories. Use an envelope or category limit when one “Big Win” repeatedly overruns.

### 6. Automatic Money Flow
Make the infrastructure do the work while motivation is available. Direct pay into checking, capture the employer match, transfer to savings goals and investment accounts, pay fixed bills, and pay credit-card balances automatically. A representative monthly sequence is: paycheck and 401(k), then savings and IRA transfers, then bills and card payment. Add buffers, align due dates, review statements, and adapt for biweekly or irregular income.

### 7. Ladder of Personal Finance
Prioritize the next rung instead of optimizing everything at once: get the employer 401(k) match; eliminate high-interest credit-card debt; fund a Roth IRA; increase 401(k) contributions; consider an HSA when eligible and appropriate; then invest in taxable or other accounts. Keep a cash buffer for the actual risks in your life. Student-loan decisions depend on rate, risk tolerance, tax advantages, and the value of investing early; a hybrid approach can be reasonable.

### 8. Prefer simple, low-cost, diversified investing
Ignore market forecasts and focus on time in the market. Choose either a target-date fund for convenience or a small portfolio of broad, low-cost index funds for control. Asset allocation matters more than finding the “best” stock. Keep fees low, automate contributions, diversify across stocks and bonds, and rebalance periodically if you manage the allocation yourself. High-risk ideas belong only in a small, genuinely disposable “fun money” sleeve after the core system works.

### 9. Negotiate high-leverage decisions
Use short, prepared conversations for card APRs and fees, bank fees, salary, cars, housing, and other large purchases. Lead with evidence and the value you provide or the business you bring. Ask, pause, and be willing to walk away; negotiate total compensation, not only salary. Never lie, do not reveal a current salary unnecessarily, and get future review promises in writing.

### 10. Keep money subordinate to life
Once the system works, feed it to match your purpose—but do not “live in the spreadsheet.” Review annually, ignore noise, use money to buy time and experiences, plan predictable large expenses, and give back. A Rich Life is the outcome: conscious choices, less anxiety, and more freedom to spend time on what matters.
