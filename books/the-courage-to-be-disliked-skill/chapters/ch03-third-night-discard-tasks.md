# The Third Night — Discard Other People’s Tasks

## Core idea

The third dialogue makes separation of tasks the book's clearest practical boundary. First identify who is responsible for an action; then identify who ultimately owns the consequence. A person owns their decisions, effort, communication, promises, requests, and boundaries, while another person's opinion, feelings, and response remain outside direct control. The book uses this distinction to challenge the demand for recognition: trying to make another person approve is an attempt to control their task. Freedom therefore includes accepting that a self-directed life may be disliked by people whose approval one once depended on.

## Recognition and freedom

The dialogue argues that recognition-seeking makes a person live according to someone else's expectations. Praise, criticism, and social status can all become forms of external control if they determine what the person is allowed to want. Giving up recognition does not mean refusing feedback or becoming inconsiderate; it means making approval a possible consequence rather than the owner of the decision. The book's version of freedom is therefore relational: act responsibly, communicate clearly, and release the demand that others validate the action. This is where the title's “disliked” becomes a test of autonomy rather than a goal of provocation.

## Applying task separation

Use three columns: **my task**, **their task**, and **shared obligation**. A request belongs to you; whether the other person accepts it belongs to them. A boundary belongs to you when it describes what you will do, not when it demands that the other person feel differently. Shared obligations still require coordination, repair, and consequences, so task separation is not a license to withdraw from a family, team, or community.

## Prompts

- Whose task is the outcome I am trying to guarantee?
- What would I do if I could not make the other person agree?
- What boundary can I carry out from my side?
- What responsibility remains mine even if I cannot control the response?

## Common misuse

“Their reaction is their task” can become emotional indifference or an excuse not to repair harm. A better reading is: their reaction is not yours to command, but your conduct and its foreseeable consequences are still yours to examine. Keep empathy and accountability; release coercion.
