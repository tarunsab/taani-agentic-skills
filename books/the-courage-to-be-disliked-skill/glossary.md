# The Courage to Be Disliked — Glossary

## Adlerian psychology

The psychological framework the dialogue presents as an alternative to explanations that treat people as permanently determined by their past. The book emphasizes goals, relationships, belonging, responsibility, and the possibility of changing one's lifestyle.

## Teleology

An interpretive focus on the purpose or goal a present behavior may be serving, rather than only on the historical cause that preceded it. Use it carefully: identifying a possible function is not the same as claiming that a person consciously chose their suffering.

## Lifestyle

The book's term for a person's characteristic way of seeing self, others, and the world, including habitual assumptions and choices. It is presented as changeable, though changing it requires courage and sustained practice.

## Feelings of inferiority

A subjective sense of being less capable, less worthy, or less advantaged in comparison with others. The book treats this feeling as a possible stimulus for growth, not as proof of objective worthlessness.

## Inferiority complex

The book's term for using an assumed deficiency as an excuse or explanation that blocks action, often by saying that success would be impossible because of a circumstance. This is a provocative framing and should not be used to erase genuine barriers or disability.

## Superiority complex

A compensating display of status, expertise, wealth, suffering, or specialness used to avoid facing feelings of inferiority. The dialogue treats boastfulness and exaggerated victimhood as related attempts to establish a superior position.

## Interpersonal relationship problems

The book's broad claim that many problems become problems through relationships: comparison, recognition, control, rejection, competition, resentment, or expectations. It does not mean that every difficulty is caused by a relationship or that structural conditions are irrelevant.

## Recognition

The desire to be praised, approved, noticed, or validated by others. The book argues that organizing life around recognition hands one's direction to other people's judgments.

## Separation of tasks

A boundary-setting method: identify who is responsible for an action and who ultimately owns its consequence. Your task includes your choices and communication; another person's feelings, opinion, and response remain their task.

## Community feeling

The book's sense of belonging to a wider human community, rather than treating the self as the center of every interaction. It involves seeing others as comrades, recognizing mutual value, and contributing without requiring superiority.

## Contribution

A way of participating in the community that gives a sense of value. In the book's framework, contribution is more stable than praise because it need not depend on a particular person noticing or rewarding it.

## Encouragement

Support that communicates equality and confidence in another person's ability to act. It differs from praise or rebuke because it does not make one person the evaluator and the other the performer.

## Self-acceptance

Acknowledging one's present abilities, limits, mistakes, and circumstances as they are, then choosing what can be practiced. It is not the same as declaring that no change is needed.

## Self-affirmation

The book's contrasting idea of pretending or insisting that a limitation is not present. The dialogue favors honest self-acceptance because it provides a workable basis for change.

## Trust

Choosing to relate to another person without demanding guarantees that they will behave as desired. The book links trust with the courage to risk disappointment while still keeping appropriate boundaries.

## Here and now

The immediate field of action in which life is actually lived. The book uses this idea to challenge postponing life until one becomes special, solves every problem, or receives final recognition.

## Courage to be normal

The willingness to stop treating exceptional status as a prerequisite for worth. In the book's sense, a normal life can still contain meaningful contribution and deliberate action.
