# Chapter 8: Going Viral

## Core Idea

Don Redelmeier brings the judgment research into medicine by acting as a generalist who asks what the specialists may have anchored on and what they have failed to observe. In the head-on crash case, the known thyroid condition makes an irregular heartbeat seem representative, while a deliberate pause reveals a collapsed lung; the point is not that specialists are foolish, but that an independent question can interrupt a compelling frame. Redelmeier’s work with Tversky also exposes a mismatch between individual and aggregate reasoning: a gamble that is unattractive once may be acceptable when repeated, and a treatment that helps one patient can impose a population-level cost. The hot-hand and weather examples show selective matching, in which people remember confirming cases and overlook the stable or disconfirming ones. The peak-end rule adds a memory mechanism: retrospective evaluation is disproportionately shaped by the most intense moment and the ending, not by total duration. The chapter turns these findings into a practical role for a checker and a careful observer, while retaining the limits of experimental analogies and the need for clinical evidence before applying a psychological insight.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Independent checker**: Give a generalist permission and responsibility to question the dominant frame, inspect the wider scene, and ask what common cause has been overlooked.
- **Individual-versus-aggregate split**: Evaluate a choice separately at the single-case level and across repeated cases; the preferred action can change with scale.
- **Selective-matching audit**: For every remembered confirmation, look for ordinary cases and disconfirming observations that were not emotionally tagged.
- **Peak-end memory check**: When evaluating an experience, record duration and the whole sequence separately from the peak and ending.

## Key Concepts

- Anchoring on an available diagnosis or explanation.
- Observation of context, not just the officially assigned facts.
- The Samuelson gamble as a contrast between one-shot and repeated risk.
- Hot-hand belief and the tendency to see patterns in random streaks.
- The peak-end rule in pain and retrospective experience.

## Mental Models

- **The second pair of eyes**: A useful outsider does not need to know more facts; they need to ask a different question.
- **The scale switch**: Move from “what happens to this person?” to “what happens across the population?” before deciding.
- **The memory crop**: A remembered event is an edited summary, not a full recording.

## Anti-patterns

- Letting a known diagnosis close the differential before checking dangerous alternatives.
- Generalizing a one-person benefit to a repeated-treatment policy without counting aggregate harms.
- Recording only streaks or pain days that fit a preferred pattern.
- Measuring service quality only by the last impression or most dramatic moment.

## Worked Example (study only)

Patients undergoing a colonoscopy-like procedure were asked about pain at intervals, and later patients remembered the procedure largely through its worst moment and its ending. A longer procedure with a gentler final phase could be remembered as less painful than a shorter procedure that ended at a high point. The result does not make duration irrelevant for the body; it shows that remembered experience and experienced duration are different outcome measures. A service designer should therefore define whether the goal is immediate comfort, total exposure, later memory, or return behavior.

## Key Takeaways

- Build a formal place for dissent in high-stakes decisions.
- Recalculate when the unit of analysis changes from one case to many.
- Audit memory for selective matching and peak-end distortion.
- Treat a psychological effect as a prompt for a better study, not as a substitute for domain evidence.
