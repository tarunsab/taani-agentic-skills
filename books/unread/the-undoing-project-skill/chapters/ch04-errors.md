# Chapter 4: Errors

## Core Idea

Kahneman’s work with pilots makes a basic statistical error visible: an extreme performance is usually followed by a less extreme one, so praise or criticism can appear to cause the next result when ordinary regression is doing much of the work. His curiosity then moves across perception, memory, attention, learning, and training, looking for questions that can be operationalized rather than explained by intuition. Experiments and cases show that hindsight makes missed warning signs seem obvious, attention weakens rather than switches cleanly off, and practice can fail to transfer from one population or task to another. The applied psychology course turns these findings into interventions: reduce the restraining forces that make change difficult, break complex skills into components, and respect the limits of short-term memory by using chunks. Kahneman’s own abandoned card-sequence idea and failed camping experiment model the discipline of dropping an attractive claim when it does not replicate. The chapter’s broader lesson is that human error is systematic enough to study, but each result remains bounded by the task, sample, and measurement that produced it.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Regression-to-the-mean check**: Before attributing an outcome to feedback, compare extreme cases with repeated observations and a suitable baseline.
- **Attention-as-weak-filter**: Assume unattended information is weakened, not necessarily erased; design warnings and checks accordingly.
- **Force-field change**: Make a desired change easier by reducing restraining forces instead of only adding pressure or incentives.
- **Component-skill training**: Decompose a complex activity into learnable parts and teach how to act when the next step is not obvious.
- **Replication discipline**: A failed repeat is a reason to revise or abandon the idea, even when the original result was exciting.

## Key Concepts

- Regression to the mean and the illusion of effective praise or punishment.
- Hindsight bias and the tendency to retrofit a coherent story after an outcome.
- Selective attention, including the possibility that a personally meaningful word crosses an ignored channel.
- Short-term memory limits and the use of chunking.
- The difference between a clever laboratory effect and a reliable applied intervention.

## Mental Models

- **The rebound trap**: Extreme performance naturally moves toward average; a response immediately before the rebound receives too much credit.
- **The leaky gate**: Attention filters information by degree, so safety systems should assume that salient signals can break through and important signals can be missed.
- **The stuck system**: To change behavior, identify the forces holding the current state in place before demanding more motivation.

## Anti-patterns

- Rewarding or punishing an individual on the basis of one result after an unusually high or low performance.
- Treating a post-event explanation as evidence that the warning signs were predictable beforehand.
- Assuming training results generalize without checking the population and task.
- Adding goals and pressure when the actual obstacle is friction, fear, or a missing component skill.

## Worked Example (study only)

Israeli Air Force instructors observed that pilots who had performed exceptionally often performed less well after praise, while poorly performing pilots improved after criticism. The instructors concluded that praise harmed performance and criticism helped it. Kahneman recognized that the initial extremes themselves predicted a move back toward average, so the apparent lesson was mostly a statistical artifact. The safer evaluation is to follow performance across repeated trials and compare it with pilots who did not receive the intervention.

## Key Takeaways

- Ask what would have happened without the intervention.
- Treat memory and hindsight as sources of distortion, not neutral records.
- Design for partial attention and limited working memory.
- Generalize an experiment only after checking its boundaries.
