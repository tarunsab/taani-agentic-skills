# Chapter 7: The Rules of Prediction

## Core Idea

Tversky’s private rules of prediction describe a mind that turns uncertainty into stories, explains outcomes more easily than it forecasts them, and treats the past as inevitable after the fact. The experiments make the mechanism concrete: people use a personality sketch to identify Tom W. as a computer scientist while ignoring the known proportions of majors, and they treat a thin description of Dick as if it erased the base rate of engineers and lawyers. The same features that make a prediction feel confident can make it less accurate, especially when they are vivid but weak evidence. Regression returns as a reminder that a good decision process can be followed by a bad outcome, while hindsight makes the prior uncertainty disappear in memory. Decision analysis tries to improve the process by framing options as gambles with probabilities and utilities, but leaders often resist numbers because a bare probability does not tell a compelling story. The chapter’s practical standard is to judge forecasts by the quality of the odds and information available beforehand, not by the elegance of the explanation constructed afterward.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Base-rate-first prediction**: Start with what is likely before the case-specific description; update only for evidence with demonstrated diagnostic value.
- **Evidence-quality test**: Ask whether a detail changes the odds or merely resembles a stereotype.
- **Confidence–accuracy separation**: Track calibration over repeated predictions instead of using confidence as proof of correctness.
- **Hindsight control**: Record forecasts or probability judgments before outcomes so later explanations cannot rewrite the original uncertainty.
- **Decision-analysis frame**: Represent an action as possible outcomes, probabilities, and utilities, then make the value trade-off explicit.

## Key Concepts

- Judgment is assigning odds; prediction is judgment applied to an uncertain future.
- Base rates are the forecast available before case-specific information.
- Worthless evidence can make a story feel more specific without improving its probability.
- Hindsight bias creates “creeping determinism,” the sense that an outcome was obvious.
- A probability can be mathematically clear and still fail to communicate without a usable narrative.

## Mental Models

- **The prior anchor**: A description should move a base rate only in proportion to its reliability.
- **The forecast ledger**: Preserve the probability, evidence, and alternatives before the result arrives.
- **The story–number bridge**: Use a narrative to make probabilities understandable, but do not let the narrative replace them.

## Anti-patterns

- Choosing a rare category because its description is vivid.
- Treating a detailed sketch as strong evidence when it was generated to fit a stereotype.
- Explaining a forecast after the outcome without checking what was actually known beforehand.
- Assuming a formal decision analysis will change behavior merely because its arithmetic is correct.

## Worked Example (study only)

If a class is known to contain 70 engineers and 30 lawyers, a description that provides no reliable occupational evidence should lead to a probability near 70/30. People instead tend to choose the occupation that best matches the sketch, even when the same sketch is paired with a different class composition. The description adds narrative detail but not necessarily diagnostic information. The correction is to state the reference class first and ask what observation would genuinely distinguish the two groups.

## Key Takeaways

- Separate what is typical of the population from what is distinctive about the case.
- Measure prediction quality over many forecasts.
- Preserve pre-outcome uncertainty in writing.
- Let probabilities guide decisions, while translating them into a story people can act on.
