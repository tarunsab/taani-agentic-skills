# Chapter 1: Man Boobs

## Core Idea

The chapter opens in NBA draft rooms, where interviews and memorable physical impressions can overwhelm a thin but more diagnostic record. Daryl Morey tries to make basketball selection a probabilistic exercise: the model should produce a better answer, not a right answer, and it must be paired with investigation of context the numbers miss. The Rockets’ experience with prospects such as Aaron Brooks, Carl Landry, Joey Dorsey, DeAndre Jordan, Marc Gasol, Jeremy Lin, and Kyle Lowry shows both sides of the method: statistical signals can uncover overlooked talent, while stereotypes, recency, endowment, and confirmation bias can distort human judgment. Morey’s countermeasures—explicit cross-racial comparisons, base-rate discipline, forcing a valuation of one’s own players before seeing a trade, and banning dismissive nicknames—are attempts to redesign the decision process rather than rely on good intentions. Satnam Singh illustrates the opposite problem: when almost no relevant data exist, a striking body or story invites a narrative that feels like evidence. The chapter’s lesson is therefore conditional: structure the judgment, collect better measurements, and preserve room for unobserved context; awareness of bias alone will not neutralize it.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Probabilistic scouting**: Treat a prospect evaluation as a forecast with uncertainty, combining historical performance, comparable competition, physical measures, and contextual investigation.
- **Cross-racial comparison rule**: Require comparisons outside the evaluator’s familiar stereotype so superficial resemblance does not become the central evidence.
- **Pre-commitment against endowment**: Estimate the value of an owned player or asset before considering a trade or new opportunity.
- **Model plus context**: Use the model to discipline impressions, then investigate information that is not captured by the variables.

## Key Concepts

- Interviews are vivid and memorable but may be a poor proxy for future performance.
- Base rates and per-minute or opportunity-adjusted measures can reveal hidden value.
- Confirmation bias changes the label attached to the same evidence: a disliked player lacks a position, while a favored player is “versatile.”
- Recency and availability make a streak, workout, or physical comparison feel more informative than it is.
- The endowment effect makes a current asset seem more valuable simply because it is ours.

## Mental Models

- **The magic-show interview**: A persuasive performance can distract from the variables that actually predict the outcome.
- **The missing puzzle pieces**: A decision with sparse data should be represented as a probability distribution with missing information, not as a confident story.
- **The bias clock**: Data can move a decision away from an initial impression, but some mental shortcuts remain active after the evaluator knows about them.

## Anti-patterns

- Letting a six-shot workout or a charming interview override a larger performance history.
- Using an attractive or disliked comparison to decide which statistics “count.”
- Treating a model’s top projection as a guarantee or using a failed projection to dismiss the entire method.
- Valuing one’s own player, project, or idea only after a competing option appears.

## Worked Example (study only)

Morey’s staff liked Jeremy Lin’s measured first step, while scouts often saw an “unathletic Asian” stereotype. The data did not prove that Lin would become a star, but they identified a relevant ability that the familiar visual story had hidden. The later draft examples show why this is not a simple victory for numbers: a model can flag Joey Dorsey as promising or miss the human reasons a player underperforms, while a prospect such as DeAndre Jordan can be neglected when contextual judgment is poor. The process improvement is to make the relevant comparison visible and to ask what the model cannot observe.

## Key Takeaways

- Build decisions around predictive evidence, not memorable presentation.
- Use explicit debiasing rules before the story hardens into a conclusion.
- Preserve a disciplined role for human investigation, especially where motivation and context are unmeasured.
- Interpret every model as a forecast with error bars.
