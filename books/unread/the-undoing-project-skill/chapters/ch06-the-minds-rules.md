# Chapter 6: The Mind’s Rules

## Core Idea

At the Oregon Research Institute, the question is no longer merely whether experts are inconsistent but how they combine cues to reach a judgment. A simple equal-weight model built from radiologists’ own diagnostic cues predicts cases more accurately and consistently than the doctors, who disagree with one another and sometimes with themselves when shown the same X-ray twice. Kahneman and Tversky then name the shortcuts that make ordinary judgment fast: representativeness substitutes similarity to a mental model for probability, availability substitutes ease of recall for frequency, and anchoring leaves estimates too close to a starting number; people also fill in unstated conditions with a normal-world assumption. These rules are adaptive much of the time, but they fail in structured ways: a stereotyped random sequence feels more likely, a vivid event feels common, and a sample of one is treated like a sample of six. The mind’s stories are not random mistakes; they are efficient procedures whose errors become visible when the world violates their assumptions. The chapter therefore supports simple models and debiasing checks without concluding that clinical experience or intuition is always inferior.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Representativeness heuristic**: Judge probability by similarity to a mental model; audit it when base rates, sample size, or logical containment matter.
- **Availability heuristic**: Judge frequency or likelihood by how easily examples come to mind; check whether retrieval is biased by vividness, recency, or publicity.
- **Anchoring and adjustment**: Start from an initial value and adjust insufficiently; generate an independent estimate before seeing a tempting anchor.
- **Conditionality audit**: Surface the ordinary conditions silently assumed in a prediction and test what happens if they fail.
- **Simple cue model**: When experts use unstable weights, a transparent and validated rule can outperform their judgment.

## Key Concepts

- Clinical versus statistical prediction and the unreliability of unaided expert cue weighting.
- Stereotyped randomness and neglect of sample size.
- Availability as an accessibility effect, not a measure of actual frequency.
- Anchors affecting estimates even when the starting value is arbitrary.
- Heuristics as both useful adaptations and sources of systematic bias.

## Mental Models

- **The prototype magnet**: A case that resembles a familiar type pulls judgment toward the type’s story, even when the base rate says otherwise.
- **The recall thermometer**: Ease of recall measures mental accessibility, not necessarily prevalence.
- **The sticky ruler**: An initial number bends later estimates toward itself unless the decision maker deliberately builds a fresh scale.

## Anti-patterns

- Letting the best story defeat a known base rate.
- Inferring a population from a tiny, extreme sample without a size check.
- Treating recent news, dramatic images, or memorable cases as frequency data.
- Assuming experience automatically fixes inconsistent cue weighting.

## Worked Example (study only)

People asked to estimate words ending in “ing” often give a higher number than for all seven-letter words with an “n” in the sixth position, even though the first set is contained in the second. The ending is easier to imagine, so availability drives the estimate. In another family of examples, a birth sequence that looks more randomly mixed feels more likely than a streak, even though every specific sequence has the same probability. Both cases show how a mental model can make an answer feel representative while ignoring the formal relationship among alternatives.

## Key Takeaways

- Name the shortcut before trusting the judgment it produced.
- Ask for the reference class, sample size, and logical containment.
- Use transparent rules when expert judgments are noisy or unstable.
- Keep the useful function of a heuristic separate from the conditions in which it fails.
