# Chapter 5: The Collision

## Core Idea

The intellectual partnership begins with a clash over whether people can be understood as informal statisticians. In a problem involving a red or white chip drawn from one of two bags, Bayes’s rule says that a piece of evidence changes prior odds by a likelihood ratio; three red draws can make one bag far more likely, yet people commonly move their confidence only a little. Kahneman sees the response as evidence that ordinary minds do not update mathematically, while Tversky’s formal instincts help state exactly what the rational update would be. Their joint investigation becomes a broader attack on the belief that small samples should resemble the populations from which they come. People expect random sequences to look representative, invoke the gambler’s fallacy, and explain away sampling variability instead of allowing a small sample to be lopsided. The disagreement is productive because it combines a clean formal benchmark with psychological observation, but the chapter also leaves an important limit in place: laboratory probability tasks can show systematic judgment errors without telling us the true odds of every real-world event.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Bayesian updating benchmark**: Start with prior odds, multiply by the evidence’s likelihood ratio, and distinguish a strong update from a feeling of certainty.
- **Law-of-small-numbers audit**: Ask whether a sample is large enough to support the stability or representativeness being inferred from it.
- **Formal benchmark plus behavioral test**: Define the normative calculation first, then observe where human judgments depart and whether the departure is systematic.
- **Productive intellectual collision**: Use disagreement between a formal model and lived psychology to identify a testable hidden assumption.

## Key Concepts

- Prior odds, likelihood, and posterior odds.
- The “conservative Bayesian” response: people often under-update relative to the formal rule while still feeling confident.
- The gambler’s fallacy and the expectation that random sequences balance quickly.
- Sampling variability: a small sample can look unlike the population without being evidence of a special cause.
- The distinction between a normative rule and a descriptive account of what people actually do.

## Mental Models

- **The odds ladder**: Evidence moves a starting probability; it does not replace the starting point.
- **The small-sample mirror**: A short sequence reflects noise as well as signal, so resemblance to the population is not guaranteed.
- **The two-lens collaboration**: One lens asks what should happen under a formal rule; the other asks why a mind departs from it.

## Anti-patterns

- Ignoring the base rate because a few observations look vivid or representative.
- Treating a short run of heads, sales, symptoms, or failures as proof that the next observation must compensate.
- Explaining a discrepancy with a convenient psychological story without checking sampling error.
- Confusing a formal answer to a known probability problem with certainty about an unmeasurable future.

## Worked Example (study only)

Suppose one bag contains red and white chips in a 75-to-25 ratio, the other has the reverse ratio, and the bags are equally likely at the start. A red draw makes the first bag three times as likely as the second; three red draws make the odds 27 to 1. The people in the story often respond as if the odds were only about 3 to 1, an under-update that can coexist with confidence. The example is useful because the benchmark is explicit: it separates the strength of the evidence from the subjective force of the story.

## Key Takeaways

- State the prior before interpreting new evidence.
- Treat small samples as noisy unless the design justifies stronger inference.
- Use formal rules as diagnostic benchmarks, not as claims that people are simple calculators.
- Keep conflict focused on assumptions and evidence; the Kahneman–Tversky partnership grows from this tension.
