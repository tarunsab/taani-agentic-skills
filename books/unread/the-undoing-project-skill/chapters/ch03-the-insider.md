# Chapter 3: The Insider

## Core Idea

Amos Tversky enters the story as a native insider of Israeli culture: mathematically gifted, physically brave, socially magnetic, and inclined to attack an idea until its hidden assumption breaks. His early work on preferences shows that complex choices can produce cycles—A preferred to B, B to C, and C to A—without making the person globally irrational; small, gradual differences and multiple dimensions can change what is salient. His work on similarity then challenges the assumption that resemblance is a symmetric distance: a son can resemble a father more naturally than the reverse, and the features noticed in a comparison depend on context. Similarity is built from common and distinctive features, including the meaningful absence of a feature, so a classification can create the very resemblance that seems to justify it. The scientific method that emerges is “negative” in spirit: find the prevailing model, locate a clean counterexample, and use it to reveal what the model cannot represent. The chapter also shows why Tversky’s mathematical precision complemented Kahneman’s psychological doubt, while cautioning that an elegant counterexample opens a research program; it does not by itself explain every preference or classification.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Feature-based similarity**: Compare noticeable common and distinctive features, with salience determined by the comparison context rather than by a fixed symmetric distance.
- **Asymmetric similarity test**: Reverse the direction of a comparison and ask whether the judgment changes; if it does, the representation is directional.
- **Paradigm-breaking experiment**: State the dominant rule clearly, design the smallest case that violates it, and use the failure to refine the theory.
- **Preference cycles**: Treat intransitive choices as a signal to inspect the dimensions and sequence of comparison before declaring irrationality.

## Key Concepts

- Tversky’s transition from humanities and poetry toward formal psychology.
- Coombs’s ideal-point view: people can evaluate an option by its distance from an internal ideal.
- Similarity as a basis for categorization, memory, and knowledge.
- Context-dependent salience and the possibility of being simultaneously more similar and more different.
- Classification as a force that can reinforce stereotypes.

## Mental Models

- **The directional mirror**: A comparison is not necessarily the same when its subject and reference are swapped.
- **The feature ledger**: Record both shared and distinctive features; do not let one vivid commonality erase an important difference.
- **The category trap**: Once two cases are placed in one category, the category makes them feel more alike, which then appears to validate the category.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming all similarity judgments can be represented as symmetric geometric distance.
- Calling a choice irrational without checking whether the alternatives were compared along different dimensions.
- Treating a useful category as a natural fact rather than a selective representation.
- Protecting a theory with auxiliary explanations instead of testing its central prediction.

## Worked Example (study only)

In the spouse-choice studies, people could prefer a spouse over a second option, prefer the second over a third, and then prefer the third over the first when small changes accumulated. In the similarity work, judgments about a son and father or about places changed when the comparison direction changed. Together these cases show that the mind constructs a comparison from the features made salient by the task; the same objects do not carry one fixed, context-free similarity score.

## Key Takeaways

- Inspect the representation before arguing about the answer.
- Counterexamples are valuable when they expose an assumption, not merely when they surprise.
- Similarity is contextual, directional, and entangled with categorization.
- Formal models become useful when they are vulnerable to decisive tests.
