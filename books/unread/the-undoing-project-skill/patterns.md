# Patterns

These patterns are extracted from the book’s experiments and applications. They are decision aids, not universal laws.

## 1. Put the reference class before the story

- **When**: A vivid case description invites a confident category judgment.
- **Move**: State the base rate first, then list which observations genuinely distinguish the case.
- **Mechanism**: Representativeness makes a matching story feel diagnostic even when it adds little information.
- **Watch-out**: A base rate is not a veto; reliable case evidence should update it.

The Tom W. and Dick examples show how a detailed sketch can displace a known population composition. The same pattern appears in scouting, diagnosis, and hiring whenever a prototype is easier to imagine than a reference class. Write down the prior before reading or discussing the narrative when possible. Then ask whether the details were measured for predictive value or merely selected because they sound characteristic.

## 2. Decompose the global impression

- **When**: An interview, consultation, or expert review produces an immediate sense of the whole person or case.
- **Move**: Replace the single verdict with specific questions, observable indicators, separate ratings, and a delayed synthesis.
- **Mechanism**: The halo effect lets one early impression contaminate every later judgment.
- **Watch-out**: Structured scores still need validation and do not capture every contextual variable.

Kahneman’s Army interview redesign and the radiology cue models both narrow the task so evidence can be compared. The intervention is valuable even when its score is imperfect because it reduces random inconsistency. It also makes disagreement inspectable: reviewers can argue about an indicator instead of defending an intuition about character. Use a global judgment only after the component forecasts have been recorded.

## 3. Make the comparison explicit before choosing

- **When**: A physical resemblance, stereotype, ownership stake, or recent event dominates attention.
- **Move**: Require alternative comparisons, cross-category analogies, or a pre-commitment made before the competing option is visible.
- **Mechanism**: Availability, confirmation, and endowment effects select the evidence that supports the first impression.
- **Watch-out**: A forced comparison can create noise if the comparison class is irrelevant.

Morey’s cross-racial rule, ban on dismissive nicknames, and pre-draft valuation exercise are process controls rather than appeals to impartiality. They work by changing what the team must look at and when it must commit. The same move applies to hiring, procurement, and prioritization: define the comparison set before the persuasive presentation. Keep an escape hatch for evidence that falls outside the template.

## 4. Insert an independent checker

- **When**: A specialist, leader, or dominant team has a compelling explanation and the cost of error is high.
- **Move**: Give an outsider a formal role to restate the problem, inspect the wider context, and name dangerous alternatives.
- **Mechanism**: A second frame can interrupt anchoring and make unattended information visible.
- **Watch-out**: The checker must have permission, time, and status to challenge the decision maker.

Redelmeier’s generalist stance and the aviation cockpit changes show that the intervention is organizational, not merely personal. The checker need not know more than the specialist; the value can come from asking a question the dominant frame excludes. A dissent ritual is strongest when it is routine before a crisis rather than introduced as an accusation. Measure whether the role finds real errors, not just whether it creates more discussion.

## 5. Test the logic hidden in the narrative

- **When**: A detailed account feels more probable, persuasive, or causal because its parts fit together.
- **Move**: Translate the story into sets, frequencies, conditional relationships, or a decision table.
- **Mechanism**: A coherent narrative can mask containment, base-rate, or probability constraints.
- **Watch-out**: Making the logic visible may improve performance without removing the underlying attraction of the story.

The Linda problem exposes a conjunction that cannot outrank its broader category. The Jason K. policy example shows the same error in a causal story: a specific intervention can be a special case of a broader change, not a more probable alternative. A frequency format often makes the relation easier to see. Use the translation step before debating which story feels realistic.

## 6. Check regression before assigning credit or blame

- **When**: A very good or very bad result follows praise, punishment, training, treatment, or a policy change.
- **Move**: Compare repeated outcomes with a baseline and ask what the extreme starting point predicts by itself.
- **Mechanism**: Natural movement toward average can look like intervention success or failure.
- **Watch-out**: Regression does not prove that the intervention had no effect; it shows that the immediate sequence is weak evidence.

The flight-instructor example makes the error emotionally persuasive because feedback is temporally adjacent to the rebound. A single before-and-after pair cannot identify the cause. Track outcomes over enough cases, include a comparison group when possible, and record the starting conditions. Reward a process that produces calibrated evidence, not a result that merely follows an extreme observation.

## 7. Change the environment when the bias is durable

- **When**: People know a bias exists but continue to reproduce it under pressure or routine.
- **Move**: Alter defaults, labels, workflow, access, or authority so the safer or more accurate action is easier and dissent is possible.
- **Mechanism**: Intention is weaker than the structure that repeatedly cues and rewards behavior.
- **Watch-out**: Choice architecture can manipulate as well as help; test effects and disclose consequential frames.

Sunstein’s automatic enrollment and school-meal access examples show population effects from small path changes. The cockpit example shows a different environmental intervention: train peers to question a captain rather than expecting the captain to defeat every mental error alone. Use the least coercive design that reliably addresses the failure mode. Verify that the new default serves the actual goal rather than simply increasing a convenient metric.

## 8. Treat collaboration as a cognitive system

- **When**: A partnership produces unusually rapid, high-quality insight and begins to attract status or criticism.
- **Move**: Preserve complementary roles while making credit, distance, conflict norms, and decision rights explicit.
- **Mechanism**: Shared attention can create ideas neither partner would generate alone, but status and criticism can destabilize the shared process.
- **Watch-out**: A joint byline or a shared history cannot substitute for current trust.

Kahneman’s doubt and Tversky’s formal, adversarial style were mutually amplifying in the room. The same differences later made feedback feel like injury, public credit feel like erasure, and physical separation harder to repair. Teams should record contributions and agree how to challenge ideas before success makes those questions existential. Review the relationship as an operating system, not only as a source of output.
