# Cheatsheet

## Fast diagnostic

1. What exactly is being predicted or chosen?
2. What is the base rate or reference class?
3. Which evidence is diagnostic, and which detail is merely vivid?
4. Is the sample large and representative enough for the claim?
5. Did an anchor, stereotype, ownership stake, or recent event set the frame?
6. Would the preference change if equivalent outcomes were described as gains and losses?
7. Is this a one-case decision or a repeated, population-level policy?
8. What would have happened without the intervention?
9. Who has permission to challenge the dominant interpretation?
10. What does the current story make easy to imagine that the data do not support?

## Prediction rules

- State the prior before the case narrative.
- Update in proportion to evidence quality, not detail count.
- Record confidence and forecast before the outcome.
- Judge a forecast over many cases; do not learn from one result.
- Treat a formal model as a disciplined benchmark, not a guarantee.

## Choice rules

- Identify the reference point: status quo, expectation, or recent change.
- Reframe gain/loss descriptions and test whether the preference survives.
- Separate probability from utility, regret, and responsibility.
- For a conjunction, the detailed case cannot be more probable than the broader case that contains it.
- For counterfactuals, distinguish “easy to imagine” from “likely to have happened.”

## Process design

- Decompose global judgments into specific, scored observations.
- Add an independent checker where the cost of anchoring is high.
- Use defaults and labels to reduce predictable friction, then measure unintended effects.
- Keep a comparison group or repeated baseline when evaluating feedback or policy.
- Protect collaborative credit and establish conflict rules before prestige arrives.

## Stop and investigate

Pause when a decision is driven mainly by a stereotype, a tiny sample, a dramatic recent event, a sunk cost, a near miss, or a story that makes the past look inevitable. The book’s models diagnose recurring errors; they do not replace domain expertise, current evidence, or ethical judgment.
