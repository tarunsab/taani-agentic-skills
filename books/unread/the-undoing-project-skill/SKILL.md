## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### Human judgment is a prediction problem

Many practical judgments are informal forecasts made with incomplete evidence. A useful process separates the question being predicted from the story that makes an answer feel plausible: establish a reference class or base rate, identify the evidence that should move the odds, and state uncertainty explicitly. Morey's basketball model, the Israeli Army interview, and the doctors' diagnostic comparisons all show the same principle in different settings. The model is a better answer, not a guarantee; unexplained context and bad measurements still matter.

### Heuristics are efficient rules with predictable failure modes

Representativeness substitutes similarity to a mental model for probability; availability substitutes ease of recall for frequency; anchoring and adjustment leave judgments too close to an arbitrary starting point. Conditionality also matters: people silently assume ordinary circumstances and fail to imagine disruption. These shortcuts are often useful because the world usually rewards fast pattern recognition, but they mislead systematically when samples are small, evidence is vivid but unrepresentative, or the story hides the logical relation among events. The practical response is to look for the footprint of the shortcut, not to declare intuition worthless.

### Regression and feedback must be separated

Extreme observations are often followed by less extreme ones even when no intervention has caused the change. The Israeli Air Force instructors who praised an exceptional performance and then saw a poorer one mistook regression toward the mean for a consequence of praise; criticism after a poor performance appeared to work for the same statistical reason. Evaluate an intervention over repeated observations and compare with a suitable baseline before rewarding or punishing it. Feedback can still matter, but an outcome immediately after feedback is weak evidence of causation.

### Prospect theory describes reference-dependent choice

People evaluate outcomes as changes from a reference point, feel losses more sharply than equal gains, and distort the impact of probabilities—especially remote ones. Framing the same outcomes as lives saved or deaths can reverse preferences, as in the Asian Disease problem. The reference point may be the status quo, an expectation, or a recent change, so the same final wealth can feel different to different people. Prospect theory is descriptive: it explains recurring deviations from expected utility; it does not say that risk-seeking losses or lottery purchases are wise.

### Counterfactual imagination is constrained, not unlimited

The simulation heuristic explains how people imagine what might have happened by undoing salient, unusual, or easily changed features of a real event. The mind tends to keep most of the scene fixed and alter a small number of plausible details, which makes some alternatives feel more available than others. This shapes regret, blame, grief, envy, and risk perception, but a vivid counterfactual is not a probability estimate. Use counterfactuals to expose hidden assumptions and preventable conditions, then return to evidence and base rates.

### Improve environments, not just intentions

Awareness of bias rarely disables a bias on command. Morey forced comparisons and valuation exercises; Redelmeier used an outsider's check; commercial aviation changed cockpit culture so subordinates could challenge a captain; Sunstein changed defaults and labels. These are choice-architecture and process interventions: move the decision to a setting where relevant evidence is visible, dissent is permitted, and the easiest path is safer. They reduce predictable errors without pretending that human nature can be trained away.

### Collaboration can create a shared instrument—and then break it

Kahneman and Tversky's most productive work came from complementary dispositions: one brought doubt, replication discipline, and psychological nuance; the other brought mathematical structure, speed, and combative theoretical ambition. Their joint method turned ordinary intuitions into experiments designed to expose hidden assumptions. The same intensity made credit, distance, status, criticism, and different needs for conflict difficult to manage. Treat intellectual partnership as a system with both cognitive gains and relationship costs.
