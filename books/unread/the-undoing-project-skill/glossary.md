# Glossary

## Allais paradox
A pair of choices in which people prefer certainty in one frame but violate the expected-utility independence axiom in a related frame. It motivates psychological accounts of risk and regret.

## Anchoring and adjustment
A judgment begins from an initial number or suggestion and moves away from it insufficiently. Generate an independent estimate before seeing the anchor.

## Availability heuristic
Estimating frequency or likelihood by the ease with which examples come to mind. Vividness, recency, and publicity can make recall misleading.

## Base rate
The prior frequency or probability of an outcome before case-specific evidence is considered. A description should move it only when the description is diagnostic.

## Behavioral economics
An approach that incorporates observed psychological behavior into economic models of choice rather than assuming perfect rationality.

## Choice architecture
The way defaults, labels, sequences, and environments shape decisions. It changes the path of least resistance without requiring perfect self-control.

## Conjunction fallacy
Judging a detailed conjunction as more probable than a broader event that contains it, usually because the details fit a representative story.

## Endowment effect
Valuing an owned object or position more highly simply because it is owned. It can distort trade, draft, and investment judgments.

## Expected utility
A normative model that weighs possible outcomes by their probabilities and utilities. Utility may rise at a decreasing rate with wealth, making risk aversion possible.

## Framing effect
A preference changes when equivalent outcomes are described differently. Gains and losses are especially powerful frames.

## Halo effect
One favorable or unfavorable global impression spills into judgments about separate traits. Structured ratings can limit the spillover.

## Heuristic
A fast rule of thumb that substitutes a simpler operation for a harder judgment. Heuristics are often useful and can still produce systematic errors.

## Hindsight bias
After an outcome, the past feels more predictable and the prior uncertainty is forgotten. Pre-outcome records help detect it.

## Law of small numbers
The mistaken expectation that a small sample will closely resemble its population or balance itself quickly.

## Loss aversion
Losses from a reference point usually have greater psychological weight than equal gains. It is descriptive, not a recommendation to avoid all risk.

## Peak-end rule
Retrospective evaluation is shaped disproportionately by the most intense moment and the ending rather than by the full duration.

## Prospect theory
The descriptive theory built around reference dependence, loss aversion, and psychologically weighted probabilities. It explains patterns that expected utility does not capture well.

## Reference point
The status quo, expectation, or other state against which an outcome is experienced as a gain or loss. It can move with context.

## Regression to the mean
An extreme observation tends to be followed by a less extreme one partly because of ordinary variation. It can be mistaken for the effect of feedback.

## Representativeness heuristic
Judging likelihood by similarity to a mental model or stereotype. It can neglect base rates, sample size, and logical containment.

## Simulation heuristic
Generating counterfactuals by mentally undoing salient, unusual, recent, or controllable features of an event.

## Tversky, Amos; Kahneman, Daniel
The collaborators at the center of the book. Their complementary styles joined formal models, psychological experiments, and practical questions about judgment under uncertainty.

## Utility versus value
Utility is the value assigned within a decision model; value in the book’s prospect-theory discussion is often change from a reference point. Neither is identical to objective money.
