# Patterns

## Search-and-grope

**When:** The opportunity is large but its final shape is not knowable in advance.  
**How:** Start from a strong technical or market intuition, build a small social and technical orbit around it, and let evidence and enthusiasm redirect the project. Clark's path from the telecomputer paper to Mosaic and then Netscape is the clearest example: the destination was not a plan, but the search kept encountering increasingly powerful conditions.  
**Trade-offs:** This preserves speed and discovery but can abandon people, create strategic whiplash, and mistake movement for learning. Set bounded experiments and record what evidence would justify a change of direction.

## Self-cannibalization

**When:** A successful product depends on a capability that is becoming cheap or widely available.  
**How:** Fund internal successors that make the current product obsolete, before a competitor does. Clark saw that Silicon Graphics had to attack its own high-end graphics position as computing commoditized.  
**Trade-offs:** The organization must accept near-term revenue loss and status loss; without a protected challenger, the core business will suppress it.

## Founder as author

**When:** A company is selling a future platform before the operating evidence is obvious.  
**How:** The founder supplies a vivid concept, selects the first cast of exceptional people, and shapes the public story; Netscape's browser narrative and Healtheon's physician metric made a complex future discussable.  
**Trade-offs:** Narrative accelerates capital and recruiting, but a story can outrun product-market fit. Keep the public promise, operating proxy, and falsifiable customer evidence separate.

## Talent funnel and one-piper leadership

**When:** A venture depends on a small number of unusually capable technical contributors.  
**How:** Use a trusted technical leader to recruit peers, create an environment where they can work at unusual intensity, and make ownership meaningful. Pavan's recruitment of the first Healtheon team shows the funnel; Clark's own authority keeps it moving.  
**Trade-offs:** The same dependency creates fragility, loyalty pressure, and a poaching risk. Build shared context and succession rather than relying on one charismatic source of legitimacy.

## Market-translation layer

**When:** Engineers can build a powerful system but customers, investors, or institutions cannot see how to adopt it.  
**How:** Add an operator who translates the technical ambition into a familiar institution, metric, and purchasing path. Mike Long's role at Healtheon is the book's “Serious American Executive” example.  
**Trade-offs:** Translation can flatten or redirect the original idea, and the translator may be treated as disposable until the company needs scale. Give the layer real authority and test the translated proposition with buyers.

## Commitment through ownership

**When:** The work requires long ambiguity and repeated setbacks.  
**How:** Convert interested participants into stakeholders through stock, responsibility, and a shared identity; the “chickens become pork” metaphor describes this shift.  
**Trade-offs:** Commitment increases persistence but can become sunk-cost entrapment. Pair incentives with explicit reorientation criteria and honest product reviews.

## Instrument-and-control loop

**When:** Software acts on a physical or operational system.  
**How:** Sense the system, represent it in software, grant commands through permissions, and provide a fallback when representation is wrong. Hyperion illustrates every stage—and the danger when the crew cannot tell which layer failed.  
**Trade-offs:** More sensors and access can increase complexity, false confidence, and blast radius. Design least privilege, local manual control, traceable causal paths, and drills that intentionally bypass automation.

## Abstract data plus tacit observation

**When:** The important failure may not be represented in the system's data model.  
**How:** Combine dashboards, logs, and code with direct observation by people who understand the underlying material process. Robert's engine knowledge and Jaime's ear for the mast detect what the computer misses.  
**Trade-offs:** Tacit expertise is hard to scale and can be dismissed as old-fashioned. Preserve it as a formal safety signal rather than treating it as anecdote.

## Chokepoint and underbelly

**When:** An incumbent controls the obvious interface, distribution channel, or standard.  
**How:** Identify the layer where the incumbent's power is concentrated, then enter through a new interface or platform that changes who can reach the customer. Netscape's browser challenged the Windows window; Healtheon aimed to sit between health-care participants.  
**Trade-offs:** The incumbent can retaliate, buy influence, or become a necessary partner. Legal remedies, platform agreements, and timing may matter as much as technical superiority.

## Proxy metric compression

**When:** Investors or decision-makers cannot inspect the whole future directly.  
**How:** Choose one observable measure—such as physicians using Healtheon—and use arithmetic to connect it to the desired outcome.  
**Trade-offs:** A good proxy creates focus; a weak one creates a persuasive but empty calculation. Track conversion to revenue, retention, and customer value alongside the proxy.
