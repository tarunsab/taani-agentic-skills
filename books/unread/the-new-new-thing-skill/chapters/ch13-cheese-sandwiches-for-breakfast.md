# Chapter 13: Cheese Sandwiches for Breakfast

## Core Idea

Healtheon's road show turns an uncertain platform into a traveling performance for investors. Mike Long uses a physician metric and a simple multiplication exercise to compress a complicated health-care future into a number that an audience can repeat. The story works while belief and market climate cooperate, but the practical conditions are absurdly fragile: Europe supplies missing equipment and bad sandwiches, journalists question Clark's temperament, and American investors immediately ask when Microsoft will enter. Morgan Stanley cancels the IPO, leaving Healtheon short of the capital it needs. Clark responds by financing the gap himself, demonstrating both conviction and the founder's ability to keep a fragile future alive when institutional confidence disappears.

## Frameworks Introduced

- The road show as a belief-production system.
- Proxy metrics and arithmetic as narrative compression.
- Founder capital as a bridge across a market-confidence failure.

## Key Concepts

The road show does not merely communicate a business; it tests whether a public audience is willing to lend its belief and money to a future. Long's “number of physicians” measure gives investors a handle for evaluating a platform whose revenue is still distant, while “you do the math” makes the audience participate in the extrapolation. But the metric can obscure whether physicians actually pay, stay, and create value for the institutions on the other side. When the market turns cold, no amount of presentation can fully substitute for a credible financing environment, so Clark's personal risk becomes a strategic asset and a concentration of risk.

## Mental Models

For every public proxy, write the conversion chain: proxy → usage → customer value → revenue → durable economics. The road show is useful when it makes the chain inspectable; it is dangerous when arithmetic is used to skip a link. A founder bridge should buy time for evidence, not merely postpone a verdict.

## Anti-patterns

- Choosing a metric because it is easy to count rather than because it predicts value.
- Confusing polished slides with product readiness.
- Treating a founder's willingness to fund the gap as validation of the business.

## Worked Example

Long multiplies a future population of physicians by an assumed annual value per physician to suggest multibillion-dollar revenue. The calculation gives Healtheon a simple public shape, but it does not resolve whether doctors will adopt the service or whether insurers and administrators will pay. When the IPO is canceled, Clark buys half of the needed $40 million himself at the failed-offering price. The episode shows how a proxy can mobilize capital and how quickly the mechanism is exposed when the market refuses the story.

## Distinctions & Limits

A metric is not its outcome, a persuasive road show is not product readiness, and founder capital is not independent validation. The physician count is useful because it makes a platform discussable, but the revenue arithmetic depends on adoption, payment, retention, and transaction value. Market climate can overwhelm a good presentation, just as a favorable climate can reward an unfinished business. Clark's rescue financing buys time while placing more downside on one person.

## Practical Application

For each investor-facing metric, write the causal chain from observed behavior to customer value and cash. Use road-show questions as discovery data: if every audience asks about the same incumbent or risk, address that dependency in the operating plan. Stress the assumptions with conservative, base, and failure cases before publishing an extrapolation. Treat bridge funding as a deliberate option with a time-bound evidence goal, not as a reason to postpone a hard decision indefinitely.

## Key Takeaways

- Narratives are operating infrastructure for companies that need future capital.
- A proxy must be linked to observed behavior and economics.
- Personal conviction can extend a venture, but it also concentrates downside.
