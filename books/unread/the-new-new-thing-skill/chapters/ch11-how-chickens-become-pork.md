# Chapter 11: How Chickens Become Pork

## Core Idea

The crew joins Clark's orbit for different reasons—computers, control, money, future status, or personal loyalty—but they become “pigs” when stock options and shared risk make them committed to the venture's outcome. Healtheon's first product is secure and technically respectable, yet Blue Cross and other institutional customers do not want it in the form the team built, and a Fidelity trial collapses. The engineers recognize the pattern from ITV: a life-consuming technical achievement has failed to find a market. They resist a mass departure through Pavan's loyalty matrix and blame the company's immediate-money orientation and weak translation layer rather than Clark, who continues to speak to engineers and protect their equity. Mike Long arrives as the serious executive who can make the ambition legible to institutions, while Clark forces the venture capitalists to keep funding a company they have begun to value at zero.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Chicken versus pig: interest versus commitment.
- Product-market fit as translation into an institution's actual incentives.
- The founder, operator, and investor as different necessary roles.

## Key Concepts

Commitment is economically useful because it keeps a team learning after the first customer rejection, but it also makes it difficult to admit that a product is misguided. The sales force pushes an administrative-benefits story while the engineers see no market, so the technical team eventually takes responsibility for selling itself and removes the intermediary. That is not enough: a large institution still needs a credible executive who can translate the platform into a purchase and partnership. Long restores the original scale of the vision, but the financing sequence shows that investors can be pulled back into risk by fear of missing the next public success.

## Mental Models

Separate three questions: are people committed, does the product solve a painful customer problem, and can the organization translate the solution into a buying process? Stock can answer the first question; it cannot answer the other two. Use commitment to fund learning, not to make a failed proposition morally untouchable.

## Anti-patterns

- Calling employees disloyal when they raise evidence of product failure.
- Treating a technically secure product as automatically wanted by the customer.
- Believing that an operator can be added without giving them real authority.

## Worked Example

Healtheon's Fidelity trial is expected to be worth about $20 million, but the deal collapses after the team has sacrificed its lives to make the system work. The result is described as “ITV again,” a recognition that the company has repeated the technical-success/commercial-failure pattern. Long's arrival does not make the original system suddenly more valuable; it supplies the institutional language, credibility, and strategic patience needed to change what the company is selling.

## Distinctions & Limits

Commitment answers whether people will stay; it does not answer whether customers want the product. Loyalty can protect a team from premature abandonment, but it can also make a bad proposition harder to change. The engineer, founder, salesperson, and serious executive contribute different forms of translation and should not be treated as interchangeable. Long's arrival helps Healtheon move forward, but it does not make the failed Fidelity trial evidence of fit.

## Practical Application

Hold customer reviews in which technical quality, user demand, buying authority, and adoption friction are scored separately. Let stock and mission reward persistence, but state the evidence that would trigger a product change or a team reorganization. Give a market translator budget and decision rights rather than asking them to polish a founder's story. Keep engineers close to customers so that translation does not sever the product from the underlying problem.

## Key Takeaways

- Commitment increases persistence but also raises the cost of changing course.
- Product-market fit is a relationship between technology and institutional incentives.
- Technical founders need translators without surrendering the insight that made the venture distinctive.
