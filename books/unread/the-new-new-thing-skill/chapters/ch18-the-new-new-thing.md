# Chapter 18: The New New Thing

## Core Idea

Lewis rejects the idea that Clark's successes are adequately explained by luck. He describes an interaction among Clark's technical feel, his taste for anarchy, and a late-1990s environment that rewarded both, with the Internet making outsiders' ideas capable of overturning whole industries. The new cultural condition creates “Internet anxiety”: established executives sense that a person operating in Clark's spirit may make their business obsolete, as Amazon, eBay, and online brokerages demonstrate. Once Healtheon has become a successful public company, Clark's attention drains away from it and turns first toward changing the experience of being rich. The myCFO concept organizes the wealthy masses through an Internet financial home, while Clark's moving wealth targets reveal that dissatisfaction, resentment, and curious amnesia keep his search alive even after he has achieved the goal he once named.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Luck as an interaction between character and environment rather than a sufficient explanation.
- Internet anxiety as the incumbent's experience of disruptive possibility.
- Dissatisfaction and selective forgetting as engines of perpetual renewal.

## Key Concepts

Paul Romer's idea that small chance outcomes can produce a very different world helps explain why Clark matters without making him a solitary cause. The Internet boom is a chemistry experiment in which capital, talent, interfaces, and public belief amplify one another; Clark is an active ingredient, not the entire reaction. His retirement promises keep changing—from an after-tax billion, to more than Larry Ellison, to briefly wanting the most—because the goal is motivational rather than terminal. The tuba in the guest room and the boxes of old papers show that escape from the past is incomplete, even when forgetting is necessary for the next self-renewal.

## Mental Models

Use a character–environment interaction model. Ask which traits the surrounding market rewards, which institutions suppress them, and what happens when the reward system scales faster than the person's judgment. For an ambitious founder, distinguish a motivating target from a stable definition of enough; otherwise the search can become self-perpetuating dissatisfaction.

## Anti-patterns

- Explaining an outcome with luck while ignoring preparation, taste, timing, and institutions.
- Treating a public success as a natural stopping point for a person whose identity depends on the next challenge.
- Assuming a new financial interface solves trust, governance, or service problems automatically.

## Worked Example

After Healtheon's IPO, Clark buys a large mansion partly to test whether boat software can administer a home, then abandons that lead and turns to myCFO. He proposes serving wealthy clients who want lower taxes and less financial hassle by pooling their data and bargaining power against banks and brokers. The idea grows from a personal grievance into a possible financial institution, but it is still deliberately vague when he wants it to become a multibillion-dollar public company within a year. The example captures both his gift for finding a large system behind a small irritation and his tendency to move before the institution has been designed.

## Distinctions & Limits

The chapter distinguishes luck from a chance interaction among prepared character, technical judgment, and a receptive environment. It also distinguishes dissatisfaction as a motive from ambition as a plan: the former can keep motion alive without identifying a good destination. A new idea is not yet a company, and myCFO's large aggregation thesis leaves trust and execution unresolved. Clark's case is illuminating precisely because it is unusual, not because it is a normal founder template.

## Practical Application

When explaining a success, list the traits, institutions, timing, and chance encounters that interacted rather than naming one cause. Audit whether a new target is solving a real problem or merely restoring suspense after a success. Turn a personal grievance into a falsifiable service hypothesis and test it with the people who would have to trust the institution. Set a stopping rule before the next target moves the goalposts again.

## Key Takeaways

- Disruption is produced by people and environments together.
- A founder's dissatisfaction can generate extraordinary motion and poor stopping rules.
- Reusable insight is not the same as a validated company.
