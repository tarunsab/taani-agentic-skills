# Chapter 12: New New Money

## Core Idea

Clark's wealth does not stabilize his life; it raises the size of the next target and intensifies his dislike of financial intermediaries. A Swiss banker tries to place him on a conservative-to-aggressive risk scale, but his portfolio is concentrated in Netscape, Healtheon, and other new companies, with almost no conventional diversification. He then turns the inconvenience of managing wealth into myCFO, a proposed Internet-based institution for the “wealthy masses” that would pool data and bargaining power against banks, brokers, insurers, and accountants. The concept is intentionally more concrete and profit-oriented than the era's advertising-funded Web companies, but it still begins as a vague desire to reorganize a large system. The chapter shows Clark applying the new-new-thing method to money itself: identify an underorganized resource, put it under one interface, and use scale to alter the terms imposed by incumbents.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Concentrated reinvestment rather than conventional diversification.
- Pooling data and assets to create bargaining power.
- “Old Internet thinking” versus a profitable service with fewer, wealthier users.

## Key Concepts

Clark believes a family office should be observable through the Internet, not merely staffed by money butlers who operate out of sight. His idea is to gather financial records, tax planning, and transactions in one place, then negotiate as if the customers' assets were a single giant pool. The proposed model charges substantial annual fees and aims at real revenue rather than attention alone, reflecting his belief that the revolution has consumed the revolutionary. The limitation is visible even in the pitch: a large market and a strong bargaining position do not establish trust, service quality, regulatory viability, or the willingness of wealthy clients to pool control.

## Mental Models

Use a “resource concentration” test. If an interface can aggregate money, data, or demand, ask whether aggregation creates a durable negotiating advantage, what trust and governance it requires, and which incumbent can retaliate. High concentration can increase leverage and increase the blast radius of error at the same time.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a large asset pool as if customers will automatically consent to centralization.
- Mistaking a profitable arithmetic model for a validated service.
- Using a new interface to remove intermediaries without replacing their trust and accountability functions.

## Worked Example

Clark tells bankers that 180,000 American decamillionaires control roughly fifteen trillion dollars and could be organized into a negotiating unit. He imagines a single financial home that can bargain with banks and insurance companies, much as an operating system sits above a computer. The pitch is clear enough to attract John Chambers, Tom Jermoluk, and other Valley figures, yet its business plan is still a “groping” hypothesis rather than an operating design. Its strength is the leverage insight; its risk is that the most important work—earning trust and serving complex clients—has not yet been specified.

## Distinctions & Limits

Aggregation can create bargaining power, but it also creates a trust and governance obligation. A profitable revenue calculation is not evidence that wealthy clients will pool data, accept a central intermediary, or receive better service. Clark's concentrated portfolio is a motivational bet, not an example of prudent diversification. The concept is a new financial institution in embryo, not a finished operating or regulatory model.

## Practical Application

Before aggregating money or sensitive data, test customer consent, fiduciary responsibility, security, and the exact benefit produced by scale. Identify which traditional intermediary functions—trust, advice, accountability, and dispute resolution—must be rebuilt. Model concentration risk separately from upside and define who can stop a harmful transaction. Use the large-market calculation to prioritize discovery, not to skip the service design.

## Key Takeaways

- Clark continually turns personal friction into a possible institution.
- Pooling can convert fragmented demand into bargaining power.
- A market-size calculation must be followed by a trust, adoption, and execution model.
