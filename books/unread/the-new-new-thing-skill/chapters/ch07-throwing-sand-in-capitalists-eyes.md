# Chapter 7: Throwing Sand in Capitalists' Eyes

## Core Idea

Clark approaches venture capital as a market whose rules he can redesign because his conceptual authority is more scarce than capital. He wants a large share of the company and no operating job, arguing that his contribution is to originate the idea and attract exceptional engineers while others perform routine company work. The young venture capitalist Hugh Reinhoff is assigned to follow him, because the investors understand that the founder's attention is the asset they must keep. Clark recruits from SGI, reacts aggressively to interference, and divides equity on terms that leave investors with less control than they expect. The Magic Diamond gives investors a large market story, but the company still needs people who can turn the concept into a product and a customer relationship.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Founder-market power: scarce vision can dominate abundant capital.
- Concept-first venture formation.
- Venture capital as translation and access rather than mere funding.

## Key Concepts

Clark's refusal to produce a conventional business plan is both a deliberate filter and a genuine risk. He is looking for passionate, highly capable engineers who can make a path appear after the starting concept, and he expects the environment to change while they work. The venture capitalists try to add planning and management, but their leverage is limited because the founder can create another company or take the idea elsewhere. The arrangement works only because Clark's track record makes the missing plan seem like evidence of originality rather than evidence of unpreparedness.

## Mental Models

Model the early venture as a bundle of scarce assets: concept, technical talent, founder attention, capital, customer access, and translation. Ownership should reflect which asset is truly constraining progress, but the bundle is unstable if one person controls too many links. A useful investment question is not simply “is the idea large?” but “what makes this team uniquely able to learn the next step?”

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a charismatic founder's lack of planning as a universal virtue.
- Adding a business plan after the fact without testing the customer mechanism.
- Assuming financiers can be replaced even when institutional translation is the bottleneck.

## Worked Example

Clark's health-care drawing places an Internet intermediary at the center of a huge administrative market. He wants the best engineers first and says the rest of the structure can follow; Pavan is the recruit whose authority helps make that possible. The investors accept a split that gives Clark four parts, Kleiner Perkins four, and NEA two, because they fear losing the opportunity more than they fear losing control. The deal captures both the power of a rare founder and the governance risk of an organization built around one person's next impulse.

## Distinctions & Limits

There is a difference between refusing a premature plan and refusing to learn. Clark's absence from operations can protect conceptual work, but it also makes governance and accountability easier to evade. Capital is abundant only relative to the scarce combination of technical taste, talent, and founder attention. The chapter describes a high-performing exception, not evidence that ordinary ventures should copy the same ownership or planning arrangements.

## Practical Application

Map the venture's scarce assets before negotiating terms, and state which decisions each contributor can actually make. If a founder will not operate the company, appoint an accountable operator with authority over delivery, customer commitments, and safety. Release capital against learning milestones rather than against the founder's aura alone. Preserve a channel through which investors and engineers can challenge the concept without turning challenge into a contest of status.

## Key Takeaways

- Capital is not always the scarce resource; attention, taste, and exceptional talent may be.
- A founder who refuses operations still needs an accountable operating layer.
- Venture terms should expose, not hide, the dependency on one person's judgment.
