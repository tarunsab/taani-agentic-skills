# Glossary

**@Home** — A cable/Internet company in the Valley and one of the firms surrounding Clark's ventures; later part of the boom's consolidation story (Ch 2, 16).

**Anarchy** — Clark's preferred condition for organizing capital, technology, and entrepreneurial action outside inherited institutional routines (Ch 7, 12, 18).

**APIs** — Application interfaces that let software use an operating system's capabilities; Microsoft's control of Windows APIs became a strategic choke point in the browser conflict (Ch 14).

**Business model** — The proposed mechanism for making money; the phrase often disguised uncertainty during the Internet boom (Ch 5, 12).

**Cannibalization** — Deliberately undermining a successful product with a better or cheaper successor before a rival does it (Ch 4).

**Chart of Many Bubbles** — Long's visual account of health-care participants and transactions, with Healtheon placed as the intermediary that could remove waste (Ch 12).

**ClarkWorld** — The self-contained world of Clark's ideas, machines, engineers, and rules, especially visible in the boat project (Ch 6–10).

**Disorganization Man** — Lewis's Silicon Valley counterpart to William H. Whyte's conformist “Organization Man”: the odd, mobile, technically gifted upstart (Ch 4).

**God Mode** — Hyperion's highest access level, allowing a user to control the whole boat; a metaphor for centralized capability without corresponding understanding (Ch 10, 15).

**Graham and Dodd investor** — A value-oriented investor associated with profits, analysis, and skepticism toward speculative growth (Ch 12, 16).

**Healtheon / Healthscape** — Clark's health-care venture, later renamed Healtheon after the desired name was unavailable; it aimed to connect fragmented administrative transactions (Ch 5, 8, 11, 17).

**Hyperion** — Clark's large computerized sailing yacht, built as a moving laboratory for sensors, software, remote control, and the Home of the Future (Ch 1, 6, 9–10, 15–16, Epilogue).

**IIT** — The Indian Institutes of Technology system, presented as a national pipeline that identifies and trains exceptional technical talent (Ch 8).

**Internet anxiety** — The fear that an outsider using Internet methods will make an established business obsolete (Ch 18).

**IPO** — Initial public offering; in the book it turns Netscape and Healtheon into liquid public stories before conventional profitability is established (Ch 5, 13, 17).

**Kamikaze investor** — The boom-era investor who follows hunches, charts, rumors, and momentum, often holding through large theoretical gains and losses (Ch 16).

**Magic Diamond** — Clark's early drawing placing health care's participants and transactions around an Internet intermediary; the first compact model for Healtheon (Ch 4–5, 7–8).

**Mosaic** — The early Web browser associated with Marc Andreessen; Clark saw it as the seed of a mass Internet platform and commissioned a “Mosaic killer” (Ch 5, 8).

**Netscape** — The company that commercialized the browser, made Clark and engineers wealthy, and helped give Internet entrepreneurship public credibility (Ch 5, 14, 17–18, Epilogue).

**New Growth Theory** — Romer's account of growth as the result of ideas and new combinations, with nonconformism as a productive social input (Ch 2, 18).

**New new thing** — A technology or business concept just before broad commercial acceptance, plus the continual search for the next such opportunity (Preface, Ch 18).

**Organization Man** — Whyte's mid-century corporate ideal of the well-rounded, conformist employee whose identity is contained by a large institution (Ch 4).

**Physician metric** — Long's proposed measure of Healtheon's progress: how many doctors use the service, used to make an abstract platform legible to investors (Ch 13).

**Portal** — A gateway site or interface that organizes access to many services; the browser-to-portal path is part of Clark's theory of Internet leverage (Ch 14).

**SCADA / PLC chain** — The industrial-control components and software path through which Hyperion's commands and sensor values travel; the chain made failures hard to localize (Ch 15–16).

**Seascape** — The programmers and project surrounding Hyperion's software, a small technical culture where patches and personal coding styles shaped the boat (Ch 9–10).

**Serious American Executive** — Lewis's shorthand for a conventional business leader who must now respond to Internet-driven change (Ch 8, 18).

**Silicon Valley Part One / Part Two** — Lewis's contrast between hardware and the engineers who built machines, and software/ideas that recombine them into scalable wealth (Ch 2).

**Social Ethic** — Whyte's description of the conformity and belonging pressures behind the Organization Man (Ch 4).

**Technology for money / technology for pleasure** — A distinction Clark resists; for him programming, machines, and ventures are part of one continuous appetite for mastery and possibility (Ch 4, 6).

**Trojan horse** — The Internet as an apparently small technical layer that enters and reshapes larger industries from inside (Preface, Ch 5, 14).

**Vertical market** — A specialized industry transformed through a platform, such as health care; Clark expected the browser and Internet to move upward into such markets (Ch 5, 14).

**Wealthy masses** — Clark's name for the large population of decamillionaires he hoped to organize through myCFO (Ch 18).
