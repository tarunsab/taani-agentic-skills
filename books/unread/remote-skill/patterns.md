# Patterns

## Four-Hour Overlap
**When to use**: A team spans time zones but still needs timely collaboration.
**How**: Shift local schedules until a meaningful shared window exists, then use the non-overlap time for focus.
**Trade-offs**: Early starts, late finishes, or less real-time collaboration may be required; rare talent can justify a larger gap, but labor savings alone do not.

## Shared Project Home
**When to use**: Files, decisions, tasks, and instructions are scattered across private inboxes and machines.
**How**: Put discussions, files, calendars, tasks, and decisions in one accessible project space; pair it with a shared code repository when needed.
**Trade-offs**: The team must maintain the shared record, but the saved waiting time outweighs the discipline.

## Screen-First Collaboration
**When to use**: Words alone make a design, demo, presentation, or feature difficult to discuss.
**How**: Share the screen live or record a narrated screencast for later review. Focus on the object of work rather than simulating eye contact.
**Trade-offs**: Screen sharing loses some high-fidelity interaction and recordings can become a time sink if over-produced; “good enough” is the intended standard.

## 80/15/5 Communication Routing
**When to use**: An office has trained people to interrupt one another for every question.
**How**: Route most questions to email, near-term questions to chat/IM, and true crises to the phone. Let the chosen medium keep its appropriate response time.
**Trade-offs**: The transition creates frustration, and email fails if senders still expect ten-minute responses.

## Weekly Progress Thread
**When to use**: Remote colleagues lack the ambient sense of what the company is doing.
**How**: Ask everyone to state what they worked on and what they intend next in a shared thread.
**Trade-offs**: It is not a precise estimation or coordination system; its value is visibility, shared momentum, and peer accountability.

## Meaningful Remote Pilot
**When to use**: An established company wants evidence before changing its default work model.
**How**: Include an entire team, project management, and stakeholders; provide the tools; run the trial for long enough to pass the adjustment period; begin with a few remote days and iterate.
**Trade-offs**: A team-sized pilot exposes real coordination costs, but a lone remote employee is not a valid test because only that person experiences the downside.

## Remote Client Trust Sequence
**When to use**: A client worries that a distant supplier will be unavailable or opaque.
**How**: Disclose location early, offer references, show work often, stay responsive, and invite the client into a shared project space.
**Trade-offs**: Remote work demands more deliberate communication, but visible progress and participation replace the reassurance of a local office.

## Work-Evidence Hiring
**When to use**: Résumés, credentials, or puzzle interviews are weak proxies for performance.
**How**: Read the cover letter, inspect role-specific work, use a paid real-world test project, and meet finalists for character and team fit.
**Trade-offs**: It takes evaluation time and money, but it reveals writing, judgment, pace, and execution more directly than parlor tricks.

## Level-the-Field Management
**When to use**: Office workers have access to hallway decisions, better audio, or more visibility than remote workers.
**How**: Keep discussions and work artifacts online, improve shared audio/screen tools, and have leaders work remotely often enough to feel the friction.
**Trade-offs**: Online records require discipline, but equality cannot depend on remote workers repeatedly asking to be included.

## Default Access and Decision Rights
**When to use**: Work pauses while a person waits for an approver, deployer, purchase authorization, or information.
**How**: Give reasonable people authority, make needed information available by default, and treat good-faith mistakes as part of learning.
**Trade-offs**: Delegation requires judgment and auditability; permission rituals may feel safer to managers while producing less value.

## Sustainable Remote Boundaries
**When to use**: Flexible schedules and home access are turning work into an always-on hobby.
**How**: Set an average around forty hours, ask whether the day was a good day’s work, model stopping, encourage hobbies and vacations, and use a different leisure device or account.
**Trade-offs**: A short sprint may sometimes be necessary, but hero hours are not a sustainable performance strategy.

## Scenery Rotation
**When to use**: Routine has numbed attention or complete isolation makes focus harder.
**How**: Rotate deliberately among a home office, café, library, park, coworking space, and office according to the task.
**Trade-offs**: Public settings add noise and connectivity risks; the goal is a better fit for a specific task, not a universal best location.
