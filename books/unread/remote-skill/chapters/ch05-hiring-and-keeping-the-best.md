# Chapter 5: Hiring and Keeping the Best

## Core Idea

Remote hiring widens the talent pool, but it also raises the standard for written communication, self-direction, and evidence of actual ability. The authors argue that once a team learns remote habits, distance itself matters much less than time-zone overlap, legal setup, and communication quality. Retention is equally important: an employee who moves already carries knowledge, relationships, and proof of performance, so losing that person because of geography is wasteful. Their hiring method is deliberately concrete—read the cover letter, inspect real work, pay for a short test project, meet finalists for character and team fit, and use contractors as a mutual trial. Compensation should value contribution rather than local cost of living, while the social atmosphere should actively protect optimism, respect, and a broad human life.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Think globally, then solve the constraints**: Search the world for talent once the team can support remote work.
  - When to use: When a local market is narrow, expensive, or dominated by competing employers.
  - How: Check overlap, language, legal and accounting requirements, and cultural perspective before hiring.
- **Retain the mover**: Preserve proven talent when an employee relocates for personal reasons.
  - When to use: When a trusted employee wants or needs to move away from headquarters.
  - How: Change the location requirement while keeping the relationship, responsibilities, and knowledge.
- **No Broken Windows**: Correct small snippy, passive-aggressive, or poisonous behaviors before they become remote-team culture.
  - When to use: When distance makes tone and morale problems easy to miss.
  - How: Address behavior early and make “no asshole-y behavior” a team expectation.
- **Work over abstractions**: Evaluate candidates through the work they would actually do, not riddles, pedigree, or résumé signals alone.
  - When to use: When hiring at scale across many locations.
  - How: Read the writing, request a real work sample, and use a paid project that solves a meaningful problem.
- **Smart and Gets Things Done**: Look for both judgment/ability and reliable execution.
  - When to use: When a candidate sounds impressive but evidence of output is limited.
  - How: Inspect quality, pace, iteration, and whether the person turns a fuzzy task into a useful result.
- **Equal pay for equal work**: Compensate remote contributors for the value of their work rather than discounting them by geography.
  - When to use: When recruiting outside expensive talent hubs.
  - How: Use strong compensation to attract and retain people who would otherwise be vulnerable to poaching.

## Key Concepts

- **Global talent pool**: The full set of potential contributors beyond a company’s local geography.
- **Remote retention**: Keeping a proven employee after a move rather than treating relocation as resignation.
- **No Broken Windows**: A norm of addressing small cultural damage before it spreads.
- **Work sample**: Evidence of the actual skill a role requires.
- **Pre-hiring**: A paid one- or two-week mini-project used before a full hire.
- **Cover letter filter**: Using a candidate’s explanation of fit as an early test of written communication.
- **Smart**: The ability and judgment needed for the work, as distinct from sounding clever.
- **Gets Things Done**: The ability to turn work into consistent progress and finished output.
- **Equal pay for equal work**: Compensation that does not penalize a contributor for living in a cheaper place.
- **Contractor trial**: A limited relationship that lets both sides test remote habits and fit.

## Mental Models

Use **evidence over proxy**: a real artifact says more about fit than a riddle, school, or résumé line. Use **retention as compounding**: experienced teammates carry context that new hires must rebuild. Use **team atmosphere as infrastructure**: optimism, clarity, and respectful tone are operational requirements in a written environment. Use **geography as leverage**: paying well outside hubs can reduce poaching and make the company unusually attractive.

## Anti-patterns

- **Geographic discounting**: Paying less for identical work because the employee lives somewhere cheaper.
- **Riddle recruitment**: Treating puzzle skill as a reliable proxy for job performance.
- **Résumé-only hiring**: Trusting past claims without seeing the candidate solve a current, meaningful problem.
- **Remote robot ideal**: Hiring for output while ignoring character, personal life, and team tone.
- **Letting small poison spread**: Ignoring snippy messages and passive aggression because they are hard to see remotely.
- **Throwing away movers**: Replacing a proven employee with a stranger solely because the person changed cities.

## It’s a big world

Once remote habits are in place, the difference between same city, same country, and another continent becomes much smaller than employers expect. 37signals began with Chicago and Copenhagen and later hired around the world, finding that the main remaining constraints were overlap, legal and accounting rules, language, and communication. International hiring also helps a product team notice cultural details, such as differing calendar conventions, and can give a firm a broader perspective with clients. The global search should not romanticize distance: weak writing or an impossible time-zone arrangement can still make a candidate a poor fit.

## Life moves on

Employees move for marriage, family, climate, or a new scene even when they love their jobs. A long-serving employee is especially valuable remotely because the person already understands the work, relationships, and company norms, so replacing that person discards accumulated knowledge and incurs hiring cost. 37signals retained people through moves from Chicago to Marbella, Utah to Idaho, and across several U.S. cities; Jellyvision and American Fidelity Assurance used similar situations to expand remote work. Retention is therefore a practical reason to remove location requirements, not just an act of sentiment.

## Keep the good times going

Distance makes the human connection more important because written communication removes the wink, facial expression, and tone that can stop a small misunderstanding. The authors recommend hiring optimistic people, continuously watching the atmosphere, and addressing minor snippy or passive-aggressive behavior under a “No Broken Windows” logic. FreeAgent’s experience shows that learning to get email and Basecamp tone right takes practice, and bad tone can occur even without bad intent. Remote culture needs an explicit norm against asshole-y behavior, drama, and bad vibes because the manager cannot always see dread developing.

## Seeking a human

Remote workers can end up with a narrower life than people who work on a large campus full of casual activities, so hiring and management should value more than a person’s ability to produce. The authors encourage people who have interests outside work and describe 37signals sponsoring travel, hobbies, and time off for activities such as cycling, gardening, trekking, and motorsports. Diverse life experience can feed better creative and professional work, while memorable experiences help people stay engaged for the long term. The point is not to manufacture leisure as a perk but to make room for humans whose work benefits from a broad life.

## No parlor tricks

Riddles and abstract quizzes are indirect signals with weak connection to the actual fit of a role and many opportunities for false negatives. The authors prefer copywriters’ copy, programmers’ code, designers’ designs, marketers’ campaigns, and realistic answers to customer-support questions. 37signals used real product questions in support applications so candidates could research and demonstrate the kind of response the job requires. A work sample matters even more for remote workers because the work itself will be the main channel through which colleagues experience their ability.

## The cost of thriving

Recruiting from a cheaper place solely to reduce payroll misunderstands the opportunity. Paying a star worker a big-city salary in a lower-cost location can make the person feel valued and can protect the company from local poachers who cannot easily match the offer. 37signals used this approach for a support person in Tennessee, a programmer in Idaho, and a designer in Oklahoma, with unusually long technology-industry tenures. Equal pay for equal work also protects the remote worker from being treated as second-class in other decisions.

## Great remote workers are simply great workers

Remote work makes it harder to substitute sociability, visibility, or office hours for results because shared repositories and progress records leave a clearer trail. The authors use Joel Spolsky’s shorthand “Smart” and “Gets Things Done” for the two qualities to inspect: capability and judgment on one hand, reliable execution on the other. Repeatedly flawed work can reveal insufficient ability, while long gaps between useful installments can reveal weak execution. The office often hides both weaknesses behind niceness and attendance; remote work makes the actual pattern easier to see.

## On writing well

Because remote collaboration happens largely through email, chat, and discussion boards, strong writing is a core operating skill rather than a cosmetic advantage. The authors recommend judging the cover letter quickly and ruthlessly for clarity and fit, since a résumé is often embellished and a large applicant pool demands a fast first filter. They emphasize reading and studying good prose, prioritizing clarity before style, and point readers toward On Writing Well, The Elements of Style, and Revising Prose. The exception is a role with genuinely little written collaboration, where other strengths may take priority, but even there good writing remains useful.

## Test project

Past work is valuable but difficult to interpret because the employer may not know who did it, under what constraints, or how efficiently it was produced. The authors therefore use “pre-hiring”: a meaningful, paid mini-project lasting roughly one week for an unemployed candidate or two weeks for someone with a current job. A designer might redesign one screen, a programmer build a small application, or a writer write; the task should solve a real problem rather than a puzzle. Paying around $1,500 in their example reflects the principle that a company should not ask candidates to do work for free.

## Meeting them in person

After skill and work evidence narrow the field to two or three candidates, the authors still want an in-person read on character and cultural fit. Their process brings each finalist to Chicago for an informal lunch, often with potential teammates rather than only the manager, followed by time in the office and a peer discussion about respect, kindness, and fit. Video group chat is a weaker substitute when an in-person meeting is impossible, and a short initial orientation at the office can help a remote hire learn faces, names, and norms. The final decision blends talent and character rather than treating either as sufficient alone.

## Contractors know the drill

Contract work is a useful training ground because a contractor must set a reasonable schedule, show progress, and turn a fuzzy request into a deliverable. It also lets both the company and worker test one another without assuming a permanent relationship before remote habits are proven. A contractor who has experienced dysfunctional clients may recognize and value a company with trust and good work practices. The arrangement is not a guarantee of fit, but it is a lower-commitment way to observe the behaviors remote work demands.

## Worked Example

The book’s hiring path is a chain of increasingly direct evidence. A manager first reads a clear cover letter, then asks for role-specific work, then pays for a one- or two-week real project, and finally brings two or three finalists into a team-centered in-person meeting. The candidate is judged on skill, pace, writing, character, respect, and collaboration, while the candidate also gets to see whether the company is worth joining.

## Key Takeaways

1. Search globally once the team can handle overlap, language, and compliance.
2. Retain proven people through moves; experience and trust compound.
3. Hire for written clarity, optimism, character, and actual work—not puzzle performance.
4. Use paid, meaningful test projects to see how candidates solve real problems.
5. Evaluate both “Smart” and “Gets Things Done.”
6. Pay for contribution rather than geography and protect the social atmosphere deliberately.
