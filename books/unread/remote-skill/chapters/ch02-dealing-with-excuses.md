# Chapter 2: Dealing with Excuses

## Core Idea

The objections to remote work are usually objections to trust, uncertainty, habit, and loss of control rather than proofs that distance makes work impossible. The authors answer them by shrinking exaggerated claims: most work is follow-through rather than breakthrough ideation, office presence does not prevent distraction, and physical proximity does not guarantee security or productivity. They replace visibility with explicit systems—shared artifacts, calibrated communication, output-based evaluation, security basics, and role-specific coverage. Some resistance is emotional and may require a gradual experiment rather than a better argument, and some bosses or roles may remain incompatible. The recurring decision rule is to solve the actual work problem, not preserve a familiar arrangement for its own sake.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Ration the magic**: Use in-person gatherings as scarce, high-value opportunities rather than the default answer to every idea.
  - When to use: When meetings are multiplying faster than the organization can execute on ideas.
  - How: Finish or advance existing work, use voice and shared screens for most collaboration, and reserve gatherings for work that truly benefits from them.
- **Trust adults, or change the team**: Treat trust as a hiring and management choice, not as a surveillance problem.
  - When to use: When a manager fears that unseen employees will stop working.
  - How: Hire capable people, judge results, address poor performance, and do not use monitoring as a substitute for judgment.
- **Graduated communication**: Match the medium to urgency—roughly 80% email, 15% chat/IM, and 5% phone for true crises.
  - When to use: When office interruption habits have become an ASAP culture.
  - How: Let non-urgent questions wait, use concise chat for near-term needs, and reserve calls for genuinely urgent matters.
- **Culture as behavior**: Define culture by recurring values and actions, not by office social events or written slogans.
  - When to use: When leaders claim remote work will dissolve culture.
  - How: Make quality, customer treatment, workload, communication, and risk preferences visible in everyday decisions.
- **Gradual exposure to control loss**: Reduce a manager’s fear through a small, observable remote experiment.
  - When to use: When opposition is emotional and arguments are not changing behavior.
  - How: Start with one predictable remote day, show the work, and increase flexibility only as confidence grows.

## Key Concepts

- **Brain trust**: A group assembled for high-level idea generation; the authors argue it should be summoned sparingly.
- **Low expectations effect**: People tend to live down to a manager’s assumption that they are slackers.
- **Security checklist**: Basic device, account, and authentication practices that make remote access safer.
- **Culture**: The organization’s spoken and unspoken values expressed through actions.
- **ASAP addiction**: The reflex to demand an immediate answer regardless of actual urgency.
- **Sunk cost**: Past spending that should not determine whether a future work setting is productive.
- **Role-specific policy**: A work arrangement shaped by the requirements of a job, not forced uniformly on unrelated jobs.
- **Output yardstick**: Judging work by what was produced rather than by attendance or visible activity.

## Mental Models

Use **the office test**: if a person can waste time, lose focus, or leak information from a desk, office presence is not the control the manager imagines. Use **the urgency ladder**: classify a request before choosing a communication channel. Use **the sunk-cost test**: compare future productivity, not past rent. Use **the culture-through-actions test**: watch decisions and behavior rather than perks.

## Anti-patterns

- **Surveillance as management**: Monitoring screens because hiring and output evaluation are weak.
- **Meeting as breakthrough theater**: Pulling people into a room whenever enthusiasm appears, without capacity to execute the ideas.
- **ASAP for everything**: Treating a non-urgent question as permission to interrupt someone’s focus.
- **Uniformity disguised as fairness**: Requiring all roles to share a location even when their work has different physical constraints.
- **Office rent as a policy**: Keeping an unproductive arrangement to justify money already spent.
- **Puzzle hiring**: Inferring job fit from riddles or abstract quizzes instead of real work.

## Magic only happens when we’re all in a room

The authors concede that face-to-face meetings can produce high-fidelity interaction, then ask how many breakthrough ideas a company can actually absorb. Most work is improving and finishing existing ideas, so frequent “Kodak moments” either abandon the last idea or create a stale backlog of new ones. At 37signals, the whole company met only about three times a year, and voice connections plus shared screens captured most collective thought with only a small loss of fidelity. Scarcity made the in-person gatherings feel special instead of turning them into routine interruptions.

## If I can’t see them, how do I know they’re working?

The visibility objection assumes people are productive because a manager can see their desks, but office workers can waste time just as easily; the book cites J.C. Penney headquarters consuming 30% of Internet bandwidth on YouTube as an example. Low expectations can become self-fulfilling, while capable adults generally respond better to trust and responsibility. The IT Collective’s Chris Hoffman summarizes the alternative: if someone cannot manage their work or produce results, the hiring decision was poor and the person should not remain on the team. Remote work therefore exposes a management problem that surveillance cannot solve.

## People’s homes are full of distractions

Homes can contain more temptations than cubicles, but the authors argue that fulfilling work is the strongest counter to distraction. Repeated distraction can be diagnostic: it may signal that the task is boring, poorly defined, or pointless rather than that the worker lacks character. Environment still matters, so a dedicated room, closed door, café, library, or park may be better than a couch in front of the television. If the work remains deadening everywhere, the answer is a different job, not merely a stricter remote-work rule.

## Only the office can be secure

Security is serious, but the authors say the relevant question is whether the system uses basic protections consistently, not whether a building looks like a castle. Their 37signals checklist covers full-disk encryption, disabled automatic login and short idle locks, HTTPS/SSL, lockable and remotely wipeable mobile devices, unique generated passwords stored in a password manager, and two-factor authentication for email. Email deserves special attention because a compromised inbox can reset passwords for other services. The practical lesson is to learn and enforce ordinary security controls rather than leaving a false gap between “inside” and “outside” the office.

## Who will answer the phone?

Customers still need timely responses during their normal business hours, but that requirement belongs to customer-facing functions rather than automatically to every employee. Jellyvision asks clients not to schedule meetings before 10am, while 37signals staggers support shifts so coverage extends across the day without forcing everyone into one schedule. Occasional odd-hour calls may be a reasonable trade for broader freedom, especially when the need is exceptional. Small teams may need regular hours for the people answering customers, but imposing the same hours on unrelated roles is false equality.

## Big business doesn’t do it, so why should we?

Large companies can carry inefficiency and bureaucracy for years, so their policies are not reliable productivity advice. Innovation often comes from doing what incumbents have not yet normalized, and an industry leader’s office-only habits may create an opening for a more flexible competitor. The authors also note that remote work was already used by large organizations such as IBM, S.C. Johnson, Accenture, and eBay, so “big business” is not a coherent veto. The decision should be based on the work and the company’s confidence, not on copying the herd.

## Others would get jealous

Fairness does not require every job to use the same location; it requires judging people by the contribution and constraints of their roles. A warehouse worker who must access inventory and an accountant who can work from home already have different requirements, so one policy cannot make their jobs identical. If remote work benefits a role, let people use it and hold them to the same team purpose and output standard. The authors’ most useful reframing is that everyone is on the same side: the best, most productive, and most happiness-inducing setup wins.

## What about culture?

Culture is not a foosball table, a party, or an outing; it is the pattern of values and actions around customers, quality, communication, workload, and risk. Strong culture reduces the need for explicit supervision because “managers-of-one” can make decisions consistent with shared expectations. Newcomers learn culture from how decisions are made and problems are repaired, not from the mission statement alone. Remote work may remove the illusion that social proximity creates culture and force the organization to define and practice its values more deliberately.

## I need an answer now!

Office habits train people to interrupt one another for every question, but urgency is not the same as importance. The authors propose a rough ladder: about 80% of questions can wait in email, 15% can use chat or instant messaging, and the final 5% can use the phone for real crises. Email creates a searchable record, while chat compresses near-term exchanges; neither works if the sender expects an answer within ten minutes. Breaking the ASAP addiction requires tolerating a delay while the brain relearns to match the medium to the actual need.

## But I’ll lose control

The fear of losing control is often emotional and instinctive, so better logic may not be enough to remove it. The authors recommend a gradual exposure: begin with one remote day, show the work and the extra progress, then expand to more days and flexible hours as the manager’s fear loses force. This is a confidence-building experiment, not a claim that every resistant boss will convert. If the fear remains immovable, the worker may need to find a different workplace rather than win an argument against a deeply held identity.

## We paid a lot of money for this office

The office rent is a sunk cost: the money is already gone whether the space is full or empty. The only relevant forward-looking question is where people can produce better work. The authors make the comparison concrete: five productive hours in the office versus six at home is 20% more productivity at home, regardless of the rent already paid. A building can still be useful, but its past price cannot make it the right work setting.

## That wouldn’t work for our size or industry

The authors list remote-friendly work across accounting, advertising, consulting, customer service, design, film, finance, government, hardware, insurance, legal, marketing, recruiting, and software. They cite large examples such as Aetna, Deloitte, and Intel, along with remote work in the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office, NASA, and the Environmental Protection Agency. Their company list spans organizations with more than 10,000 employees, 1,000–10,000 employees, and fewer than 1,000, including AT&T, DreamWorks, GitHub, Automattic, and FreeAgent. The conclusion is not that every job is remote, but that “industry” and “scale” should not be used as untested categorical excuses.

## Worked Example

The book’s 80/15/5 communication ladder turns an office interruption into a routing decision. A question that can wait for hours goes to email; a question needed within minutes goes to chat; only a genuine crisis merits a phone call. The system preserves a written record for most work, shortens the near-term exchange, and protects the phone from becoming another general-purpose interruption channel.

## Key Takeaways

1. Replace visibility with trust, explicit systems, and output evidence.
2. Reserve in-person time for work that can absorb its high coordination cost.
3. Match communication channel to urgency and do not transfer ASAP expectations into email.
4. Make security a consistent device-and-account practice, not an office superstition.
5. Treat culture as observable behavior and role-specific fairness as the real standard.
6. Use small experiments when resistance is emotional, while accepting that some environments will not change.
