# Chapter 4: Beware the Dragons

## Core Idea

Remote work solves some office problems while exposing a different set of risks: isolation, blurred boundaries, sedentary habits, weak pilots, client anxiety, and legal complexity. The authors’ response is not to return to the office but to design counterweights—social contact, explicit check-in and check-out habits, ergonomic and health support, a team-sized trial, visible client work, and professional legal advice. They distinguish working remotely from working at home, so a worker can use coworking spaces, cafés, libraries, or a rented desk when home is a poor fit. The chapter’s examples show that the risks are manageable when they are treated as operating requirements instead of surprises. It also makes clear that international employment and tax arrangements can create real liabilities and should not be improvised.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Check-in, check-out**: Define a healthy work boundary and assess whether the day was a good day’s work instead of measuring hours without end.
  - When to use: When flexible hours are turning into work from morning until bedtime.
  - How: Set reasonable expectations, ask whether the day was productive, and investigate a “no” rather than compensating with endless hours.
- **Meaningful pilot**: Test remote work with an entire team and enough time to adapt, not with a single isolated employee.
  - When to use: Before adopting remote work across an established organization.
  - How: Include project management and stakeholders, provide infrastructure, and let the group experience the trade-offs together.
- **Remote client trust sequence**: Disclose distance, provide references, show work often, stay available, and involve the client in a shared project space.
  - When to use: When clients worry that a remote supplier is hard to reach or may disappear.
  - How: Replace proximity reassurance with transparency, responsiveness, evidence, and participation.
- **Home is optional**: Treat remote work as freedom from a required office, not as a requirement to work in a spare bedroom.
  - When to use: When the home lacks space, social energy, or a productive environment.
  - How: Use cafés, coworking, sublets, or a small rented office according to the work and the worker.
- **Professional legal setup**: Treat tax nexus, labor classification, benefits, and currency as implementation constraints that require specialists.
  - When to use: When hiring across state or national borders.
  - How: Consult qualified legal and accounting professionals before scaling the arrangement.

## Key Concepts

- **Cabin fever**: The strain of insufficient live human interaction during remote work.
- **Check-in/check-out**: A deliberate boundary around starting and ending work.
- **Good day’s work**: A self-assessment of whether meaningful progress was made, rather than a raw hour count.
- **Five Whys**: Repeatedly asking why to search for the root cause of a disappointing result.
- **Ergonomics**: Designing equipment and posture to avoid long-term physical harm.
- **Single-team pilot**: A remote trial involving enough related people to test the actual workflow.
- **Client anxiety**: A customer’s natural concern after paying a remote supplier that cannot be visited easily.
- **Nexus**: A taxable presence that can create additional state tax obligations.
- **Contractor**: An independent worker who invoices through a recognized business arrangement rather than receiving local-employee benefits.

## Mental Models

Use **risk by design**: every remote risk should have a corresponding practice, such as social contact for isolation or a dedicated workstation for distraction. Use **group exposure**: a whole pilot team can judge trade-offs together, while a lone remote worker only experiences the downside. Use **trust through evidence** with clients: disclose, show, respond, and invite participation. Use **legal complexity as a scaling threshold**, not as a reason to ignore the opportunity or pretend the rules do not exist.

## Anti-patterns

- **Home-office confinement**: Assuming remote work means staying alone at home every day.
- **Always-on work**: Treating flexibility as permission to stretch the workday indefinitely.
- **Lone-outpost pilot**: Sending one employee away while the rest of the organization keeps office-only habits.
- **Remote invisibility to clients**: Waiting for clients to ask how communication and progress will work.
- **DIY cross-border compliance**: Treating tax and employment classification as a casual administrative detail.

## Cabin fever

Instant messages, email, and online chat can surround a worker with digital activity without providing enough live human contact. The authors argue that remote workers should deliberately use spouses, children, friends, neighbors, coworking spaces, and local activities as sources of social life rather than expecting coworkers to supply every need. Even an introvert is not designed for total solitude, and remote work can make that condition easier to drift into. Cabin fever is therefore a real but addressable risk: leave the home office, share space with others, or schedule ordinary community interaction.

## Check-in, check-out

Flexible hours remove the office’s forced stopping point, so remote workers can accidentally turn a normal day into a 7am–9pm stretch. The authors say the greater danger in a successful remote culture is often overwork, because passionate people can keep going without a manager seeing the burnout. 37signals expects an average forty-hour week and encourages people to ask whether they completed a “good day’s work”; an easy yes permits a satisfying stop, while a no can trigger a Five Whys investigation into what went wrong. The boundary is about sustainable progress, not punching a clock.

## Ergonomic basics

Long-term remote work requires a proper desk, chair, and screen rather than a permanent rotation through a couch and kitchen counter. The book cites Accenture’s “Ergonomics for Professionals” process, including selected equipment and access to an ergonomics expert, as evidence that the issue can be managed systematically. Home also makes personalization and variation easier: standing, stools, exercise balls, or alternating positions may fit a person better than a standardized office chair. The practical limit is that comfort and variety do not replace basic equipment; the body still needs support and movement.

## Mind the gut

Remote work can remove the small walking requirements built into commuting and office life, so a worker may take even fewer steps than a sedentary office worker. The authors cite Aetna’s finding that its remote-working population tended to be heavier and its use of online personal trainers as a response. 37signals offered a monthly health-club stipend and fresh produce deliveries, while the individual practices are simple: walk to lunch, exercise during a break, walk the dog, or cook healthy food with the time saved from commuting. The core distinction is that freedom from the commute creates time for health but does not create movement automatically.

## The lone outpost

A remote experiment fails predictably when one person is sent away and expected to work with an office-based system that was never redesigned. The isolated worker experiences every coordination cost alone, while office colleagues cannot share the benefits or learn the new habits. The authors recommend a meaningful pilot that includes a whole team, project management, and stakeholders, lasts long enough to get past the adjustment period, and has the required technology in place. American Fidelity Assurance used a natural-fit pilot group, then turned its members into advocates after productivity and morale improved.

## Working with clients

37signals’ earlier web-design consultancy worked for dozens of clients, including HP, Microsoft, and Getty, while meeting only a small handful in person. The case demonstrates that remote client work can support substantial fees when the relationship is designed around trust and visible progress. The supplier has to compensate for the client’s inability to visit by communicating more deliberately and showing the work itself. The client is not merely told to trust the remote team; the client is included in the work and can see the relationship progressing.

### What’s the secret?

There is no hidden trick; the authors reduce the method to a sequence of transparent behaviors. Disclose the remote arrangement before the contract, offer references without waiting to be asked, show work frequently, and remain unusually available through the channels the client uses. Give the client a shared space for schedules, work in progress, feedback, and tasks so that the client can follow along and contribute. These practices replace the reassurance of a local door with evidence, responsiveness, and participation.

## Taxes, accounting, laws, oh my!

Remote work is legal, but cross-border implementation can create material obligations. In the United States, an out-of-state employee may establish a taxable nexus and affect sales-tax responsibilities, while international hiring raises questions about local offices, contractor classification, benefits, invoices, and currency risk. The authors describe a local office as expensive and slow and contracting as a common starting bridge, but they also warn that every country has its own legal maze. The practical boundary is clear: enterprising companies do this, but owners and workers should consult qualified lawyers and accountants rather than treating the arrangement as informal.

## Worked Example

The chapter’s client sequence is a reusable trust-building workflow. A remote consultancy says where it is based before signing, supplies references without waiting to be asked, shows work early and often, remains unusually responsive, and creates an online space where the customer can review work, give feedback, and share responsibility. Each step replaces a missing form of local reassurance with evidence that the project is alive and accessible.

## Key Takeaways

1. Design social, physical, and time boundaries instead of expecting freedom to self-regulate.
2. Judge a remote pilot at the team level and give it enough time to reveal the real rhythm.
3. Treat the home as one remote location, not the definition of remote work.
4. Build client trust through early disclosure, references, visible progress, availability, and participation.
5. Take health and ergonomics seriously because the commute’s incidental movement disappears.
6. Escalate cross-border tax and classification questions to specialists before the arrangement grows.
