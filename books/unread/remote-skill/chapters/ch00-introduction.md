# Chapter 0: Introduction

## Core Idea

Remote argues that the enabling technology already exists, while the harder upgrade is changing human expectations about where and when good work happens. The authors define the opportunity in practical terms: fewer interruptions, more access to talent, freedom from destructive commutes, and greater control over life. They also make the limits explicit: remote work introduces challenges around trust, communication, culture, boundaries, and isolation. Their evidence comes mainly from 37signals, which grew from a Chicago–Copenhagen partnership into a thirty-six-person company distributed across the world. The book is therefore meant to be a working playbook for employers and workers, not a prediction detached from practice.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Work over location**: Judge the result and the conditions that produce it, rather than treating office presence as the work itself.
- **Asynchronous collaboration**: Use written, recorded, and shared artifacts so people can contribute without being online at the same moment.
- **Remote as a system**: Combine tools, expectations, hiring, management, and personal routines; changing location alone is insufficient.

## Key Concepts

- **Interruption factory**: An office environment that fragments meaningful work into short, low-quality intervals.
- **Distributed workforce**: A team whose members work from different places, and often different schedules.
- **Human upgrade**: The change in habits and assumptions required to use remote-work technology effectively.
- **Remote work**: Work performed outside a required office, not necessarily work performed at home.
- **Trade-off**: A benefit that brings a corresponding cost or management problem.

## Mental Models

Use **work over place** when evaluating a policy: ask what produces a better result and a better life, not which location looks familiar. Use **remote as a system** when planning adoption: tools, hiring, communication, management, and boundaries must reinforce one another. Use **practice before theory** when assessing a claim: look for the operating evidence behind it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Technology-only adoption**: Buying collaboration tools while preserving office-era expectations about instant answers and visible attendance.
- **Remote-as-home-only**: Treating remote work as a single location instead of a choice among home, cafés, libraries, coworking spaces, and offices.
- **Future-only thinking**: Assuming the opportunity belongs to a later generation when the necessary capabilities already exist.

## Worked Example

The authors’ company began with one founder in Chicago and one in Copenhagen, then expanded to a globally distributed team serving customers around the world. That experience is used as the book’s practical laboratory: the same system has to support focused work, collaboration across time zones, client relationships, hiring, management, and personal life.

## Key Takeaways

1. Remote work is primarily an organizational and cultural change, not a hardware purchase.
2. Start from the work that needs to be done and design the location and schedule around it.
3. Expect trade-offs and build practices that address them directly.
