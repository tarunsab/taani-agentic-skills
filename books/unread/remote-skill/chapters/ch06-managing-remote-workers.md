# Chapter 6: Managing Remote Workers

## Core Idea

Managing remote workers means replacing attendance supervision with knowledge of the work, clear access, timely conversations, and removal of unnecessary obstacles. The authors recommend starting remote work early when possible, or starting small with trusted existing employees when a company is already established. Managers should lead and verify work, not manage chairs; that requires understanding the task well enough to divide it, anticipate delays, and help solve problems. Remote teams need in-person meetups and occasional sprints, but those should be purposeful and scarce, while open-source projects demonstrate that global asynchronous collaboration can scale. The manager’s most important duty is sustainable performance: level the field for remote workers, check in conversationally, remove permissions bottlenecks, watch for overwork, and use rare face time well.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Start early; if not, start small**: Adopt remote habits before office culture hardens, or run a low-risk pilot with trusted current employees.
  - When to use: When deciding whether to launch remote work in a new or established company.
  - How: Offer a few remote days, test different roles, iterate, and let evidence shape the policy.
- **Stop managing the chairs**: Replace time-and-place supervision with work leadership and verification.
  - When to use: When managers measure arrival, departure, or desk visibility.
  - How: Know the work, break it into manageable pieces, assign well, resolve delays, and inspect output.
- **Level the playing field**: Make office and remote workers equal participants in communication, decisions, tools, and status.
  - When to use: When office employees have ambient access that remote employees lack.
  - How: Improve shared audio/video, keep discussions online, and have leaders experience the remote side.
- **One-on-ones**: Hold casual recurring conversations that surface small morale and workflow issues before formal reviews.
  - When to use: When remote workers have fewer incidental conversations with managers.
  - How: Talk every month if possible or at least every few months; keep twenty to thirty minutes with an hour available.
- **Remove the roadblocks**: Give people default access and decision authority so work can proceed across time zones.
  - When to use: When a person must wait for one manager, deployer, or approver.
  - How: Empower reasonable decisions, accept good-faith mistakes, and replace permission rituals with useful work.
- **Sustainable hours**: Watch for overwork as the primary danger in a successful remote team.
  - When to use: When flexible schedules and passion are turning work into an always-on hobby.
  - How: Set an average around forty hours, model limits, encourage breaks and hobbies, and intervene early.
- **Scarcity increases value**: Reduce routine face time so the remaining in-person time receives attention and is used wisely.
  - When to use: When meetings and face-to-face discussion have become abundant and wasteful.
  - How: Move routine communication online and reserve gatherings for complex or social work.

## Key Concepts

- **Chair management**: Supervision based on where and when employees sit rather than on results.
- **Work leadership**: Understanding tasks, delays, dependencies, and people well enough to help progress.
- **Meetup**: A periodic whole-team gathering to connect faces, share work, and set direction.
- **Sprint**: A short intense period in which a smaller group works together to finish a specific project.
- **Intrinsic motivation**: Engagement arising from interesting problems and meaningful work rather than external pressure.
- **Level playing field**: Equal ability for office and remote workers to hear, contribute, decide, and be recognized.
- **One-on-one**: A casual recurring manager–worker conversation about how things are going.
- **Roadblock**: A permission, access, dependency, or fear that prevents independent progress.
- **Overwork**: Unsustainable hours or constant availability that increases burnout risk.
- **Scarcity principle**: A limited resource is more likely to be conserved, appreciated, and used well.

## Mental Models

Use **management as leverage**: the manager’s value is in making good work easier, not in filling the calendar with status activity. Use **open source as a proof of coordination**: motivation, public work, and occasional meetings can support large distributed systems. Use **the remote-worker shoe test**: leaders should experience the communication friction they ask others to tolerate. Use **scarcity as a design tool**: less routine access can increase the quality of the access that remains.

## Anti-patterns

- **Chair management**: Rewarding early arrival and late departure while ignoring output.
- **Lone-manager advantage**: Letting office managers make decisions in hallway conversations that remote workers cannot hear.
- **Manager-as-roadblock**: Requiring permission for spending, access, deployment, or vacation when responsibility could be delegated.
- **Annual-review blindness**: Waiting for a formal yearly review to discover morale or workflow problems.
- **Hero-hours culture**: Treating nights, weekends, or constant availability as evidence of commitment.
- **Abundant face time**: Holding frequent in-person meetings until they become routine, tired, and expensive.

## When’s the right time to go remote?

The easiest time to build a remote culture is early, before office habits become the company’s default language. The authors compare a young company to children who grow up with computers and an established company to parents introduced to them late: both can learn, but the transition costs differ. An established company should start with trusted employees working remotely a couple of days a week, test different roles, and iterate rather than waiting for a perfect policy. Small experiments make the change low risk while exposing which work and team practices need adjustment.

## Stop managing the chairs

Visible arrival and departure times are weak measures of productivity, and remote work exposes the weakness of a manager whose job is merely to watch chairs. The actual managerial role is to understand what needs doing, why delays happen, how to solve sticky problems, how to divide work, and who is suited to each project. A programming manager need not write every line, but technical understanding makes that leadership more credible and effective. Location has little bearing on whether copy is good, code is correct, or design fits the need.

## Meetups and sprints

Remote work does not eliminate the value of getting together; it changes the reason for doing so. 37signals met at least twice a year for four or five days to discuss projects and direction and, more importantly, connect screen names with real faces and personalities. FreeAgent used the Edinburgh arts festival, while Fotolia brought a globally distributed team to Marrakech, illustrating that a meetup can be social as well as operational. Smaller sprints, product launches, and industry conferences can provide concentrated project work and team bonding without turning every week into a travel schedule.

## Lessons from open source

Open-source projects demonstrate that thousands of people can build complex software through asynchronous communication, public repositories, intrinsic motivation, and occasional meetings. The authors point to Linux, MySQL, PHP, and Ruby on Rails as systems that exceed the complexity of ordinary business software, and note that nearly 3,000 contributors from many places helped evolve Rails without most meeting one another. The mechanism is not magic: interesting problems reduce the need for supervision, information is public so knowledgeable people can self-select, and conferences provide optional social contact. A company facing a smaller coordination problem can learn from this rather than assuming geographic dispersion makes complexity impossible.

## Level the playing field

Remote workers become second-class citizens when office colleagues make decisions in rooms they cannot enter or use inadequate audio that prevents participation. The authors recommend putting discussions online, sharing the same screens, improving the intercom, and having senior leaders work remotely so the people with power feel the friction themselves. Herman Miller’s design head Betty Hase is offered as an example of a remote leader overseeing a distributed team. Equality is not a slogan; it is an infrastructure and behavior test: can the remote worker hear, contribute, decide, and be considered without asking for special rescue?

## One-on-ones

Remote workers have fewer accidental conversations, so managers should create a recurring channel that is casual rather than an extra performance review. 37signals tried to speak with every remote person at least every few months, with a twenty- or thirty-minute conversation and an hour kept open if the discussion needed room. The purpose is to surface concerns before they accumulate; annual reviews are too broad and too infrequent for small morale or workflow problems. A simple “what’s up, how are things?” conversation can reveal more than a formal checklist.

## Remove the roadblocks

Remote work requires progress at all hours, so waiting for the only person who can deploy, authorize a refund, or grant access becomes a direct productivity loss. The authors recommend empowering people to make decisions, accepting good-faith mistakes as the price of self-sufficiency, and giving default access to the information and applications needed for the job. 37signals gave everyone a company credit card with an instruction to spend wisely, removed vacation permission rituals, and asked people to put time off on the calendar and coordinate. Managers can enjoy being gatekeepers, but permission power is not the same as value creation.

## Be on the lookout for overwork, not underwork

The successful remote team’s main risk is often not laziness but the absence of a clear stopping point. Multiple time zones keep someone online, and home keeps files and devices within reach after a normal day, so work can expand into evenings and weekends. The authors say managers and owners must model sustainable behavior, target roughly forty hours a week on average, and watch for burnout even when the worker loves the job. 37signals used extra summer days off, hobby support, and tailored vacations to counter the tendency toward overwork. Long-term performance comes from people who work enough to progress and enough less to remain healthy.

## Using scarcity to your advantage

Face-to-face conversation is useful for complicated matters, but abundance turns it into routine, tired, and wasteful interaction. Remote work makes face time scarce, which can cause people to look forward to gatherings and use them more productively. 37signals’ occasional week together worked because it was unusual; the authors predict that constant access would make the same time wildly wasteful. Moving ordinary conversations online can therefore increase the value of the rare in-person exchange rather than reducing communication quality overall.

## Worked Example

The recommended adoption sequence for an established company is deliberately incremental. Offer a few trusted employees two remote days a week, try the arrangement across different roles, and observe the work long enough to get past initial withdrawal from office habits. Keep the team connected through open tools, periodic one-on-ones, and a shared trial rather than isolating one person. If output and morale improve, increase remote time; if a constraint appears, adjust the workflow instead of declaring remote work impossible.

## Key Takeaways

1. Build remote habits early or begin with a meaningful, low-risk pilot.
2. Manage the work: understand it, divide it, staff it, and remove delays.
3. Make remote workers equal in access, voice, status, and decision participation.
4. Use meetups and sprints for real social or project value, not routine presence.
5. Empower reasonable decisions and make needed information available by default.
6. Watch for overwork and model sustainable hours.
7. Let scarce face time become more valuable by moving routine communication online.
