# Chapter 0: Authors’ Note

## Core Idea

The authors frame Remote as a practical response to a change that was already underway, rather than as a speculative manifesto. They point to remote work’s growth in the United States from 2005 to 2011 and to Yahoo!’s decision in February 2013 to dismantle its remote-work program as evidence that the subject had moved into public controversy. The Yahoo! episode became a useful test case because its arguments reproduced nearly every objection the book examines. The authors’ claim is not that remote work is effortless or universally appropriate, but that it deserves an in-the-trenches evaluation of benefits, trade-offs, tools, and operating practices.

## Key Concepts

- **Remote-work debate**: A practical question about how work is organized, not merely a technology trend.
- **Trade-off**: The benefits of remote work come with costs and constraints that must be managed.
- **Practice-based knowledge**: The book’s guidance comes from 37signals’ experience, not from remote-work theory alone.

## Mental Models

Think of a public controversy as a stress test for an operating model: use the objections raised in a prominent case to inspect whether the model has real answers.

## Anti-patterns

- **Sound-bite reasoning**: Treating a dramatic corporate decision as proof that remote work succeeds or fails everywhere.
- **Benefit-only advocacy**: Ignoring the losses of proximity, structure, and easy social contact.

## Worked Example

The authors use Yahoo!’s 2013 policy reversal as a backdrop. The reversal did not settle the question; it supplied a live catalogue of objections—trust, culture, communication, and control—that the book answers with examples from 37signals and other organizations.

## Key Takeaways

1. Evaluate remote work through operating evidence, not headlines.
2. Preserve both the upside and the compromises in the analysis.
3. Treat the book as a field guide grounded in 37signals’ distributed experience.
