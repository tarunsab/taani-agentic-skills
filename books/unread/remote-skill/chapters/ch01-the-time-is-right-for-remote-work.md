# Chapter 1: The Time Is Right for Remote Work

## Core Idea

The chapter’s central claim is that the traditional office is often the least suitable place for meaningful work because it fragments attention and imposes a commute and schedule that do not match how people think. Remote work lets people control interruptions, choose productive hours, and draw talent from beyond expensive geographic hubs. The authors connect this freedom to a broader change in what counts as luxury: time, location, and the ability to pursue a life while still working. They do not present remote work as costless or universally applicable; lost in-person contact, weak boundaries, and family interruptions are real constraints. The practical conclusion is to treat remote as a flexible operating choice—sometimes full-time, sometimes hybrid, and sometimes supported by an office that is useful but not required.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Interruption factory**: Treat an office whose day is shredded by meetings and drop-ins as a productivity problem, not as a neutral default.
  - When to use: When creative, thoughtful, or important work cannot find sustained focus time.
  - How: Identify which interruptions are imposed and replace them with a controllable work setting.
- **Asynchronous collaboration**: Separate collaboration from the requirement that everyone work at the same time.
  - When to use: When teammates work across time zones or need flexible family and energy schedules.
  - How: Keep a reasonable shared workweek, preserve necessary overlap, and let the remaining hours vary.
- **The new luxury**: Evaluate freedom and time as benefits that can be enjoyed during working life rather than deferred until retirement.
  - When to use: When location, commute, or office hours prevent people from living near family or pursuing meaningful interests.
  - How: Use remote and hybrid options to blend work with the life people actually want.
- **Not all or nothing**: Choose the smallest remote arrangement that improves the work without assuming that every person, day, or role must follow one policy.
  - When to use: When an office still serves a purpose or some tasks require physical presence.
  - How: Mix remote workers, office workers, hybrid schedules, and occasional gatherings according to the work.

## Key Concepts

- **Interruption factory**: A workplace that breaks the day into fragments too short for deep work.
- **Asynchronous work**: Collaboration that does not require the same people to be active simultaneously.
- **Talent hub**: A geographic center that attracts a concentrated industry but is not the only source of strong talent.
- **Deferred living**: Postponing desired places, relationships, and activities until retirement or a future windfall.
- **Golden handcuffs**: Work incentives that keep someone in a life they do not want.
- **Remote-first freedom**: The ability to choose where and when work happens without making the office the prerequisite.
- **Sunk cost**: Money already spent that should not determine whether a future option is productive.
- **Quality of life**: The worker-side value of time, flexibility, family access, and meaningful work.

## Mental Models

Think of **focus as a scarce resource**: the office can consume it through interruptions even when everyone is technically working. Think of **time zones as an opportunity for flexible coverage**, not merely as a scheduling nuisance. Think of **geography as a market boundary**: remote hiring expands the talent pool and can reduce dependence on poaching within a single hub. Think of **the office as a luxury or tool**—worth keeping when it creates value, but not as proof that work is happening.

## Anti-patterns

- **Presence equals productivity**: Counting attendance, late hours, or desk time instead of meaningful output.
- **9-to-5 absolutism**: Forcing creative work into a fixed clock when the person’s best thinking happens earlier, later, or in a different rhythm.
- **Hub nationalism**: Assuming the best talent, ideas, or culture can only come from a famous city.
- **Remote equals outsourcing**: Reducing a quality-of-life and talent-access strategy to lower labor cost.
- **Sunk-office policy**: Keeping people in an unproductive office because rent has already been paid.
- **Binary policy**: Making the entire organization choose between an office-only model and a fully remote model.

## Why work doesn’t happen at work

The authors describe the busy office as an “interruption factory”: meetings, calls, and casual drop-ins chop a workday into pieces that are too small for meaningful concentration. Creative and thoughtful work needs an uninterrupted stretch long enough to enter a productive zone, which is why people often come early, stay late, or work on weekends when they need to finish something important. Remote work can restore that zone because the worker controls the setting, can move to a better place, and can use headphones or other boundaries. Home, a café, or a library can still contain distractions, but those distractions are more passive and more controllable than an institutional interruption.

## Stop commuting your life away

The commute consumes time, energy, patience, and parts of family and personal life before the workday even begins. The book cites research associating commuting with stress, poor sleep, obesity, pain, high blood pressure, depression, and relationship strain, while also pointing to environmental costs. Its simple arithmetic is more actionable than the rhetoric: a 1.5-hour daily commute can reach roughly 300–400 hours a year, comparable to the time 37signals spent building Basecamp. Those recovered hours can become work, exercise, family time, or rest. The authors therefore treat commuting as a business cost as well as a personal inconvenience.

## It’s the technology, stupid

Earlier generations could not easily collaborate across cities or continents because the necessary communication and file-sharing tools did not exist. Internet services such as screen sharing, shared task lists, instant messaging, and cloud file access changed remote work from an impractical ambition into a normal possibility. The remaining obstacle is often psychological: people trained in office-and-clock assumptions have to release nostalgia for the old arrangement. The tools are not described as difficult to learn, but using them well requires willingness to change habits and expectations.

## Escaping 9am–5pm

Distributed teams often move from synchronous collaboration toward asynchronous collaboration, which allows people to coordinate without sharing every working hour. At 37signals, a roughly forty-hour week is retained while the distribution of those hours is flexible enough for early birds, night owls, and parents. The IT Collective’s film editors are offered as a concrete example: they sometimes work nocturnally, overlap with the rest of the team to review progress, and then return to their preferred schedule. Not every function can be entirely schedule-free—customer support still needs coverage—but the constraint should apply to the function that needs it rather than to everyone by default.

## End of city monopoly

Cities became talent hubs through industrial concentration, but the same population density also produced expensive housing, crowded transport, and office routines. As digital access to culture, entertainment, and information improves, the authors argue that work no longer has to purchase the city’s advantages as a package. They predict that a new privilege will be the ability to leave the city for a place chosen for quality of life, family, or personal interests. This is a prediction from the book’s 2013 perspective, not a universal rule: cities still offer culture and social density, and many people will choose to remain in them. The operative idea is that location becomes a choice rather than a monopoly.

## The new luxury

The authors contrast old corporate perks—corner offices, cars, meals, and on-site services—with the more valuable freedom to live near the people and activities one cares about. Remote work can let someone ski, surf, stay close to family, or spend more time with children while remaining employed. This rejects deferred living: work and retirement do not have to be separate life phases if the work arrangement can flex around real priorities. The option need not be a permanent move; a few weeks elsewhere or a hybrid schedule can capture some of the benefit. Freedom and time are the durable luxuries, while material perks can become substitutes for the life the worker keeps postponing.

## Talent isn’t bound by the hubs

Industry hubs often defend their status by claiming that their geography creates the necessary magic, but the authors point out that strong talent exists elsewhere. 37signals hired excellent people from places such as Caldwell, Idaho, and Fenwick, Ontario, and had no employee in San Francisco despite competing in software. Hiring outside a dense hub can also reduce the constant poaching that happens when many employers are within walking distance of one another. People who are not immersed in the local echo chamber may spend less time comparing employers and may be happier with the work they have. The principle is to search for ability and fit globally rather than treating a famous city as a proxy for quality.

## It’s not about the money

The book distinguishes remote work from outsourcing: the primary reasons are quality of life, access to capable people, and better work, not simply lower wages. For knowledge work such as writing, programming, design, advising, and customer support, improving quality can matter more than squeezing out a little more hourly output. A cheaper location is not automatically a better source of work, and a remote worker should not be treated as a discount substitute. Cost savings may follow, but the authors frame them as a side benefit of a better arrangement.

## But saving is always nice

Although money is not the central argument, the authors recommend using financial savings as a persuasive entry point with managers focused on budgets. IBM’s cited telework figures describe a large reduction in office space, billions in proceeds and sublease income, and continuing annual savings; the book also cites commuting and fuel savings for workers and reduced emissions. This creates a “Trojan horse” argument: a manager may approve remote work for cost reasons while workers receive more time and freedom as the deeper benefit. The tactic works only when the resulting arrangement also protects quality and sustainable work; savings achieved by degrading the job would miss the book’s main point.

## Not all or nothing

Remote work can coexist with an office, local employees, occasional gatherings, and roles that must be physically present. Herman Miller’s knowledge and design team is cited as fully remote across ten cities, Jellyvision as a mix of fully remote, part-time remote, and headquarters workers, and 37signals as a company that kept an office after remote work had already become normal. The office can be useful for presentations, clients, and company-wide meetings while remaining a luxury rather than a prerequisite. A policy should therefore match the work and the company’s stage instead of treating uniformity as fairness.

## Still a trade-off

Remote work removes some daily interruptions and commuting costs, but it also removes easy in-person contact and the structure that the office imposes automatically. Workers may miss brainstorming in the same room, procrastinate without a routine, or find family interruptions difficult to contain. The authors do not resolve these costs by declaring them good or bad; they recommend harvesting the benefits while building countermeasures for the drawbacks. This trade-off frame is important because remote work is not a magic escape from the need for judgment, boundaries, or social connection.

## You’re probably already doing it

Many companies already trust remote outsiders—lawyers, accountants, payroll providers, ad agencies, and other specialists—to perform critical work away from their walls and direct supervision. The authors ask why those same companies distrust employees who work from home, especially when office workers often spend the day emailing or messaging colleagues who sit nearby. The comparison exposes a double standard: the organization already relies on remote execution when it labels the worker an “outside” firm. Auditing the work that is already performed remotely can reveal that the company is more distributed than its office policy admits.

## Worked Example

The chapter combines two practical calculations. A 1.5-hour daily commute can consume 300–400 hours each year, while 37signals reports that building Basecamp took about 400 programmer hours; the comparison turns commute time into a visible business resource. Separately, 37signals’ office history shows the “not all or nothing” principle: it moved from a traditional Chicago office to rented desks, then later kept a designed Chicago office for gatherings and presentations even though daily office attendance was unnecessary.

## Key Takeaways

1. Protect uninterrupted focus by making the work setting controllable.
2. Design schedules around energy, time zones, and customer coverage—not nostalgia for 9am–5pm.
3. Search beyond talent hubs and judge people by capability rather than geography.
4. Use cost savings to open the door, but optimize for work quality and life quality.
5. Treat an office as one tool among several; keep it when it adds value.
6. Plan explicitly for lost proximity, weaker structure, and family or home distractions.
