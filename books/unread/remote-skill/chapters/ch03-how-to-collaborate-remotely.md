# Chapter 3: How to Collaborate Remotely

## Core Idea

Remote collaboration works when the team replaces accidental office coordination with deliberate overlap, visibility, and social connection. A small amount of synchronous time can prevent slow handoffs, while asynchronous artifacts let people make progress when others are offline. Shared screens and screencasts make the work visible; centralized files, discussions, calendars, and code prevent knowledge from being trapped in one inbox or computer. Progress updates and output-based evaluation replace the ambient awareness of an office with evidence that everyone can inspect. The chapter also treats remote work as a capability that should be practiced locally and used for resilience, while warning that meetings and managers become harmful when they are applied by default.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Four-hour overlap**: Preserve a meaningful daily intersection for real-time collaboration, even when the rest of the schedule is flexible.
  - When to use: When teammates work across time zones and full-day synchronous work is impossible.
  - How: Shift schedules to create overlap; accept asynchronous work without overlap only when exceptional talent justifies the cost.
- **Seeing is believing**: Share the object of work, not merely people’s faces.
  - When to use: When words alone make a design, presentation, or feature difficult to discuss.
  - How: Use screen sharing for live collaboration and lightweight screencasts for delayed review.
- **All out in the open**: Make files, instructions, decisions, schedules, and code accessible by default.
  - When to use: When an absent coworker would otherwise lose a half-day waiting for context.
  - How: Use a shared project home, repository, calendar, and searchable discussion trail.
- **Progress as a shared ritual**: Make forward motion visible to peers without turning the update into a formal estimation exercise.
  - When to use: When remote workers lack the ambient sense of what the company is doing.
  - How: Ask what people completed and what they intend next, then let the visible commitment reinforce progress.
- **Remote as disaster readiness**: Treat distributed capability as a backup for office, weather, health, and personal disruptions.
  - When to use: When an office failure could make the whole organization unavailable.
  - How: Practice remote work before the emergency and keep customers insulated from the disruption.

## Key Concepts

- **Overlap**: Shared working time used for interaction that benefits from immediacy.
- **Screencast**: A recording of a screen with narration that can be reviewed asynchronously.
- **Open-by-default information**: Project material accessible to everyone who needs to work with it.
- **Virtual water cooler**: A persistent group chat used primarily for social cohesion and informal exchange.
- **Progress thread**: A recurring written update about completed and intended work.
- **Output yardstick**: Evaluation based on produced work rather than presence.
- **Local remote**: Working outside the office while remaining in the same city or nearby.
- **Single Point of Failure (SPoF)**: A dependency whose failure stops the system from functioning.
- **M&Ms**: The authors’ shorthand for meetings and managers when they are over-applied.

## Mental Models

Think of **overlap as a bridge, not a full schedule**: enough shared time prevents delay while preserving protected focus time. Think of **the project space as the office**: if the answer exists only in someone’s head, desk, or inbox, the team is still location-dependent. Think of **progress as a social signal**: a visible commitment creates peer accountability without surveillance. Think of **remote practice as a backup system**: the organization becomes less vulnerable when the office fails.

## Anti-patterns

- **Zero-overlap outsourcing**: Accepting a full day of communication delay as the normal price of remote collaboration.
- **Face-only conferencing**: Using video to simulate presence while leaving the actual work invisible.
- **Private inbox workflow**: Storing project decisions, files, and instructions in individual accounts.
- **The lone remote employee**: Sending one person away while everyone else continues operating as if the office were the only system.
- **Meeting and manager saturation**: Using synchronous gatherings and status demands as default work containers.

## Thou shalt overlap

37signals found that roughly four hours of overlap was enough to avoid the delay and isolation associated with outsourced work. To create it across Chicago and Copenhagen, Chicago worked about 8am–5pm while Copenhagen shifted to roughly 11am–7pm local time, leaving a useful intersection despite a seven-hour difference. The remaining hours could be used for uninterrupted focus or personal needs, which often increased productivity. A much larger gap, such as Los Angeles and Shanghai, is possible but generally not worth the friction unless the person brings rare talent; saving labor cost alone is not a good reason.

## Seeing is believing

Remote collaboration does not have to describe a screen verbally while everyone imagines a different object. Screen-sharing tools let people inspect a presentation, website change, Photoshop sketch, or text document together, and they preserve the useful part of sitting beside the same computer. This is distinct from video conferencing: the primary subject is the work, not the participants’ faces. For asynchronous review, a narrated screencast can demonstrate a feature or explain sales and marketing work without requiring everyone to meet. The authors advise letting the recording be good enough rather than turning it into a polished production that consumes the time it was meant to save.

## All out in the open

Remote workers lose time when the next task, required file, or decision is locked inside a coworker’s computer or inbox. 37signals used Basecamp as a shared home for discussions, tasks, calendars, files, and decisions, paired it with GitHub for code and review, and kept a shared calendar for availability. The specific tools can vary, but the underlying requirement is stable: everyone should be able to find the materials and instructions needed to continue without waiting for a person in another time zone. Open information is both a coordination system and a cultural habit.

## The virtual water cooler

Productivity is not improved by turning every remote worker into an eight-hour machine. 37signals’ Campfire chat provides a persistent room for jokes, news, lunch plans, live events, and informal questions, allowing people to enter and leave at natural breaks rather than demanding constant attention. Its primary function is social cohesion, even though work questions can also be answered there. The value is a controlled, shared form of “quality waste”: coworkers retain human connection without recreating every office interruption.

## Forward motion

An office creates a feeling of continuous information flow through hallway conversations, coffee breaks, and lunch, but remote teams need to create that feeling deliberately. 37signals uses a weekly thread titled “What have you been working on?” in which people report recent work and intended next steps. It is not a precise estimation or coordination system; it simply makes progress and commitment visible across the company. Public updates also make exaggerated accounts of easy tasks harder to sustain because peers with relevant knowledge can see the work and its pace.

## The work is what matters

When a manager cannot observe someone’s desk, presence metrics lose their force: arrival time, breaks, browser tabs, and visible busyness cannot serve as the main evidence. The remaining question is more direct—what did the person actually produce? Evaluating the work itself clarifies who is contributing and removes petty judgments that have little relationship to results. The standard can be demanding, but it is tied to the thing the organization is paying for rather than to a ritual of being seen.

## Not just for people who are out of town

Remote means not being in the office all day, not necessarily being far away. 37signals used local remote work from homes, cafés, libraries, and coworking spaces, with only a subset of the Chicago team physically present on most days. The authors recommend a meaningful trial of at least three months, beginning with perhaps two remote days and three office days before reversing the ratio. This low-risk conditioning period lets a team learn its remote habits before hiring its first distant employee.

## Disaster ready

An office can be an organizational Single Point of Failure: power, Internet, air conditioning, severe weather, flu, childcare, illness, or a home repair can make the usual workplace unavailable. American Fidelity Assurance kept serving customers when its Oklahoma City headquarters closed for bad weather, and it asked non-remote employees to work remotely periodically so they would be ready in an emergency. Remote capability also helps with ordinary personal disruptions that would otherwise cancel a workday. The result is not merely flexibility but continuity—the organization can keep moving while the office is unavailable.

## Easy on the M&Ms

Meetings and managers are not inherently bad, but both become destructive when they are the default way to discuss, monitor, and organize work. A one-hour meeting with five other people is a six-hour productivity cost, and status demands can interrupt the work they are supposed to measure. Remote tools leave a paper trail, so unnecessary intervention becomes easier to see and often more purposeful and compressed. The chapter’s warning is to use meetings and management as carefully as salt: enough to improve the dish, not enough to ruin it.

## Worked Example

The Chicago–Copenhagen schedule shows how a distributed team can preserve four shared hours without forcing everyone into a universal 9am–5pm day. Chicago’s 8am start overlaps with Copenhagen’s 11am start, while each side retains non-overlap time for focused work. The local-remote experiment supplies the adoption sequence: two days remote and three days in office, then three days remote and two in office, over a trial long enough to reveal the new rhythm.

## Key Takeaways

1. Design for a useful overlap window, not for total synchronous presence.
2. Put the work on shared screens and in shared repositories.
3. Make social connection and progress visible without demanding constant attention.
4. Judge output directly and let local remote practice prepare the organization.
5. Build remote capability before disasters reveal that the office was a Single Point of Failure.
6. Count the full cost of every meeting and intervention before scheduling it.
