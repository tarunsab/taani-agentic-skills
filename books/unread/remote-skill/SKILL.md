## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**Work over place and presence.** Use this when an organization is treating attendance, desk visibility, or a fixed schedule as proof that work is happening. The authors argue that offices often fragment focus through meetings and interruptions, while remote settings let people control those distractions. Judge what was produced and how the arrangement supports concentration, not when someone arrived or whether a manager saw a browser window. This does not mean every task belongs at home: physical requirements, customer coverage, and collaboration needs still shape the right location.

**Remote is not all or nothing.** Use a hybrid or role-specific pattern when an office still adds value or a particular job needs physical access. The book’s examples range from fully remote teams to mixes of remote, part-time remote, and headquarters workers, and 37signals kept an office as a useful luxury rather than a necessity. A day can also be split between quiet remote work and collaborative office time. The decision rule is to fit the work instead of making uniformity stand in for fairness.

**Asynchronous work with useful overlap.** Use asynchronous collaboration to free people from a universal 9am–5pm schedule, but preserve enough shared time for timely interaction. 37signals found roughly four hours of overlap workable across Chicago and Copenhagen by shifting local hours. The non-overlap time becomes protected focus time or family time, while extreme gaps are accepted only when rare talent justifies the friction. A flexible schedule is not the same as a full-day communication delay.

**All out in the open.** Use shared project homes, calendars, repositories, documents, and progress threads when a remote worker would otherwise wait for a file, instruction, or decision. The authors’ system used Basecamp, GitHub, shared calendars, and persistent chat to make work findable. Shared artifacts replace the ambient information flow of an office and leave a record that can be inspected later. The mechanism is cultural as well as technical: important work should not live only in one person’s inbox or computer.

**Trust adults and judge the work.** Use this when a manager wants surveillance because unseen workers might slack off. Office presence does not prevent distraction, and low expectations can invite people to behave as if they are untrusted. Hire capable people, inspect results, address poor performance, and change the hiring decision when trust or self-management is absent. Remote work is not a license for weak accountability; it is a reason to make accountability evidence-based.

**Route communication by urgency.** Use the 80/15/5 ladder to protect focus: most questions can wait in email, a smaller portion needs concise chat, and only true crises need the phone. Do not transfer office interruption habits into a demand that every email receive an immediate answer. The written record is a feature, not a consolation prize, because it preserves decisions and reduces repeated questions. The ladder works only when senders accept that different channels have different response times.

**Culture is behavior; managers are facilitators.** Use “managers-of-one” and the No Broken Windows norm when loosening supervision. Culture is expressed in customer treatment, quality, workload, communication, risk, and how small problems are repaired—not in foosball tables or mission statements. Managers should understand the work, remove roadblocks, provide access, and hold casual one-on-ones rather than manage chairs. Small snippy behaviors matter because written distance can let bad tone spread before anyone sees its effect.

**Hire through evidence and retain proven people.** Search globally when local geography is a constraint, but check overlap, language, and compliance. Filter writing early, ask candidates to show real work, use a paid test project, and meet finalists for character and team fit. Look for both “Smart” and “Gets Things Done,” not puzzle performance or office charm. When a trusted employee moves, changing the location requirement is usually cheaper and more valuable than discarding knowledge.

**Treat adoption as a designed experiment.** Start remote work early if possible; if the company is established, start small with a meaningful team rather than a lone outpost. Level the playing field by making leaders experience the remote side, and run the pilot long enough to learn the new habits. Use meetups and sprints as purposeful, scarce resources, not as a return to constant presence. A failed implementation is evidence about missing systems or boundaries, not automatically evidence against remote work.

**Protect sustainable human performance.** Use routines, separate work and leisure devices, movement, social contact, and explicit stopping points to counter the risks of freedom. The authors warn that overwork is often more likely than underwork when passionate people can work across time zones and from home. Treat a motivation slump as a barometer of weakly defined work, blockers, burnout, or a corrosive environment before blaming character. Remote freedom is valuable because it supports family, health, travel, and variety; it is not valuable if work consumes the life it was meant to improve.

**Use scarcity to improve face time.** Reserve in-person meetings for work that benefits from high-fidelity interaction, complex discussion, or social connection. Frequent meetings create backlog, distraction, and a large opportunity cost, while occasional gatherings become more valuable when people cannot take them for granted. A screen share or screencast often captures the useful object of collaboration without requiring a room. The aim is not to eliminate people or meetings, but to make each one earn its cost.
