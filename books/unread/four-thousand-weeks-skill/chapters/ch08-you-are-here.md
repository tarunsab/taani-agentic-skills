# Chapter 8: You Are Here

## Core Idea

Instrumentalizing time means treating the present as a tool for reaching a better future, and it can make every day feel like an obstacle to get through. The “when-I-finally” mind postpones fulfillment until a workload, relationship, skill, social condition, or life stage is finally in order, but the imagined endpoint simply generates a new endpoint. Burkeman argues that even attempts to be intensely present can repeat the same mistake when they turn present experience into a performance to optimize. A newborn, a walk, a meal, or a conversation has value as it unfolds, not only as evidence of a future result. The “causal catastrophe” is especially clear in parenting when childhood is evaluated only by the adults it produces. Because every moment arrives once and will not return, present attention is not a rival to long-term work; it is the only place that work and life actually occur.

## The Causal Catastrophe

Parenting advice divided Burkeman into “Baby Trainers” and “Natural Parents,” yet both camps often justified current practices by the future psychological or economic quality of the adult child. He calls this the causal catastrophe: judging a way of bringing up children by the kind of adult it produces, thereby making childhood merely a training ground. Present experience still matters even when a habit affects the future, and it must be weighed rather than automatically sacrificed. The same reasoning applies to any work whose value cannot be proven by a visible final result. Tom Stoppard’s image of a child’s purpose being to be a child, and not merely to grow up, protects intrinsic present value without denying that future consequences exist.

## The Last Time

Sam Harris’s observation is that finite life contains many activities done for the last time: holding a child, visiting a childhood home, swimming, making love, or speaking deeply with a friend. Usually one cannot know which instance is final, so treating each moment only as a means to a later one misses its irretrievability. Burkeman links this to capitalism’s broader pressure to turn time and human beings into instruments for future profit. The billable hour makes the problem explicit: an hour not sold can feel wasted, making family life or uncommodified participation hard to value. The fisherman and businessman parable illustrates the absurdity of deferring present enjoyment until a future retirement that simply reproduces the enjoyment already available.

## Absent in the Present

Trying too hard to savor an experience can drain it, as in Pirsig’s tourists who photograph the Rosetta Stone more than they look at it, or Burkeman’s effort to appreciate the northern lights. The same self-consciousness appears when someone tries to force presence while washing dishes or participating in an assigned increase in sexual activity. The problem is not that attention to the present is wrong; it is that the attempt treats “being here” as a state one can obtain from outside the moment. In reality, thoughts about failing to be present are also present-moment events, and there is no external place from which to extract life’s value. A less strained posture is to recognize that one is already here and let experience be what it is.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Instrumentalization**: Diagnose a present activity valued only as a means to a later result.
- **When-I-finally mind**: Notice deferred fulfillment and bring some value into the current activity.
- **Causal catastrophe**: Evaluate present life on its own terms as well as by future consequences.
- **Last time**: Treat finite experiences with care because their final instance is unknowable.
- **Already here**: Drop the project of obtaining presence from outside the moment one already inhabits.

## Key Concepts

- **Instrumental time**: Time valued for what it produces later.
- **Intrinsic value**: Worth possessed by an experience or activity as it is lived now.
- **When-I-finally mind**: The belief that life begins after a future condition is achieved.
- **Causal catastrophe**: Judging childhood only by future adult outcomes.
- **Last time**: The unmarked final occurrence of a finite activity.
- **Telos**: An end or outcome toward which an activity is directed.

## Mental Models

- Ask of any goal: **Does the process matter if I never see the final result?**
- Pair a future-consequence question with a **present-quality question**.
- When presence becomes effortful, notice that **the effort is already part of the present**.

## Anti-patterns

- **Postpone life until readiness**: Readiness becomes another future condition.
- **Judge a present solely by its product**: It devalues childhood, care, rest, and work whose results exceed a lifetime.
- **Perform presence**: Self-monitoring can obscure the experience it is meant to improve.

## Worked Example

When his son was born, Burkeman bought competing parenting books and tried to optimize the newborn months for future development. He later noticed that this treated the child as a means to his own future security and made him look past the child’s actual movements, needs, and age. The corrective was not to ignore vaccinations or other future responsibilities, but to see the baby’s present life as valuable in itself. “Zero years old” lasts for one year only, whether or not the future adult turns out as hoped.

## Key Takeaways

1. The future is important, but it cannot be allowed to erase present value.
2. Childhood, care, leisure, and long projects are not merely means to later outcomes.
3. Every finite activity has an unknowable last instance.
4. Presence cannot be manufactured from outside the moment.
5. Long-term commitment is lived through present acts, not postponed until fruition.
