# Chapter 12: The Loneliness of the Digital Nomad

## Core Idea

Individual control over when and where to work is often treated as the ideal form of temporal freedom, but it can deprive time of the coordination that gives it social value. Burkeman describes time as a **network good**: like a telephone network, it becomes more useful when other people can access and synchronize with it. Super Mario’s cruise-ship life shows the loneliness that can accompany eliminating chores and obligations while gaining maximum personal schedule control. Digital nomadism makes the trade-off explicit, but flexible work, remote work, and on-demand scheduling distribute it more widely. Shared vacations, fika, marching, singing, and community commitments show how common rhythms support happiness, cohesion, and collective action. The practical aim is not to surrender all boundaries, but to choose some constraints that make a common life possible.

## The Loneliness of the Digital Nomad

Mario Salcedo has removed housework and fixed domestic obligations by living on cruise ships, yet the portrait Burkeman cites shows him isolated among nominal friends and trying to insist on his happiness. Salcedo treats time like money—a good whose value rises with the amount one controls—while Burkeman argues that time also derives value from coordinated access. Socializing, parenting, business, politics, and technological work all require one person’s time to meet another’s. Possessing unlimited unshared time can therefore be actively unpleasant, resembling the old punishment of ostracism. The problem is not that Salcedo must be unhappy, but that maximum sovereignty is a poor guarantee of a meaningful life.

## In and Out of Sync

Digital nomads often gain movement and choice at the cost of the shared rhythms through which close relationships develop. Mark Manson’s account of visiting major landmarks alone, and a fellow traveler’s tears at seeing families cycling together, make the loneliness concrete. The same trade-off appears in flexible workplaces: personal control can increase while coordination with friends, family, and colleagues becomes harder. Terry Hartig’s study of Swedish vacation timing found that antidepressant use fell more when a larger share of the population was on holiday at the same time, including among retirees and unemployed people. The result suggests that well-being depends partly on **social regulation of time**, not only on private freedom.

## Keeping Together in Time

William McNeill’s military marching experience felt meaningful even though the drills were not needed for modern battlefield transport. Synchronized movement produced a sense of enlargement and collective well-being, and his historical work connects rhythm, marching, singing, and coordinated labor to group cohesion across societies. Synchrony appears in mundane places too: applause finds a rhythm, walkers match pace, choir voices combine, and food-cooperative workers share a temporary bond. The effect can be dangerous when commanders use group belonging to make soldiers willing to die, so synchrony is not automatically benign. But it reveals why shared action can make time feel thicker and more real than isolated individual activity.

## The Freedom to Never See Your Friends

Individual time sovereignty has legitimate uses: boundaries can protect people from bullying bosses, exploitative work, and people-pleasing. Yet when a society makes every schedule private and market-responsive, it desynchronizes rest, work, family life, and political organizing. The least privileged may have no sovereignty at all under unpredictable on-demand scheduling, while the more flexible may find work leaking into every gap. The Soviet experiment with staggered five-day calendars shows the extreme: factories ran continuously, but friends, spouses, families, and religious communities could rarely share a day off. Burkeman’s recommendation is to join recurring physical-world commitments—choirs, teams, campaigns, religious groups, or community practices—and let their rhythms sometimes outrank personal optimization.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Time as a network good**: Ask who else must be available for time to be useful.
- **Social regulation of time**: Use shared rules and schedules to support rest, relationships, and collective work.
- **Synchrony**: Recognize coordinated movement and shared timing as sources of belonging and power.
- **Communal freedom**: Trade some individual schedule sovereignty for participation in a common rhythm.

## Key Concepts

- **Time sovereignty**: Individual control over schedule and location.
- **Network good**: A good whose value rises with others’ access and coordination.
- **Synchrony**: Coordinated timing or movement among people.
- **Social regulation of time**: External shared rhythms that shape when people work, rest, and meet.
- **Desynchronization**: Loss of common temporal grooves across social life.
- **Fika**: A shared workplace pause for coffee, cake, and informal connection.

## Mental Models

- Treat a calendar as a **coordination infrastructure**, not only a personal possession.
- When flexibility increases loneliness, ask **What shared constraint is missing?**
- Use **rhythm before convenience** for recurring groups and relationships.

## Anti-patterns

- **Maximize personal schedule freedom**: More control can make common time impossible.
- **Confuse nomadism with communal flexibility**: Traditional nomads coordinate intensely; solitary digital travel may isolate.
- **Assume shared rhythms are inefficient**: Their value may be relational, civic, and psychological rather than throughput-based.

## Worked Example

The Soviet five-day week divided workers into color-coded calendars so factories never stopped. The system promised more frequent rest and less crowding, but friends assigned different colors could not meet, families were split, and holidays became solitary. Its failure was not simply inconvenience; it destroyed the common timing on which social life depended. The experiment makes visible what synchronized Swedish vacations and a workplace fika provide more gently.

## Key Takeaways

1. Time is valuable partly because other people can meet you in it.
2. Flexibility and travel have loneliness and coordination costs.
3. Shared pauses can improve well-being beyond individual rest time.
4. Synchrony creates belonging but can also be weaponized, so its effects need judgment.
5. Choose recurring communal commitments alongside protective personal boundaries.
