# Chapter 9: Rediscovering Rest

## Core Idea

Burkeman challenges the modern defense of leisure as useful only because it makes workers more productive. When rest must justify itself through recovery, self-improvement, or future experience, it becomes another task and loses the quality of leisure. Ancient philosophy and older communal calendars treated leisure as a central human end, with work as an interruption or necessity, rather than treating every free hour as an investment. Modern people also collaborate with the system: idleness aversion makes stopping feel unsafe, guilty, or empty. Genuine rest therefore requires deliberate rules, preparation, and tolerance for initial discomfort. Atelic activities—walking, music, conversation, hobbies—offer a practical alternative because their value lies in the doing, not in reaching a final accomplishment.

## The Decline of Pleasure

Take Back Your Time objected to defending vacations and lazy mornings merely by claiming they improve the economy, because that rationale still makes work the measure of life. The instrumental view turns running, meditation, travel, reading, and social events into means for future fitness, calm, experience, or status. Leisure then resembles a chore, despite the fact that many people have more nominal free time than earlier generations. Aristotle’s account reverses the hierarchy: true leisure is reflection and contemplation chosen for its own sake, while business literally means “not-leisure.” Industrialization separated work and leisure, then recast free time as recovery for more work. Burkeman’s conclusion is that some “wasteful” pleasure is the only way to inhabit free time without wasting it.

## Pathological Productivity

Danielle Steel’s extreme schedule illustrates idleness aversion: productivity can become a refuge from grief and difficult emotion rather than a simple work ethic. Max Weber’s account of the Protestant work ethic traces a similar psychological structure to a society in which relentless striving and thrift were signs that one might belong to the saved, even though predestination made salvation uncontrollable. Secular productivity can preserve the same promise of eventual justification, perfection, or invulnerability. Exercise, meditation, and self-improvement may therefore resemble penance when their main function is to expunge guilt about resting. Rest for its own sake requires accepting that no future state will make the present’s limits disappear.

## Rules for Rest

The Sabbath demonstrates that rest does not happen automatically when work stops; it needs boundaries, preparation, habit, and social sanction. A Shabbat elevator and detailed prohibitions look excessive only if one assumes that humans can casually downshift from striving. The communal day off also makes rest universal and temporarily asserts that whatever has been completed may be enough. Personal digital sabbaths can help, but they lack the reinforcement of a shared rule and remain vulnerable to willpower failure. The initial discomfort of genuinely stopping is evidence of how deeply productivity has been internalized, not proof that rest is wrong.

## Hiking as an End in Itself

A country walk is an **atelic activity**: it has no outcome whose completion exhausts its value, and the most efficient way to reach the endpoint would be not to begin. Kieran Setiya contrasts such activities with telic projects pursued for a result, and uses them to explain the emptiness of a life driven only by achievements. Schopenhauer’s pendulum between the pain of not having a desired outcome and the boredom after obtaining it leaves little room for activity valuable in its own process. Walking, music, conversation, and similar activities give a person a way to be involved without needing to finish or excel. They are especially relevant at midlife, when mortality makes endless deferral of fulfillment harder to ignore.

## Rod Stewart, Radical

Hobbies challenge instrumental culture by being worth doing without profit, prestige, or measurable improvement. Rod Stewart’s elaborate model railway is subversive partly because it conflicts with his public image and is therefore hard to interpret as a branding strategy. His admitted mediocrity is part of the freedom: an activity with no realistic prospect of acclaim can suspend performance anxiety. Karen Rinaldi’s surfing makes the same point more intensely; being bad at it taught patience, humility, and freedom to pursue the futile. A hobby becomes less restorative when it is converted into a side hustle or another performance metric.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Rest for its own sake**: Use leisure as an end, not merely as recovery for work.
- **Idleness aversion**: Recognize the discomfort that drives compulsive productivity.
- **Sabbath rule**: Protect rest with a recurring boundary, preparation, and social support.
- **Atelic activity**: Choose activities that cannot be completed and need not produce a result.
- **Mediocrity as freedom**: Let a hobby remain unoptimized and noncommercial.

## Key Concepts

- **Leisure**: Time chosen for its own sake, historically treated as life’s center of gravity.
- **Idleness aversion**: Difficulty tolerating nonproductive time.
- **Sabbath**: Communally reinforced interruption of striving.
- **Atelic activity**: Doing whose value is not exhausted by completion.
- **Telic activity**: Doing organized around an outcome.
- **Hobby**: An atelic, often deliberately nonprofessional activity.

## Mental Models

- Ask **What would I do if the activity’s only payoff were doing it?**
- Treat a recurring day or block of rest as **infrastructure**, not a reward for finishing.
- Use **safe mediocrity** to practice presence without performance stakes.

## Anti-patterns

- **Relax to become more productive**: Keeps work as the ultimate measure of value.
- **Fill leisure with self-improvement**: Converts rest into another obligation.
- **Monetize every hobby**: Removes the refuge from performance and profit.

## Worked Example

Burkeman’s hike across the wet Yorkshire Dales has no productive endpoint: he walks through rain, notices grouse and a disused church, and enjoys the moor’s bleakness. It counts as atelic because the value is in the walking and noticing, not in arriving at a measurable result. Rod Stewart’s model railway supplies the complementary example: a time-consuming, imperfect hobby pursued for love rather than brand or achievement. Together they show why “doing it badly” can be a form of freedom.

## Key Takeaways

1. Leisure does not need an economic defense.
2. Rest may initially feel uncomfortable because idleness aversion is learned and reinforced.
3. Rules and shared rhythms make genuine rest more likely.
4. Atelic activity restores value to the process itself.
5. Hobbies can protect time from profit, performance, and improvement pressure.
