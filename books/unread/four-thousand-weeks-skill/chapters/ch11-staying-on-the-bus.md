# Chapter 11: Staying on the Bus

## Core Idea

Patience looks passive when it is used to tell less powerful people to wait, but in an accelerated culture it can be a form of power. To resist hurry is to gain purchase on processes that cannot be forced, and to experience the work itself rather than deferring all value to completion. Jennifer Roberts’s three-hour painting exercise makes the initial discomfort of slow attention impossible to evade, then shows how perception deepens after impatience is exhausted. M. Scott Peck’s lawn-mower story shows the practical side: tolerating not knowing can reveal a solution that impulsive action obscures. Burkeman draws three rules from this: develop a taste for problems, practice radical incrementalism, and stay with a conventional route long enough for originality to emerge.

## Watching and Waiting

Roberts assigns students to look at one artwork for three uninterrupted hours because a quick glance can create the illusion of seeing while leaving the painting’s structure unnoticed. The first stretch is restless and absurdly long, but after the viewer stops trying to escape the slow passage of time, details, relationships, mood, and spatial ambiguity become perceptible. The assignment works by imposing an external constraint that counteracts a culture of speed; “not rushing” becomes the task. Peck’s repair of a car brake follows the same sequence: he gets comfortable, studies an apparent jumble without immediate action, and eventually sees a small latch whose movement solves the problem. Patience is therefore an active alertness, not resignation; it permits information and solutions to arrive.

## Three Principles of Patience

### 1. Develop a taste for problems

A problem is simply something that demands one’s attention, and a life with no such demands would have nothing worth doing. Treating each problem as a second problem because it “should not” exist creates needless urgency and a fantasy of a problem-free future. Giving up that fantasy makes it possible to engage with one problem after another as the substance of a meaningful life. Patience becomes easier when the presence of difficulty is no longer interpreted as evidence that life is off track.

### 2. Embrace radical incrementalism

Robert Boice’s research on academics found that sustained writers made writing a smaller, repeatable part of each day, sometimes using very short sessions and regular weekends off. Their willingness to produce little on a given day resulted in more over the long term than deadline-driven binges. Stopping when a preselected session ends is part of the practice: pushing onward can be impatience about not being finished and can teach the mind to hate the work. Small, returnable sessions build endurance and respect the speed of creative process.

### 3. Stay on the bus

Arno Minkkinen’s Helsinki bus-station parable compares early career work to routes that initially share the same stops. Switching genres whenever the work looks derivative sends the artist back to the beginning, while staying on the route allows it eventually to diverge and become distinctive. The principle applies beyond art to marriage, parenthood, place, and community, where depth is reserved for people who remain through the unglamorous early stages. Staying is not blind loyalty: evidence can justify leaving, but impatience with ordinary beginnings is not evidence of failure.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Patience as power**: Use deliberate slowness to resist a speed culture and engage processes on their terms.
- **External tempo constraint**: Make not rushing the assignment when default habits would rush it.
- **Taste for problems**: See necessary difficulty as the material of meaningful activity.
- **Radical incrementalism**: Work in small repeatable sessions, stop on time, and return.
- **Stay on the bus**: Remain through the common early route long enough for originality or depth to develop.

## Key Concepts

- **Patience**: Active, alert resistance to forcing a process’s pace.
- **Purchase on reality**: Practical influence gained by working with conditions rather than against them.
- **Radical incrementalism**: Sustainable progress through small repeated efforts.
- **Unoriginality phase**: Early period in which work resembles existing work while skills develop.
- **Endurance**: Ability to remain with a problem or process over time.
- **Taste for problems**: Willingness to see demands as the substance of a life.

## Mental Models

- Use a **three-hour painting** to test whether impatience is obscuring information.
- When stuck, shift from **solve immediately** to **study until the structure appears**.
- Ask whether leaving is evidence-based or simply an attempt to skip the common route.

## Anti-patterns

- **Call patience resignation**: Active pacing can increase agency and perception.
- **Burst-and-burn work**: Intense sessions can produce avoidance of the work later.
- **Switch at the first sign of derivation**: Originality often comes after the shared learning phase.

## Worked Example

Roberts’s Degas exercise begins with forty minutes of regret and restless thoughts about choosing another painting. After roughly eighty minutes, the viewer gives up trying to make the experience move faster. The painting then reveals social expressions, an ambiguous shadow, a figure that can appear solid or ghostly, and the sensory atmosphere of the room. The second-order change is not that the painting became objectively different; the viewer’s surrender made a deeper encounter possible.

## Key Takeaways

1. Patience can be muscular resistance to a culture of hurry.
2. Problems are not interruptions to meaningful life; they supply its demands.
3. Sustainable small sessions can outperform anxious bursts.
4. Stopping on time protects the ability to return.
5. Distinctive work and relationships usually require staying through unremarkable beginnings.
