# Glossary — Four Thousand Weeks

**Atelic activity** — An activity whose value lies in doing it rather than completing an outcome; walking, music, and conversation are examples (Ch 9).

**Attention economy** — A business system that captures and sells human attention, often using emotionally compelling content and persuasive design (Ch 5).

**Being-towards-death** — Heidegger’s name for living with clear awareness that existence is finite and death may arrive at any time (Ch 3).

**Bottom-up attention** — Involuntary attention captured by salient events; it is necessary for survival and cannot be eliminated (Ch 5).

**Causal catastrophe** — Treating a present activity, especially childhood, as valuable only for the future result it produces (Ch 8).

**Deep time** — Immersive “timeless” experience in which clock time recedes and reality is encountered directly (Ch 1, Ch 9).

**Efficiency trap** — The tendency for efficiency gains to generate more demand, higher standards, or more inputs, leaving no felt surplus of time (Ch 2).

**Eigenzeit** — The time inherent to a process itself; useful work often requires letting a process take its own time (Ch 1).

**Existential overwhelm** — The unbridgeable gap between the many worthwhile experiences available and the few a finite person can live (Ch 2).

**Finitude** — The condition of being a limited human life, already situated between birth and an uncertain death (Ch 3, Ch 14).

**Hofstadter’s law** — A task takes longer than expected, even after accounting for the tendency to underestimate its duration (Ch 7).

**Idleness aversion** — Discomfort with resting or doing nothing, often expressed as a compulsion to remain productive (Ch 9).

**Intimate interrupter** — The inner urge to flee a meaningful but difficult present task by redirecting attention to an easier distraction (Ch 6).

**Joy of missing out** — Relief and meaning found in recognizing that choosing one finite path necessarily means forgoing others (Ch 3, Ch 4).

**Limit-embracing life** — Organizing time on the assumption that not everything can be done, then choosing priorities and neglect deliberately (Ch 1).

**Network good** — A good whose value depends on others having access to it and coordinating with you; time has this social property (Ch 12).

**Paradox of limitation** — Fighting human limits increases stress and emptiness; accepting and working with them increases meaning and usable agency (Ch 1).

**Parkinson’s law** — Work expands to fill the available time; Burkeman extends the idea to the definition of what needs doing (Ch 2).

**Patience** — Active willingness to let reality, other people, and processes unfold at their own pace (Ch 10, Ch 11).

**Provisional life** — Living as if real meaning and security begin later, after one has finally mastered time or achieved the right life conditions (Ch 14).

**Radical incrementalism** — Making small, repeatable progress at a sustainable pace, including stopping when the planned session ends (Ch 11).

**Second-order change** — A shift in perspective that changes the whole pattern, such as giving up the attempt to outrun anxiety (Ch 10, Ch 11).

**Social regulation of time** — Shared external rules and rhythms that make rest, coordination, and common life possible (Ch 12).

**Task orientation** — Scheduling life by the needs and rhythms of tasks rather than by an abstract, externally imposed timeline (Ch 1).

**Telic activity** — An activity organized around an end state or completed outcome, contrasted with an atelic activity (Ch 9).

**Time sovereignty** — The ideal of individually controlling when and where one works or rests; Burkeman contrasts it with communal synchrony (Ch 12).

**Top-down attention** — Voluntary attention directed by intention; limited influence over it is essential for meaningful experience (Ch 5).

**Variable rewards** — Unpredictable rewards that encourage repeated checking, such as refresh-to-see-what-appears interaction design (Ch 5).

**Work in progress** — Projects currently active; limiting their number exposes finite capacity and reduces avoidance through switching (Ch 4).
