# Chapter 7: What Is Happening to Boys?

**Part**: 3 — The Great Rewiring: The Rise of the Phone-Based Childhood

## Core Idea

Haidt argues that boys took a different route through the Great Rewiring: their decline began earlier, the evidence is more diffuse, and the causal story is more speculative than the girls-and-social-media case. Structural changes reduced the value of physical strength, weakened school and work pathways, and reduced positive male role models, while safetyism removed the risky, agency-building play boys often prefer. The expanding virtual world then offered games, pornography, online communities, and eventually pocket access to stimulation, agency, and pseudo-connection without requiring the real-world risks of friendship, work, dating, or adult responsibility. The result is not simply more clinical depression; it is disengagement, loneliness, “failure to launch,” retreat into bedrooms, and a shift from externalizing toward internalizing symptoms. Video games have real cognitive, social, and mood benefits for many users, but a minority develops substantial impairment and many heavy users pay a large opportunity cost. Haidt's unifying explanation is that boys were pushed away from an increasingly inhospitable real world and pulled into a virtual world that could satisfy desires without building the competencies needed on Earth.

## No Chance of a Successful Life

Boys' agreement with the statement that people like them have little chance of a successful life rose gradually from the late 1970s and accelerated in the early 2010s. Girls began at a higher level of internalizing distress, but their comparable pessimism changed much later and more abruptly. This contrast is one reason Haidt describes boys' decline as a long push-pull process rather than a single social-media shock. The indicator points to perceived purpose and opportunity, not a complete measure of mental illness. It sets up the argument that economic and cultural signals made the real world less inviting before smartphones intensified withdrawal.

## The Long Decline of Males

Richard Reeves's evidence shows boys and men falling behind girls in grades, reading, high-school completion, college degrees, and some measures of employment and well-being. Deindustrialization reduced jobs that rewarded physical strength, while the service economy increasingly rewarded sitting still, listening, communicating, and formal educational attainment. Women moved from 42% of bachelor's degrees in 1972 to 59% in 2019, while boys also faced higher rates of ADHD, suspension, and expulsion. Haidt accepts Reeves's structural account but adds two psychological factors: safetyism removed more of the rough-and-tumble play boys favored, and connected devices pulled them away from embodied social life. A world of floundering men is not a victory for women because families, workplaces, and communities need competent, engaged adults of both sexes.

## Boys Who Fail to Launch

“Failure to launch” describes a young adult who cannot enter work or independent life and returns to the parental home; the related U.K. term NEET means not in education, employment, or training. Young men are more likely than young women to live with parents and make up most non-disabled, non-parent NEETs in the cited data. Japan's hikikomori show an extreme form: retreat to a bedroom can reduce anxiety in the short run while making outside competence and re-entry harder. The internet changed the economics of withdrawal by supplying games, information, social contact, and sex without the boredom that once forced a homebound boy to confront his situation. Haidt uses these cases to show how a room can feel like freedom while functioning as an avoidance loop.

## Boyhood Without Real-World Risk

Boys historically showed more externalizing behavior and risk-taking, while girls showed more internalizing distress. Around 2010, both sexes reported more internalizing symptoms and fewer externalizing ones; boys' risk-taking enjoyment and injury hospitalizations fell especially sharply. The decline in fights, alcohol, accidents, and unplanned pregnancies is good in itself, but Haidt asks whether boys also lost healthy risk-taking, adventure, and embodied competence. Safetyism is likely to affect boys more because rough-and-tumble and risky play are more central to their typical play patterns. A safer statistic can therefore coexist with a developmental loss if the missing experiences taught judgment, self-reliance, and courage.

### Internalizing and Externalizing Symptoms (U.S. Teens)

The U.S. symptom figure shows the unusual convergence: boys moved toward more internalizing symptoms while both sexes showed less externalizing behavior. This is not an argument to restore violence or reckless harm. It is evidence that lower visible misconduct does not settle whether boys are gaining the experience and confidence needed for adult life.

### Enjoyment of Risk-Taking

Boys' reported enjoyment of risk-taking also declined, especially around the years when real-world independence was being withdrawn. Haidt treats this as a possible loss of healthy approach behavior, not as a reason to celebrate danger for its own sake. The developmental question is whether boys still have safe ways to test limits, solve problems, and build agency.

### Hospital Admissions for Unintentional Injuries

Hospital admissions for unintentional injuries fell as boys spent less time in unsupervised physical activity. Fewer serious injuries are beneficial, but the measure cannot tell us whether the fall reflects safer judgment, less exposure, or both. Haidt places it beside the risk-taking data to show why protection and development must be evaluated together.

## The Virtual World Welcomes Boys

Arcade games, home computers, the public internet, first-person shooters, and massively multiplayer games expanded from the 1970s through the 1990s. Wi-Fi, broadband, YouTube, pornography sites, and internet-connected Xbox and PlayStation consoles then made private, long sessions possible in the 2000s. Boys could pursue agency through exploration, competition, mastery, and simulated war while interacting with others from bedrooms. The attraction was not irrational: the real world offered fewer physical jobs, more adult supervision, and less freedom, while the virtual world supplied immediate feedback and an audience. The problem is that simulated agency and virtual communion do not necessarily develop work, friendship, risk, or self-governance in the physical community.

## The Virtual World Consumes Boys

Before smartphones, companies could capture boys' attention mainly when they sat at a computer or console. Smartphones exposed them to games, feeds, video, pornography, and advertising at every waking moment, while free-to-use and pay-to-progress models intensified the competition for attention and money. Boys' social and status negotiations therefore moved online along with their entertainment, producing a bazaar of platforms rather than one simple cause. The early-2010s rise in depression, anxiety, self-harm, and suicide makes the smartphone a critical threshold, but Haidt explicitly says the boys' evidence is less settled. The central mechanism is cumulative: a virtual world that can meet immediate desires begins to consume the time needed to become capable in the real one.

## Pornography

Haidt treats pornography as an example of an evolved lure—sexual desire—being separated from the real-world skills and relationship through which it historically led to intimacy and reproduction. Broadband made an effectively infinite supply of high-intensity porn private, free, and available to minors; the cited Swedish study reports daily use among boys rising from 11% in 2004 to 24% in 2014. Heavy use can become the easy route to sexual reward, reducing motivation to approach potential partners and correlating with lower interpersonal and sexual satisfaction, especially among males. The book is careful not to say all pornography is harmful, but it warns about immersion during the period when sexual and romantic systems are being rewired. Generative AI, virtual partners, and spatial porn may make the substitute more compelling, increasing the risk that avoidance becomes a preferred lifestyle.

### Daily Porn Users, Swedish 12th Graders

The Swedish figure shows daily pornography use among boys rising from 11% in 2004 to 24% in 2014. Haidt uses it as an indicator of the changing availability and normalization of high-intensity sexual content, not as evidence that every viewer is addicted. The relevant developmental concern is that private, unlimited access can substitute for the uncertain work of real-world intimacy during puberty.

## Video Games

Video games are more complicated than pornography or image-based social media. Studies cited by Haidt associate some game use with working memory, response inhibition, school competence, mood improvement, and cooperation, and he rejects a blanket ban. The main risks are impairment among heavy users and opportunity cost: a substantial minority loses interest in other activities, withdraws from family and friends, lies, or cannot control play. A four-group model separates casual, engaged, problematic, and addicted gamers; Haidt uses roughly 7% of adolescent boys as a middle-ground estimate for substantial internet-gaming disorder or impairment, while noting disagreement about diagnosis and underlying conditions. The right question is not only “how many hours?” but whether gaming is displacing sleep, eating, hygiene, school, work, friendship, and the ability to stop.

## All Screens and No (Real-World) Play

Online games can involve real friends, teamwork, and shared goals, but the platform usually supplies the rules, rewards, and adjudication. Free play requires children to act as legislators and judges of their own group, while a game removes much of that practice and supplies disembodied thrills rather than embodied risk. If online gaming were a net substitute for friendship, boys should have become less lonely; instead, the chapter cites rising loneliness and a fivefold increase in American men reporting no close friends from the 1990s to 2021. Stable teams and real-world packs build durable bonds more reliably than shifting virtual groups, though virtual groups can be precious to boys who have no other community. Heavy gaming should therefore be judged by whether it supports or replaces the real-world relationships and responsibilities that give it meaning.

## Technology, Freedom, and Meaninglessness

Émile Durkheim's concept of anomie—normlessness or loss of binding social order—explains why unlimited choice can feel like being lost rather than liberated. Communities provide roots, stable people, shared rules, and goals that constrain desire while making action meaningful. Children raised across anonymous, rapidly mutating networks instead encounter a tornado of memes, micro-dramas, aliases, and disappearing relationships. This environment can satisfy some immediate wants but offers little stable path from childhood to adult identity. Boys and girls reach the same pit through different routes: loneliness, isolation, weakened community, and a rising sense that life is meaningless.

## Life Often Feels Meaningless

The shared endpoint is not merely a lack of entertainment but a loss of purpose and belonging. When children have many choices yet few stable communities, they can experience freedom as drift and stimulation as anomie. Boys may reach that state through gaming and withdrawal; girls may reach it through comparison and relational turmoil. The common remedy is meaningful participation in real people, places, and responsibilities.

## In Sum

Boys' decline begins with a long push away from real-world agency and is intensified by a virtual world that supplies stimulation, status, sex, and simulated mastery. The chapter distinguishes structural headwinds from the later smartphone threshold and treats its causal claims as more tentative than the girls' social-media case. Gaming can help, but pornography, impairment, and opportunity cost can turn a refuge into an avoidance loop. Re-engagement requires risk, work, mentorship, friendship, and meaning in the physical world.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Push-pull analysis**: Identify what makes the real world less hospitable and what makes the virtual world more attractive.
- **Failure to launch / NEET**: Use to track whether adolescence is producing work, training, responsibility, and independent adult participation.
- **Hikikomori loop**: Retreat reduces anxiety briefly, but reduced practice makes re-entry harder and reinforces retreat.
- **Gaming impairment test**: Look for loss of control, withdrawal, conflict, relapse, and interference with functioning rather than hours alone.
- **Anomie**: Use when freedom without stable community, norms, and purpose produces meaninglessness.

## Key Concepts

- **Agency**: Desire to explore, master, compete, assert, and have an effect.
- **Failure to launch**: Failure to move into employment, independence, or adult responsibility.
- **NEET**: Not in education, employment, or training.
- **Hikikomori**: Extreme social withdrawal and retreat into a bedroom, originally described in Japan.
- **Internet gaming disorder**: Gaming pattern involving substantial impairment or distress.
- **Problematic gamer**: Heavy player with negative consequences but less complete loss of control.
- **Externalizing symptoms**: Outward anger, violence, risk, or conduct problems.
- **Anomie**: Normlessness and loss of binding social order or purpose.
- **Opportunity cost**: Real-world development and relationships forgone for virtual activity.

## Mental Models

- Ask what is pushing a young person away before treating the virtual world as the whole cause.
- Separate games that strengthen a real-world pack from games that replace every pack.
- Evaluate a technology by the competencies it develops, not just the pleasure or mastery it simulates.
- Treat “freedom” as incomplete when there is no path to meaning, responsibility, and re-entry into community.

## Anti-patterns

- **Celebrating every decline in teen risk**: Reduced accidents can hide a parallel loss of healthy adventure and judgment practice.
- **Banning all games indiscriminately**: The source distinguishes benefits, engaged use, problematic use, and addiction.
- **Calling a bedroom-only life freedom without checking capability**: Avoidance can feel autonomous while making the outside world more frightening.
- **Using the girls' social-media mechanism for boys by default**: Boys' pathways involve different platforms, incentives, and evidence.

## Worked Example

Chris describes years in which gaming and pornography filled nearly every waking moment, displacing sleep, school, in-person play, and later dating. He remembers genuine fun and does not regard all gaming as harmful, but after moderating his use with help from friends and family he could see the lost socialization, weak conversational fluency, limited world knowledge, and sense of being a “hollow operating system.” The example shows the difference between enjoyment and impairment. It also shows why recovery requires real relationships and responsibilities, not merely removal of a screen.

## Key Takeaways

1. Boys' crisis is more diffuse: structural decline and safetyism preceded the smartphone threshold.
2. Smartphones intensified an existing push-pull by making high-stimulation virtual activities portable and private.
3. Pornography can separate sexual reward from relationship-building; heavy use is the relevant concern.
4. Games can help some users, but roughly one in thirteen adolescent boys may suffer substantial impairment under the cited estimate.
5. The common endpoint for boys and girls is loneliness and anomie when virtual networks replace rooted communities.
