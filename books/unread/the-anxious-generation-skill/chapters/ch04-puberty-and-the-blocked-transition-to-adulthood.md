# Chapter 4: Puberty and the Blocked Transition to Adulthood

**Part**: 2 — The Backstory: The Decline of the Play-Based Childhood

## Core Idea

Puberty is a period of accelerated neural pruning and myelination, so the experiences adolescents repeat can become durable features of the adult brain. Haidt describes this as both a risk period, because sustained stress can do more harm, and an opportunity period, because helpful interventions can have greater leverage. Safetyism and smartphones are “experience blockers”: one removes real-world challenge and the other crowds out real-world experience with an undifferentiated digital stream. Human societies historically used rites of passage and age-graded responsibilities to guide children through separation, transformation, and reincorporation into adult roles. Modern secular societies weakened those shared markers while the internet erased meaningful age thresholds and exposed minors to adult content. Haidt proposes a norm-based ladder of increasing freedom, responsibility, mobility, mentorship, and digital access rather than leaving children to drift between dependency and unbounded virtual adulthood.

## Puberty, Plasticity, and Vulnerability

The adolescent brain is not simply getting bigger; it is selecting and insulating the connections that experience uses repeatedly. Pruning removes infrequently used synapses, while myelination makes frequently used long-distance pathways faster, like wet cement becoming fixed. Because these processes accelerate at puberty, repeated activities—archery, painting, games, social media, or other rewarding practices—can shape lasting habits and identities. Laurence Steinberg's distinction is central: adolescence is not necessarily more stressful than other periods, but its plastic brain is more vulnerable to sustained stress and also more responsive to beneficial intervention. The sensible implication is to pay special attention to nutrition, sleep, exercise, social context, and the sources of cultural learning during the first years of puberty.

## Experience Blockers: Safetyism and Smartphones

Children need a wide range of social experiences, including some fear, conflict, and exclusion in doses that can be recovered from. Safetyism removes too many of these experiences; smartphones remove even more by making passive, stimulating, always-available activity easier than physical and social participation. Haidt uses the cuckoo's egg as a metaphor: once a smartphone or game console arrives, it can push competing activities out of the nest by capturing the family's time and attention. Text and emojis do not tune the same systems as facial expressions, voice, eye contact, body language, and synchronous repair, even though video calls are closer than passive viewing. The chapter does not say digital experience is empty—online communities and information can be valuable—but argues that the developmental opportunity cost is decisive when screens dominate sensitive years. Remove both blockers before trying to optimize the child's behavior inside them.

## Rites of Passage

Arnold van Gennep found a recurring three-part structure in puberty rites: separation from childhood and parents, a transition guided by non-parental adults through challenges or instruction, and joyful reincorporation into the community with new status and responsibility. Apache girls' sunrise dance, Blackfoot vision quests, and Jewish Bar or Bat Mitzvahs illustrate different cultural content serving this common function. Boys' and girls' rites often reflected gendered social roles, but the book's developmental point does not require reproducing those roles. When adults fail to provide meaningful rites, adolescents often invent their own in fraternities, gangs, sororities, or peer groups; without elder guidance, these can become cruel, humiliating, or dangerous. Adolescents appear to want belonging, mentors, challenge, and public recognition of growth, not merely biological maturation.

## Why Do We Block the Transition to Adulthood?

Modern societies lost many formal rites and then weakened informal milestones such as going to movies alone at 13, driving at 16, and becoming a legal adult at 18. The decline in driver's licenses, paid work, alcohol, and sexual intercourse began in the 1990s or early 2000s, before the sharp 2010–2015 mental-health surge, so the broader retreat from real-world adulthood predates smartphones. At the same time, the internet made age almost irrelevant: children could encounter adult content, anonymous adults, and addictive platforms as soon as they could use a browser, with no meaningful increase in rights or responsibility at each age. The result was a blocked pathway in which children were over-supervised in physical space yet underprotected in virtual space. COVID-era online life intensified a pattern already in place.

## Teens Engaging in Adult Activities

Haidt's figures show that conventional adult activities declined well before the Great Rewiring, but the online world became more available and more attractive at the same time. A child could open a platform by claiming to be 13 or 18, and companies often had no incentive to verify the claim because younger users were valuable long-term customers. There was no digital equivalent of a PG-13 film rating, a driver's-license milestone, or an 18th-birthday change in legal status. “Everyone is the same age” online means no age-graded practice in judgment, responsibility, or independence. This mismatch helps explain why an adolescent can have access to adult content without acquiring adult competence.

### But it doesn’t have to be this way.

The loss of shared milestones is historically recent, not an inevitable consequence of modern life. A large, diverse, secular society may not agree on one sacred initiation ceremony, but it can still agree that children should gain freedom, responsibility, and mentors step by step. The relevant choice is between designing a visible path to adulthood and leaving the path to algorithms, peers, or chance.

## Building a Ladder from Childhood to Adulthood

Haidt proposes shared norms rather than a single legally mandated ceremony. At 6, children can take household responsibility; at 8, local unsupervised play and small errands; at 10, wider roaming and perhaps a basic phone; at 12, apprenticeships and trusted adults beyond parents; at 14, high school, work, teams, and a first smartphone as a community norm; at 16, internet adulthood and enforceable social-media access; at 18, legal adulthood; and at 21, full legal adulthood in U.S. law. The ladder links freedom to demonstrated responsibility and makes growth visible to children, parents, and communities. It is intentionally adaptable to local safety, culture, and individual pace. The larger principle is to replace undifferentiated dependency-plus-online-access with age-graded challenges, mentors, rights, and duties.

## In Sum

Puberty makes experience unusually formative, but modern societies have blocked both the real-world practice and the social markers that once guided the transition to adulthood. Safetyism keeps children dependent while the internet makes adult-scale content available without adult-scale responsibility. Rites of passage and age-graded milestones offer a practical alternative: challenge, mentorship, public recognition, and growing rights in a visible sequence. The ages can vary, but the developmental ladder should not disappear.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Adolescent plasticity**: Use puberty as a window when repeated inputs can leave lasting neural and identity effects.
- **Experience blockers**: Diagnose whether safetyism or connected devices are crowding out necessary experience.
- **Van Gennep's three phases**: Design a transition with separation, guided transformation, and reincorporation.
- **Age-graded ladder**: Link increasing freedom, responsibility, mobility, mentorship, and digital rights to visible milestones.

## Key Concepts

- **Pruning**: Removal of infrequently used neural connections during development.
- **Myelination**: Insulating axons to speed transmission in repeatedly used pathways.
- **Plasticity**: The brain's heightened capacity to be shaped by experience.
- **Chronic stress**: Stress lasting days, weeks, or years and harder to adapt to than brief challenge.
- **Acute stress**: A quickly arising, time-limited challenge such as an ordinary playground conflict.
- **Rite of passage**: Community process that marks a change in social status.
- **Separation / transition / reincorporation**: Van Gennep's three phases of a rite.
- **Internet adulthood**: Legal or social threshold for opening accounts and accepting terms.
- **Age-graded autonomy**: Increasing rights and duties that correspond to growing competence.

## Mental Models

- Treat puberty as wet cement: repeated inputs deserve careful selection.
- Design adolescence as a bridge with rungs, not a cliff from parental control to adult independence.
- Keep cultural learning age-graded; do not let algorithms flatten all content into one stream.
- Distinguish “more challenge” from “more chronic stress”; the goal is calibrated growth.

## Anti-patterns

- **Blocking all risk and calling it preparation**: It deprives children of the practice needed to assess and recover from risk.
- **Giving smartphone access as the only adolescent milestone**: It grants content and attention capture without responsibility or mentorship.
- **Confusing account age with maturity**: A self-entered birth date is not an ability threshold.
- **Treating every rite as harmless**: Peer-created hazing shows why adult guidance and safe reincorporation matter.

## Worked Example

The proposed ladder makes the transition concrete. A child might receive chores and allowance at six, walk and play locally at eight, roam more widely with a basic phone at ten, begin a neighbor apprenticeship at twelve, start high school and perhaps receive a smartphone at fourteen, open social-media accounts at sixteen, and assume legal adult rights at eighteen. Each rung combines freedom with responsibility rather than treating independence as a single permission. The ages are norms, not a universal prescription; the source's point is that communities should make development visible and cumulative.

## Key Takeaways

1. Puberty is both a vulnerability window and a high-leverage opportunity for intervention.
2. Safetyism and smartphones block different halves of the experience children need.
3. Rites of passage supply mentors, challenges, belonging, and public recognition of growing status.
4. Modern online life removes age thresholds while offering children adult content and adult-scale audiences.
5. Use shared, adaptable milestones to connect freedom with responsibility and real-world competence.
