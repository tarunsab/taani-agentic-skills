# Chapter 11: What Schools Can Do Now

**Part**: 4 — Collective Action for Healthier Childhood

## Core Idea

Haidt says schools often respond to the mental-health surge by adding therapists, social-emotional curricula, and special programs while missing two large levers already underfoot. The “two whales” are truly phone-free schools and much more free play; together they are intended as prevention rather than treatment after distress has accumulated. A phone-free school stores phones and texting-capable devices for the entire day, releasing attention and social life from the pocket. A play-full school expands unstructured, mixed-age, minimally supervised play through recess, Play Club, better playgrounds, and fewer rules. The chapter's examples suggest improvements in belonging, behavior, attendance, and learning, but Haidt calls for whole-school randomized experiments rather than treating anecdotes as settled causal proof. The broader goal is to start in elementary and middle school, before anxiety and device addiction flow into high school.

## Phone-Free Schools

Mountain Middle School in Colorado banned phones from students' pockets, hands, and school day in 2012, using clear consequences. Students who previously sat silently scrolling began talking with one another and teachers, while academic performance and the school's rating improved. Haidt links the policy to three foundational harms—attention fragmentation, social deprivation, and addiction—and to reductions in social comparison. A class-only ban is ineffective because students hide use during lessons and immediately return to feeds between classes; it also turns teachers into full-time phone police. Lockers or lockable pouches create a simple, visible rule and preserve adult communication during emergencies through staff-managed channels. The policy should cover smartwatches and other devices that can text or pull students into the virtual world, while still allowing schools to use internet tools intentionally for learning.

## Learning, Inequality, and the Digital Divide

UNESCO's report, as summarized by Haidt, recognizes the internet's educational value but finds little evidence that personal mobile devices improve the typical classroom and associates phone use with disruption and lower performance. The relevant target is not internet-free education: search, Khan Academy, coding, statistical tools, graphic design, AI tutors, and teacher demonstrations can be valuable. It is the personal, attention-seeking device with notifications and social feeds that has the worst cost–benefit ratio. Haidt also connects the post-2012 reversal in U.S. test-score trends to attention fragmentation, with the largest drops among lower-scoring students. Because lower-income, Black, Latino, and single-parent households report more screen time and less supervision on average, unprotected access may now widen educational inequality rather than close an earlier access gap.

## Play-Full Schools

Kevin Stinehart added longer recess with little adult intervention, a half-hour of playground time before school, and a mixed-age Play Club after school. The Play Club used loose parts and a no-phone zone, gave children near-complete autonomy, and intervened only for emergencies; the two rules were not deliberately hurting anyone and not leaving without telling the responsible adult. In the first semester, Kevin reported happier and kinder students, fewer behavior problems, more friendships, and a fall in truancy, bus violations, and office referrals. Haidt contrasts this with adult-led social-emotional curricula: free play teaches empathy, regulation, collaboration, creativity, and self-direction as a side effect of children pursuing their own activity. The example is promising but remains an implementation report, which is why Haidt wants school-level trials.

## The Let Grow Project

The Let Grow Project asks a child from kindergarten through middle school to do something they have never done alone, after agreeing with their parents on a reasonable task. Walking the dog, making a meal, running an errand, or managing a small responsibility provides a controlled bridge from dependence to competence. The child usually succeeds, changing both the child's identity and the parent's estimate of what is possible. Haidt describes a seventh-grade health class that completed many projects—cooking, biking, babysitting, speaking to adults, and going to the park—and reported lower anxiety and more confidence. The project also addresses the collective trap because school homework makes independence visible and normal across a group of families.

## Better Recess and Playgrounds

Haidt proposes three changes: more recess, better playgrounds, and fewer rules. Recess was cut as schools pursued test preparation after *A Nation at Risk*, No Child Left Behind, and Common Core pressures, even though pediatric research associates recess with social, physical, cognitive, and academic benefits. Adventure playgrounds use loose parts, tools, natural materials, and water to give children control over the environment, cooperation, creativity, and manageable risk. The source cites Governors Island's junk playground and nature playgrounds as models while retaining adult monitoring for serious danger. At New Zealand's Swanson Primary, a “No Rules Recess” study produced more activity and minor pushing but less injury, vandalism, and bullying after the school let children manage their own play. The point is not to abolish all adult presence; it is to transfer ordinary responsibility from hovering adults to children.

## Re-engage Boys

Haidt draws on Richard Reeves to recommend more CTE and vocational pathways and more male teachers. Boys often have few male role models in school, and the cited evidence finds that male teachers can improve boys' academic outcomes, especially in English. A gender-balanced staff can also show that caring, education, administration, and literacy—the “HEAL” fields—are open to men in an economy moving away from physical-strength jobs. Without positive local models, boys may look online for identity and be pulled toward extreme communities. Better recess and real-world agency are part of the same re-engagement strategy.

## The Education Experiment We Most Need

Haidt proposes a whole-school factorial experiment across elementary and middle schools. Interested schools would be randomly assigned to phone-free, play-full, phone-free plus play-full, or control conditions, with outcomes measured over two years. The design tests both the separate and combined effects and captures culture-level changes that individual-level studies miss. Variants could add the Let Grow Project or compare different implementation styles. The experiment is important because it makes the recommendation falsifiable and can identify which combination produces the greatest improvement in mental health, belonging, achievement, and behavior.

## In Sum

Schools can change the developmental environment for every child at once. Full-day phone storage restores attention and face-to-face social opportunity, while longer, looser play restores agency, conflict repair, and physical competence. The examples are encouraging but not substitutes for randomized whole-school evidence, so the proposed factorial trial keeps the package testable. The two whales are mutually reinforcing: a phone-free day creates room for play, and a play-full culture gives children a reason to put the phone away.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Two whales**: Prioritize phone-free and play-full schools before adding many smaller programs.
- **True phone-free day**: Store phones, smartwatches, and texting-capable devices for the entire school day.
- **Play-full school**: Add unstructured play before/after school, longer recess, loose parts, and minimal adult direction.
- **Let Grow Project**: Assign one agreed-upon independent task to normalize autonomy and responsibility.
- **Whole-school factorial experiment**: Compare phone-free, play-full, combined, and control schools to test culture-level effects.
- **HEAL pathway**: Use health, education, administration, and literacy roles as visible routes for boys.

## Key Concepts

- **Phone-free school**: School day in which personal communication devices are stored, not merely unused during lessons.
- **Play-full school**: School that gives abundant, child-directed free play and autonomy.
- **Play Club**: Mixed-age, no-phone, loose-parts free-play period with minimal adult intervention.
- **Loose parts**: Movable materials children can combine, drag, build with, or repurpose.
- **Adventure playground**: Playground designed for imaginative control, nature, and manageable risk.
- **Let Grow Project**: Independent-task homework agreed by child and parent.
- **Digital divide**: Shift from unequal access to unequal protection from online distraction and harm.
- **Factorial experiment**: Design testing separate and combined interventions across groups.

## Mental Models

- Treat school culture as the unit of intervention when the harm is networked.
- Remove the device from the pocket rather than asking teachers to win a constant attention contest.
- Use play as prevention: practice the skills therapists later try to repair.
- When rules are necessary, reserve adults for serious safety while children govern ordinary play.

## Anti-patterns

- **Class-only phone bans**: They shift phone use to hallways and force hidden use during lessons.
- **Adding curricula while leaving the cause untouched**: More social-emotional instruction cannot compensate for constant notifications and lost play.
- **Punishing with recess removal**: It deprives the students who may need the developmental benefits most.
- **Overmanaging adventure play**: Adult direction removes agency, negotiation, and self-governance.
- **Assuming internet-free is the same as phone-free**: Schools can use shared or teacher-directed technology without carrying personal attention traps.

## Worked Example

At Central Academy of the Arts, Kevin's Play Club kept the school open after class for mixed-age, loose-parts play with only two rules and emergency-level adult intervention. Alongside longer recess and morning playground time, the school reported a change from about 225 annual office referrals to about 45, with truancy and bus violations also falling. The example is a promising natural experiment, not a randomized proof; its value is that the mechanism is visible. Children practiced friendship, regulation, responsibility, and problem-solving while playing, so the same activity served prevention, belonging, and skill development.

## Key Takeaways

1. The high-leverage school package is full-day phone storage plus abundant, minimally managed play.
2. Use shared technology for learning; remove personal attention magnets that compete with teachers and peers.
3. Let Grow projects normalize small independence across a whole community.
4. Improve recess by increasing time, adding loose/natural materials, and reducing rules.
5. Test policies at the school level so culture and network effects are measured.
