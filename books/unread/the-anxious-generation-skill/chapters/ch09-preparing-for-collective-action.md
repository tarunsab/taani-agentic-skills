# Chapter 9: Preparing for Collective Action

**Part**: 4 — Collective Action for Healthier Childhood

## Core Idea

Haidt rejects the idea that the phone-based childhood is irreversible just because it has become normal. In 2010, families, schools, and companies lacked much of the evidence now available; once a product is shown to be dangerous, society can recall it, redesign it, or change the rules around it. The obstacle is a collective action problem: each person may prefer a healthier norm, but acting alone can impose social costs on a child or parent. The chapter identifies four response types—voluntary coordination, social norms and moralization, technology, and laws or institutional rules—that can change incentives and make the better choice easier. It also adds caveats: families and children differ, research can fail to replicate, implementation should be tested and adapted, and parenting is hard under conditions of incomplete knowledge. The practical posture is coordinated experimentation, not helplessness or a promise that one policy fits every setting.

## Collective Action Problems

A collective action problem occurs when individually rational choices create a bad group outcome, such as overfishing a shared pond. In a sixth-grade class, a few children with smartphones and social-media accounts can make everyone else feel excluded, so each family gives in even though all would be better off if no one had them yet. Parents face the same trap around independence: letting a child walk or play alone is harder when no other children do so and a neighbor might call the police. Technology companies face a competitive version because a platform that enforces its age rule can lose underage users to a rival that does not. The stable equilibrium can therefore be widely recognized as harmful while still being individually difficult to escape.

## Four Collective Responses

**Voluntary coordination** lets families make a credible commitment together; Wait Until 8th uses a pledge that becomes binding when enough families in a grade sign it. **Social norms and moralization** change what a community treats as normal or shameful, so reasonable childhood independence can become ordinary again rather than a sign of neglect. **Technological solutions** change the choice architecture through lockable pouches, better basic phones, or quick privacy-preserving age checks. **Laws and rules** change incentives and establish a floor, such as age verification, clarified neglect standards, or a school requirement that devices be stored all day. The four methods can reinforce one another: a law makes coordination easier, a school rule protects all students, and a norm makes the rule socially sustainable.

## A Few Caveats

Haidt says his recommendations are general principles, not a universal implementation manual. A child, family, school, or jurisdiction may need a different pace or a different design, and he urges readers to innovate and measure outcomes. He acknowledges that social-science studies sometimes disagree or fail to replicate and directs readers to the online supplement for corrections and new evidence. He also acknowledges the difficulty faced by parents, teachers, administrators, coaches, and adolescents themselves. The chapter's confidence is therefore in coordinated action and experimentation, not in pretending that uncertainty has disappeared.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Collective action problem / social dilemma**: Use when private incentives push people toward a group outcome they would not choose together.
- **Voluntary coordination**: Use pledges, cohorts, or shared commitments to remove the social penalty for a healthier choice.
- **Norm change**: Make the desired practice ordinary and the harmful equilibrium less socially inevitable.
- **Technology as coordination infrastructure**: Use devices and privacy-preserving defaults to change incentives at scale.
- **Rules and law**: Set enforceable floors when voluntary action cannot overcome competition or fear.

## Key Concepts

- **Collective action problem**: Individually rational choices that produce a collectively bad result.
- **Social dilemma**: Another name for the same coordination trap.
- **Voluntary coordination**: Group commitment that makes a private choice safer to sustain.
- **Social norm**: Shared expectation about what people normally or ought to do.
- **Moralization**: Treating a behavior as a matter of approval, condemnation, or duty.
- **Technology solution**: Product or feature that changes options and incentives for many people.
- **Institutional rule**: Policy imposed by a school or organization to coordinate behavior.
- **Equilibrium**: Stable pattern that persists even when participants dislike its consequences.

## Mental Models

- Ask “who bears the cost if I act alone?” before blaming a family for conformity.
- Match the response to the trap: pledge, norm, design, or law.
- Treat normality as an intervention target; visible coordination can change perceived exclusion.
- Build feedback loops: try a policy, measure outcomes, adapt, and share the results.

## Anti-patterns

- **Telling an isolated parent to simply be stronger**: It ignores the exclusion and enforcement costs created by the group.
- **Relying only on personal willpower against commercial competition**: Firms have incentives to recruit younger users and capture more attention.
- **Assuming a rule alone changes culture**: Schools and families also need shared norms and workable technology.
- **Treating uncertainty as a reason for inaction**: The source recommends targeted experiments and updates rather than paralysis.

## Worked Example

Alexis Spence explained that she wanted Instagram at 11 because missing posts meant being out of the loop and being mocked. Her parents' prohibition did not remove the peer network; it made her fear exclusion while competitors made underage access easy. If ten families in her grade sign a shared pledge, each child can remain without a smartphone while still having peers to play with, changing the payoff from “alone and excluded” to “part of a protected group.” Haidt notes that Wait Until 8th is useful coordination but still too early in his view, illustrating how a partial solution can improve a trap without fully solving it.

## Key Takeaways

1. Normality is not proof of safety; harmful equilibria can be stable because costs are individualized.
2. Coordinate before asking a child or parent to bear the social cost alone.
3. Use four levers together: voluntary commitments, norms, technology, and rules.
4. Preserve local variation and test outcomes; source-grounded confidence does not eliminate uncertainty.
