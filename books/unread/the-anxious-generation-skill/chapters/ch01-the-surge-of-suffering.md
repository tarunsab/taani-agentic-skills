# Chapter 1: The Surge of Suffering

**Part**: 1 — A Tidal Wave

## Core Idea

Haidt begins with parents who feel that smartphones, social media, and video games have swept their children away, then asks whether the stories correspond to a wider change. He identifies a sharp, synchronized rise in adolescent anxiety, depression, self-harm, and suicide beginning mainly in the early 2010s, with girls—especially preteen and younger teen girls—hit hardest. The pattern is concentrated in internalizing disorders and in Gen Z, while older cohorts and earlier internet users show much smaller changes. Self-reported symptoms are supported by behavioral indicators such as emergency treatment for self-harm and suicide rates, so greater willingness to report cannot explain the whole surge. Cross-national timing across the Anglosphere, Nordic nations, and many PISA regions makes the financial crisis, U.S. politics, and individual American tragedies insufficient as stand-alone explanations. Haidt argues that the most plausible common cause is the movement of adolescent social life from basic phones and in-person activity to smartphones, social media, and other always-on digital activities between roughly 2010 and 2015.

## The Wave Begins

There was little sign of an impending adolescent mental-illness crisis in the 2000s, but the pattern changed abruptly in the early 2010s. Haidt treats the timing and cohort concentration as a population-level puzzle: individual cases still have many causes, but the synchronized rise needs a common explanation.

### Major Depression Among Teens

The U.S. teen depression data show a “hockey-stick” increase beginning around 2012, with roughly a 150% relative increase from 2010 and a much larger absolute rise for girls. By 2020, before the full effect of COVID shutdowns was visible, about one in four American teen girls had experienced a major depressive episode in the prior year; the post-2020 increase was an added steepening, not the origin of the crisis. The relevant window is the Great Rewiring, not the claim that every individual case is caused by a phone.

## The Nature of the Surge

The rise is concentrated in internalizing disorders—anxiety, fear, sadness, hopelessness, rumination, and withdrawal—rather than only in outward conduct problems. Externalizing disorders turn distress outward through anger, violence, risk-taking, or conduct problems; both sexes have shown more internalizing and fewer externalizing symptoms since the early 2010s.

### Mental Illness Among College Students

College surveys show the same concentration: reported diagnoses of anxiety and depression rose more than other conditions during the 2010s. Haidt uses this age group as a second view of the cohort change, not as proof that campus life caused it. The college pattern supports the claim that the surge is principally about internalizing distress.

### What Is Anxiety?

Haidt distinguishes fear, an alarm response to an imminent or perceived threat, from anxiety, anticipation of future threat. Anxiety becomes pathological when the alarm is repeatedly activated by ordinary events rather than proportionate danger. It can become a body–thought feedback loop of tension, dread, rumination, catastrophizing, and further fear.

### Anxiety Prevalence by Age

Anxiety is the defining symptom family of the young-person surge, with depression following closely behind. Depression combines sustained low mood or hopelessness with loss of interest and is reinforced by social disconnection. These definitions connect later chapters' mechanisms—loneliness, social comparison, fragmented attention, and blocked experience—to the specific symptom profile.

## It’s Not Real, Is It?

Skeptics correctly note that self-report surveys can rise because young people become more willing to disclose symptoms or begin labeling normal discomfort as illness. Haidt therefore looks for indicators not generated solely by adolescents' descriptions.

### Emergency Room Visits for Self-Harm

Emergency treatment for self-harm nearly tripled for U.S. girls aged 10–14 between 2010 and 2020 and doubled for older teen girls, while women over 24 moved in the opposite direction. This behavioral indicator is not just a change in how willing adolescents are to describe distress. It also measures a different outcome from a survey checklist, which makes convergence more informative.

### Suicide Rates for Younger Adolescents

Suicide rates for 10–14-year-olds rose sharply, beginning around 2008 and surging in 2012. Suicide attempts and nonsuicidal self-injury are not identical, so the measures should not be collapsed into one statistic. Their movement alongside self-reported anxiety and depression nevertheless makes a real behavioral crisis difficult to dismiss, while leaving room for reporting changes and multiple causes.

## Smartphones and the Creation of Gen Z

Haidt separates two internet waves. Personal computers and dial-up or early broadband spread through the 1990s and 2000s without an equivalent collapse in teen mental health; the second wave paired smartphones with social media and reached a majority of homes around 2012–2013. Basic phones supported direct calls and cumbersome one-to-one texting, whereas smartphones offered 24/7 internet access, millions of apps, push notifications, and feeds that could follow a child everywhere.

### Communication Technology Adoption

U.S. teen smartphone ownership rose from 23% in 2011 to 79% by 2016, while “almost constantly online” reports grew from about one in four teens in 2015 to nearly half by 2022. Front-facing cameras, Instagram's smartphone-only launch, Facebook's 2012 purchase of Instagram, and beauty filters made self-presentation and comparison especially intense for girls. Boys moved more deeply into online multiplayer games, YouTube, Reddit, pornography, and other virtual activities, so the same device ecology produced different pathways.

## Aren’t They Right to Be Anxious and Depressed?

Haidt tests explanations based on wars, terrorism, economic insecurity, climate change, school shootings, and political polarization. Collective threats can be legitimate sources of concern, and phones can amplify them through emotionally contagious feeds, but none of these explanations alone predicts the same age, sex, and international inflection.

### Teenage Depression vs. Adult Unemployment

The Great Recession peaked before the mental-health rise and unemployment then fell while teen depression continued upward. This timing does not fit a simple economic-causation account. Haidt argues that isolation and uselessness are more depressive than danger faced together, so the economic story is at most one contributor rather than the common mechanism.

## All Over the Anglosphere and the Rest of the World

Canada, the U.K., Australia, Ireland, and New Zealand show similar early-2010s declines in adolescent mental health despite different levels of violence, economic insecurity, and national politics. The international pattern does not prove every mechanism, but it constrains explanations: a common technology shift fits the timing better than a country-specific event.

### Excellent or Very Good Mental Health, Canadian Women

Canadian surveys show a marked decline in the share of young women describing their mental health as excellent or very good. The direction and timing match the U.S. pattern, even though the Canadian policy and political setting is different.

### Self-Harm Episodes, U.K. Teens

U.K. behavioral data also show rising psychiatric self-harm contacts among adolescents. That parallel matters because it is not merely another national self-report series.

### Mental Health Hospitalizations, Australia

Australian mental-health hospitalization data rise for young people in the same broad period. Haidt uses the result as cross-national behavioral corroboration, not as proof that every hospitalization has the same cause.

### The Rest of the World

The pattern extends beyond the Anglosphere, although it is not uniform. The broad comparison is useful precisely because it reveals both a common Western shift and important regional variation.

### High Psychological Distress, Nordic Nations

Nordic countries show the same 2010–2015 increase in high psychological distress. Their relatively different welfare states and national politics make a single American event a poor explanation.

### Alienation in School, Worldwide

In the 37-country PISA comparison, school loneliness and outsider feelings stayed fairly flat until after 2012 and then rose across Western regions, with Asia as the main exception in the displayed data. This school-belonging measure links the mental-health surge to a social experience rather than to diagnosis alone.

## In Sum

The chapter's evidence converges on a synchronized, cohort-specific rise in internalizing distress beginning in the early 2010s. Self-reports are supported by emergency treatment for self-harm, suicide mortality, cross-national behavioral data, and school alienation. The Great Rewiring fits the timing better than the Great Recession, a single political event, or a simple rise in willingness to disclose symptoms. The evidence is population-level and compatible with multiple causes for any individual young person.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Great Rewiring window**: Use 2010–2015 as the population-level period in which smartphones, social media, and always-on access changed development.
- **Internalizing versus externalizing**: Use to classify whether distress is turned inward (anxiety/depression) or outward (anger/risk/violence), while remembering both can coexist.
- **Triangulation of evidence**: Compare self-reports with behavioral indicators and cohort/cross-national timing before inferring a mental-health trend.
- **Opportunity-cost explanation**: Ask what the phone displaces—friends, sleep, exercise, and real-world participation—rather than treating use as isolated exposure.

## Key Concepts

- **Major depressive episode**: A symptom-defined period of depressed mood or lost interest, with associated cognitive and physical symptoms.
- **Anxiety disorder**: Excessive anticipation of threat in which the alarm system is repeatedly activated by ordinary events.
- **Internalizing disorder**: Distress primarily experienced inwardly through anxiety, fear, sadness, rumination, or withdrawal.
- **Externalizing disorder**: Distress expressed outwardly through conduct problems, anger, violence, or risk-taking.
- **Nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI)**: Deliberate self-harm without intent to die, often used as a coping behavior.
- **Gen Z**: The cohort born after roughly 1995 whose puberty overlapped with the smartphone/social-media wave.
- **Great Rewiring**: Haidt's name for the rapid social and developmental transition from 2010 to 2015.
- **“Almost constantly” online**: A survey marker of continuous mental availability and attention to the virtual world, not just active screen minutes.

## Mental Models

- Use cross-national synchrony as a constraint on causal stories.
- Separate “the data show a population shift” from “this explains every individual's illness.”
- Treat timing, cohort, sex differences, and behavioral outcomes as a four-part diagnostic pattern.
- Ask whether a proposed cause predicts the observed age and sex gradients.

## Anti-patterns

- **Dismissing the crisis as self-report inflation**: Behavioral changes in self-harm and suicide move in the same direction.
- **Blaming one headline event**: National tragedies and economic cycles do not explain the same inflection across many countries.
- **Equating the first internet wave with the smartphone wave**: A home computer with limited access is not an always-on pocket portal.
- **Treating correlation as individual proof**: Haidt's claim is population-level and is developed with stronger causal evidence in later chapters.

## Worked Example

Two family stories illustrate the pattern. Emily's parents repeatedly tried to restrict Instagram; during a six-week phone-free camp and a later two-month period with a flip phone, her mother reported that Emily returned to her usual self, while smartphone access was followed by agitation and glumness. James initially benefited from a PlayStation during COVID school closures, but prolonged Fortnite use was followed by anger, depression, withdrawal, and conflict when the devices were removed. These accounts do not establish causality on their own, and James's social isolation complicates the decision to cut access. They do show why parents experience the issue as both a mental-health question and a collective-action trap.

## Key Takeaways

1. The adolescent mental-health surge is unusually synchronized, cohort-specific, and concentrated in anxiety/depression.
2. Self-report changes are not the only evidence; self-harm treatment and suicide rates also moved sharply.
3. The timing fits the smartphone/social-media wave better than the Great Recession or a single U.S. event.
4. Girls were hit hardest and earliest, while boys followed a more diffuse path that needs separate analysis.
5. Later chapters must explain how a phone-based childhood interferes with development, not merely show that use and distress correlate.
