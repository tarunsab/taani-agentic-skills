# Chapter 10: What Governments and Tech Companies Can Do Now

**Part**: 4 — Collective Action for Healthier Childhood

## Core Idea

Haidt argues that many technology companies are locked in a race to extract attention because advertisers, not users, are the paying customers. The same design choices that make platforms engaging—algorithmic feeds, infinite scroll, push notifications, streaks, public metrics, and weak age enforcement—also exploit developmental vulnerabilities and crowd out real-world life. Government policy can change this incentive structure by imposing a duty of care for minors, raising the age of internet adulthood to 16, supporting privacy-preserving age verification, and funding phone-free schools. It can also reverse offline overprotection by narrowing vague neglect laws, supporting free play, designing child-accessible public spaces, and expanding vocational and youth-development pathways. Haidt favors design regulation that is viewpoint-neutral and focused on incentives rather than government control of speech. The chapter is a menu of coordination infrastructure: make child-protective choices easier for parents, safer for platforms, and more normal for communities.

## The Race to the Bottom of the Brain Stem

Tristan Harris's phrase names the competitive logic by which companies move from simple attention capture to deeper manipulation of identity and social validation. Advertising-funded platforms need more users, more time per user, and more user-generated content; failure to exploit a psychological weakness can leave a company at a market disadvantage. Age enforcement is weak because younger users form durable habits and are valuable future customers, while algorithmic feeds learn quickly which content keeps a person scrolling. Autoplay, infinite scroll, loot boxes, free-to-play models, streaks, and default-public settings reduce stopping cues and increase social labor. Haidt's target is not every piece of user content but the company-controlled design and business incentives that amplify it.

## What Governments and Tech Companies Can Do to End the Race

### 1. Assert a Duty of Care

The U.K.'s Age Appropriate Design Code is Haidt's model for treating minors as a distinct responsibility class. It defines children as under 18 and requires services to act in their best interests, use high privacy defaults, hide geolocation by default, and explain policies and controls clearly. He argues that design rules can be viewpoint-neutral because they regulate defaults and targeting rather than the political content users post. California and other jurisdictions adopted related approaches, while Haidt prefers national action because the internet does not respect state borders. The principle is to make the platform bear responsibility for predictable design harms instead of placing all policing on parents.

### 2. Raise the Age of Internet Adulthood to 16

COPPA's age 13 was a late-1990s compromise about data collection, not a finding about adolescent maturity or self-control. It also required action only when a company had direct evidence that a user was under 13, which enabled widespread age misrepresentation. Haidt proposes 16 as a more realistic legal threshold for opening accounts, uploading content, receiving customized feeds, and accepting terms that allow data collection; he does not mean that all online content should be blocked until 16 or that 16-year-olds are adults. The age is chosen as a compromise beyond the most vulnerable puberty years while retaining Age Appropriate Design protections for minors under 18. The argument is about enforceable account adulthood, not ordinary web browsing, educational use, or every form of online communication.

### 3. Facilitate Age Verification

Haidt rejects a universal mandate that every user hand a social platform a government ID because databases can be hacked and anonymity has value. He describes a menu of alternatives: a trusted verifier can report only an old-enough/not-old-enough result, people can vouch for one another, a verified person can receive a privacy-preserving token, or biometrics can be used by a service that already verified the user. None is perfect, so his most parent-friendly proposal is device-based age check. Apple, Google, and Microsoft could let a parent's minor device account signal to a site that the user is underage, after which the site asks only whether the person meets its minimum age; the parent could turn the feature off. This shares responsibility across operating systems, parents, and platforms without requiring adults to disclose their identity or asking parents to know every harmful site.

### 4. Encourage Phone-Free Schools

Governments can fund phone lockers or lockable pouches and support school-level research on mental health and achievement. This policy removes devices from students' pockets and hands rather than relying on teachers to police hidden use during class. It is a practical bridge between online design and offline development because it protects attention, socializing, and learning for all students at once. The next chapter develops the school implementation in detail.

## What Governments Can Do to Incentivize More (and Better) Real-World Experience

### 1. Stop Punishing Parents for Giving Children Real-World Freedom

Haidt uses Debra Harrell's arrest after her nine-year-old played at a supervised sprinkler park while her mother worked to show how vague neglect laws can criminalize ordinary independence. Reasonable Childhood Independence laws define neglect as blatantly, willfully, or recklessly disregarding an obvious danger, not simply taking one's eyes off a child. Such laws protect parents who grant age-appropriate autonomy for developmental or economic reasons while preserving intervention for real abuse and serious danger. The cited states show bipartisan support, but many jurisdictions still leave authorities broad discretion. Government's role is to stop treating reasonable freedom itself as evidence of neglect.

### 2. Encourage More Play in Schools

State education departments can recognize that recess and free play support cognitive performance, social development, and mental health rather than stealing from academic achievement. Haidt recommends more play before and after school and continuation through middle school, with some opportunity even in high school. The aim is not a tightly scripted activity but child-directed play that builds autonomy and self-governance. The evidence and implementation are developed in chapter 11.

### 3. Design and Zone Public Space with Children in Mind

Sidewalks, crosswalks, traffic calming, mixed-use neighborhoods, nearby playgrounds, School Streets, and Play Streets can make independent access possible. The chapter cites a finding that children who can reach a playground by walking or biking are six times more likely to visit it than children who need an adult to drive them. A public realm organized around parent taxis makes screen-based retreat more likely, while accessible mixed-use space lets children be visible, social, mobile, and responsible. The design target is not a risk-free environment but a legible, walkable environment where children can participate.

### 4. More Vocational Education, Apprenticeships, and Youth Development Programs

Career and technical education offers hands-on pathways in shop, mechanics, agriculture, business, and other fields, and the cited research finds particular graduation and earnings benefits for boys in specialized schools. Apprenticeships reduce the employer's risk by subsidizing training, helping young people move from school into paid work. Gap years, years of service, and wilderness programs can build skills, responsibility, self-reliance, and community contribution for young people without a clear college path. These programs address both the real-world competence gap and boys' search for agency and purpose.

## In Sum

The chapter treats platform design and offline policy as one coordination problem. Governments can make child-protective defaults enforceable, while schools, public spaces, vocational programs, and independence laws make real-world experience possible. Age 16 is proposed as an enforceable internet-account threshold, not as a ban on browsing or a claim that sixteen-year-olds are fully adult. The central policy test is whether a rule distributes responsibility, protects privacy, and restores the experiences that connected products displace.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Race to the bottom of the brain stem**: Use to identify competitive attention extraction that will not be solved by voluntary restraint alone.
- **Duty of care / Age Appropriate Design Code**: Regulate child-specific defaults, privacy, geolocation, transparency, and design incentives.
- **Internet adulthood at 16**: Separate account/contract adulthood from browsing, education, and legal adulthood at 18.
- **Privacy-preserving age verification**: Prefer yes/no or device-based age signals over universal ID disclosure.
- **Reasonable Childhood Independence law**: Define neglect around reckless danger, not around the absence of constant supervision.
- **Real-world infrastructure**: Use school policy, public-space design, and vocational pathways to make independence practicable.

## Key Concepts

- **Duty of care**: Responsibility to design services in minors' best interests.
- **Age Appropriate Design Code (AADC)**: U.K. framework for child-protective defaults and transparency.
- **Internet adulthood**: Threshold for opening accounts and giving data under terms of service.
- **Age verification**: System that establishes whether a user meets a minimum age.
- **Device-based age check**: Parent-controlled minor-device signal used to enforce age gates.
- **Race to the bottom**: Competition that rewards ever deeper exploitation of psychological vulnerabilities.
- **Reasonable Childhood Independence laws**: Laws narrowing neglect to blatant, willful, or reckless danger.
- **Career and technical education (CTE)**: Hands-on education linked to skilled work.
- **School Street / Play Street**: Temporary traffic closures that support child mobility and play.

## Mental Models

- Regulate design incentives when content moderation would threaten speech or miss the mechanism.
- Distribute responsibility: operating systems, platforms, parents, schools, and governments each control a different lever.
- Pair online guardrails with offline affordances; a child cannot play independently in a place they cannot reach.
- Ask whether a policy creates an age gate, a stopping cue, a safe default, or a real-world alternative.

## Anti-patterns

- **Expecting platforms to self-regulate under a younger-user growth race**: The competitive incentive rewards weak enforcement.
- **Mandating government ID as the only age check**: It creates privacy and security risks and can drive users away without solving all cases.
- **Regulating political viewpoints instead of controllable design**: Design defaults can be viewpoint-neutral.
- **Criminalizing any unsupervised childhood**: It teaches parents to overprotect and pushes children toward screens.
- **Treating academic time as the only productive time**: Play, CTE, and apprenticeships can build competencies school drills do not.

## Worked Example

Haidt's device-based “age check” lets a parent mark a minor's phone or computer through the operating system. When a child attempts to open an account, the platform asks the device whether age check is on and whether the user meets the platform's threshold, such as 16 for social media or 18 for pornography. The site receives a gate result rather than a government ID or browsing history; adults are unaffected, and parents who choose otherwise can disable the setting. The example shows a coordination technology that reduces underage access and peer pressure while preserving adult anonymity.

## Key Takeaways

1. Advertising incentives create a race that makes voluntary child protection unreliable.
2. Regulate design, defaults, and age gates rather than trying to police every viewpoint or post.
3. Raise enforceable internet account adulthood to 16 while keeping minors protected through 18.
4. Use privacy-preserving, shared age verification instead of putting all responsibility on parents.
5. Make real-world independence legal, walkable, playable, and connected to work and mentorship.
