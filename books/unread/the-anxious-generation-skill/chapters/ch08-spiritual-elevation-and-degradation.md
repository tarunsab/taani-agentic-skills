# Chapter 8: Spiritual Elevation and Degradation

**Part**: 3 — The Great Rewiring: The Rise of the Phone-Based Childhood

## Core Idea

Haidt shifts from social-scientific diagnosis to a broader account of what phone-based life does to consciousness, relationships, and meaning for adults as well as children. He uses “spirituality” in a functional sense: people move upward toward sacredness, moral beauty, connection, and self-transcendence, or downward toward disgust, pettiness, ego, and degradation. The phone-based environment tends to pull downward because it is disembodied, asynchronous, self-focused, constantly stimulating, and organized around rapid judgment. Haidt draws on Durkheim, psychology, Buddhism, Taoism, Christianity, and other traditions to identify six practices that counteract the pull: shared sacredness, embodiment, stillness/silence/focus, self-transcendence, slow anger and quick forgiveness, and awe in nature. These practices are not offered as proof of a particular theology; they are ways of structuring attention, bodies, time, and community that research and tradition associate with well-being and cohesion. The practical lesson is to deliberately choose inputs and settings that restore presence, ritual, meaning, and connection rather than filling every moment with algorithmic content.

## The Three Dimensions of Social Space

Haidt's model places ordinary social life in three dimensions. The horizontal axis distinguishes closeness and distance; the vertical axis of hierarchy tracks rank, status, and deference. A third “divinity” axis distinguishes what feels sacred, pure, morally elevating, and upward from what feels disgusting, impure, petty, or downward. Moral elevation can make people want to imitate admirable action, while degradation turns attention away or pulls behavior toward contempt. Haidt uses this axis to name a cost of phone use that clinical symptom counts miss: even a person who is not depressed may become scattered, judgmental, self-focused, and less capable of sustained moral attention.

## Spiritual Practices

David DeSteno's review, as presented by Haidt, finds that prayer, meditation, confession, and atonement rituals can improve well-being even when researchers do not claim that prayer changes external events. The mechanism often reduces self-focus and opens a person to something beyond the individual self. When practiced collectively, especially with synchronized movement, these activities increase trust and cohesion and reduce anomie and loneliness. Haidt's six practices are therefore portable across religious and secular settings, though he notes that nonreligious people may need to build them intentionally because they do not receive them automatically through a faith community.

## 1. Shared Sacredness

Durkheim's distinction between the profane and the sacred describes a movement between self-focused daily life and collective experiences in which shared meaning temporarily predominates. Religious services, pilgrimages, civil-rights marches, sports rituals, singing, and synchronized celebration can generate collective effervescence, a charged sense of belonging. Strong communities are not produced by communication alone; they are strengthened when people enter a meaningful time and place together and return with more trust. Virtual networks rarely provide the same consensual structure because feeds never close, schedules are individualized, and everything is available all the time. A family can recreate some structure through shared holidays, regular service, a moral or charitable group, or a weekly digital Sabbath.

## 2. Embodiment

Rituals use bodies in proximity and often in synchronous motion: kneeling, bowing, chanting, rocking, singing, dancing, and eating together. Experimental work cited by Haidt links synchronized movement to more trust, cooperation, and perceived similarity. Shared meals matter because breaking bread in the same place and time binds people in a way no virtual simulation fully reproduces. Zoom weddings, funerals, and services during COVID made the loss of physical co-presence visible even when communication continued. Seek live, communal, body-involving activities—religious or secular, including sport or music—when the goal is belonging rather than mere information transfer.

## 3. Stillness, Silence, and Focus

Meditation traditions train the “jumping monkey” of ordinary consciousness toward samadhi, or mental unity. Studies of meditation and monastic practice cited in the chapter associate sustained practice with lower fear and negative emotionality, while even brief daily mindfulness can reduce irritability and stress. The phone-based life destroys the levee that protects attention by flooding consciousness with alerts, sounds, feeds, and the expectation that every pause should be filled. Stillness can be practiced through meditation, silent retreats, quiet walks, nature, or simply not adding audio and video to every journey. The practice is not passive disengagement; it is training the capacity to remain present with oneself and others.

## 4. Transcending the Self

Self-transcendence is a reduction in the self-monitoring perspective that asks what I want, what others think of me, and how I am performing. Haidt connects this to the default mode network, a set of brain structures more active during egocentric processing and less active during meditation, prayer, awe, and some other spiritual experiences. Social media is almost the inverse: the user stands on a platform to manage a public self, track likes and followers, and seek status. Taoist warnings about glory, gain, superiority, and right-versus-wrong therefore map closely onto the platform's incentives. Reduce self-branding inputs and create conditions for prayer, meditation, service, art, or awe that shift attention toward a larger whole.

## 5. Be Slow to Anger, Quick to Forgive

Haidt connects moral traditions' warnings about judgment with his moral-psychology principle that intuitions come first and strategic reasoning second. People often feel a fast moral reaction and then invent a justification, while judging others more harshly than themselves. Social media accelerates this sequence into public condemnation with little context, no bodily cue of the target's humanity, and strong pressure to display loyalty by joining the pile-on. Religious and philosophical traditions do not require abandoning moral distinctions; they recommend noticing hypocrisy, delaying anger, and preserving the possibility of forgiveness and relationship repair. A useful counter-practice is to pause before amplifying a judgment and ask whether the same standard, context, and grace would apply to oneself.

## 6. Find Awe in Nature

Awe arises when something is perceived as vast and difficult to fit into existing mental structures, producing a pleasurable sense of smallness and openness. Nature is one of the most reliable triggers because humans evolved in living environments and have a biophilic impulse to affiliate with other forms of life. Haidt's students' phone-free awe walks through New York parks produced reports of greater presence, generosity, calm, connection, and reduced anxiety. A screen can show beautiful places or moral acts, but quantity, speed, and lack of effort reduce the depth of the experience compared with being physically present. Haidt recommends walking without AirPods or a phone in natural settings and deliberately bringing children to beautiful places without turning the experience into content.

## The God-Shaped Hole

Haidt uses Pascal's phrase as a metaphor for a human hunger for meaning, connection, and elevation; he notes that religious believers and secular accounts disagree about its origin. His common implication is that the empty space will be filled by something, and mass media—especially algorithmic feeds—can pump it full of trivial or degrading material. The content entering attention therefore matters even when it does not produce a clinical disorder. Ancient advice that thought shapes the mind becomes a modern rule for controlling notifications, feeds, and the people or images one repeatedly watches. The remedy is not to reject all technology but to restore deliberate boundaries and higher-quality experiences.

## In Sum

Phone-based life can degrade the conditions that support sacredness, embodiment, stillness, self-transcendence, forgiveness, and awe. These practices are not limited to one religion; they are recurring ways of structuring bodies, time, attention, and community. The chapter extends the mental-health analysis to meaning and moral consciousness, while acknowledging that its spiritual language is interpretive rather than a clinical diagnosis. Deliberate rituals, quiet, shared meals, service, and nature are practical counterweights to an always-on feed.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Divinity axis**: Use to ask whether an activity elevates toward moral beauty and connection or degrades toward ego, contempt, and fragmentation.
- **Profane versus sacred**: Structure time, space, objects, and rituals so a group can move together beyond ordinary self-interest.
- **Six spiritual practices**: Shared sacredness; embodiment; stillness/silence/focus; self-transcendence; slow anger/quick forgiveness; awe in nature.
- **Default mode network**: Treat self-branding and social comparison as inputs that sustain egocentric processing.
- **Awe trigger**: Seek vastness plus a need to revise one's mental structures, especially in nature and collective experiences.

## Key Concepts

- **Spiritual degradation**: Downward movement toward ego, judgment, triviality, and moral or attentional fragmentation.
- **Moral elevation**: Uplifted feeling produced by witnessing admirable action and wanting to imitate it.
- **Profane**: Ordinary, self-focused daily consciousness.
- **Sacred**: Collective, meaningful realm entered through ritual and shared attention.
- **Collective effervescence**: Energized communion generated by shared ritual.
- **Self-transcendence**: Reduced self-focus and connection to something larger.
- **Default mode network (DMN)**: Brain network associated here with egocentric, self-referential processing.
- **Samadhi**: Meditative absorption or mental unity.
- **Awe**: Emotion triggered by perceived vastness and a need to update mental structures.
- **Biophilia**: Human urge to affiliate with living systems and nature.

## Mental Models

- Use the sacred/profane test to decide whether a digital habit structures or dissolves meaningful time.
- Ask whether a practice uses bodies, synchrony, shared attention, and durable community.
- Treat self-branding as a default-mode amplifier and awe, meditation, and service as counterweights.
- Replace “more content” with “more presence” when evaluating spiritual or emotional benefit.

## Anti-patterns

- **Filling every pause**: Constant audio, alerts, and feeds prevent stillness and unified attention.
- **Confusing virtual access with communion**: Seeing a ritual or landscape on a screen is not equivalent to embodied participation.
- **Publicly judging before understanding**: Social-media speed and audience pressure amplify moral intuition without context or forgiveness.
- **Turning awe into content immediately**: Recording can displace the self-forgetting attention that makes awe transformative.

## Worked Example

Haidt assigns NYU students a slow walk outdoors without phones. Yi-Mei first notices spring beauty in Washington Square Park, then continues to Central Park, where light on a lake makes the familiar world feel newly alive. She reports that the experience reduced petty worry, increased calm and generosity, and made her want to connect with people. The example combines nature, stillness, embodiment, and self-transcendence; its lesson is not that every walk cures anxiety, but that deliberate removal of the phone creates room for an experience a feed cannot fully supply.

## Key Takeaways

1. Phone-based life can degrade attention and moral consciousness even when it does not produce a diagnosis.
2. Shared ritual, bodies in motion, silence, self-transcendence, forgiveness, and nature counteract the platform's incentives.
3. Sacredness requires structured time, space, and collective participation; infinite feeds flatten all three.
4. Social media sustains self-focus, rapid judgment, and audience capture, while awe can reduce self-focus and increase meaning.
5. Deliberately control inputs; the mind takes on the color of what it repeatedly attends to.
