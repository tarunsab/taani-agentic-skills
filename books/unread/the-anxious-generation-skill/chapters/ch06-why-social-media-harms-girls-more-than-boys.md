# Chapter 6: Why Social Media Harms Girls More Than Boys

**Part**: 3 — The Great Rewiring: The Rise of the Phone-Based Childhood

## Core Idea

Haidt uses Alexis Spence's path from an underage Instagram account to depression, anorexia, and repeated hospitalization as a concrete case of a platform becoming a developmental environment. He argues that social media harms girls more than boys because girls use visually oriented platforms more, have stronger average communion motives, and are more exposed to comparison, relational aggression, emotional contagion, and sexual predation. Correlational studies show a larger and clearer dose–response relationship for girls, while experiments and natural experiments support the claim that social media is a cause rather than only a correlate. He adds an important group-level argument: social media changes the culture of a whole school, including students who do not use it, so individual effect sizes can understate the collective impact. The chapter is not a claim that every girl is harmed or that every increase in gender dysphoria or mental illness is socially induced; it distinguishes genuine disclosure and support from possible sociogenic amplification. The practical focus is to protect puberty, favor a few close embodied friendships, and treat image-based public platforms as a different risk class from private communication or the broader internet.

## The Evidence That Social Media Harms Girls

Studies that merge all digital activity and all teens can produce very small average associations, but Haidt and Jean Twenge report larger effects when the exposure is specifically social media and the analysis separates girls. In the British Millennium Cohort Study, girls' depression probability rose with each increase in weekday social-media use; girls reporting five or more hours per weekday were about three times as likely to be depressed as nonusers. Boys showed a weaker curve that rose mainly at heavy use. Haidt warns that effect estimates depend on the measure and subgroup, which is why “digital media” is too blunt a category for this question.

### Depression by Level of Social Media Use, U.K.

The U.K. figure makes the dose–response pattern visible: as weekday social-media use rises, girls' reported depression rises more sharply than boys'. The association is not a diagnosis for any one user and does not prove that every hour has the same effect. It is one piece of a larger case that becomes more persuasive when combined with experiments, rollout studies, and the mechanisms described below.

## Is Social Media a Cause or Just a Correlate?

Reverse causation, genetics, permissive parenting, and loneliness could make depressed young people seek social media, so correlations alone are insufficient. Randomized controlled trials that reduce social-media use report improvements in loneliness and depression, while experiments exposing teen girls to manipulated Instagram selfies lower body image. Quasi-experiments add group-level support: when Facebook rolled out across colleges, mental health and healthcare use worsened, especially for women; when high-speed internet arrived in Spanish regions, girls' mental-health hospital discharges increased while sleep, homework, and family socializing declined. Haidt's synthesis is that social media is a causal contributor, not merely a marker of prior distress, while acknowledging that no single experiment reproduces the entire childhood ecology.

## Does Social Media Affect Groups as Well as Individuals?

Individual experiments treat social media like sugar consumed by separate people, but a network changes the behavior of everyone inside it. When smartphones entered schools, students spent less time talking, laughing, and making eye contact between classes, at lunch, and at recess; even students who abstained were surrounded by a less social culture. An abstainer can therefore become more isolated while friends remain online, which can suppress the measured benefit of abstention. College rollout studies and high-speed-internet variation suggest that group effects may be larger than the individual-level effects. The strongest tests would randomize whole schools or communities to phone storage or reduced social-media access and measure changes in culture as well as symptoms.

## Girls Use Social Media More Than Boys

Boys moved toward YouTube, Reddit, and online multiplayer games, while girls became heavier users of Instagram, Snapchat, Pinterest, Tumblr, and other visually oriented platforms. In a cited U.K. well-being survey, Instagram was rated worst among several major apps, Snapchat next, while YouTube received the only positive overall rating. By 2015, roughly one in seven American girls reported 40 or more hours per week on social media, a level that was nearly impossible before smartphones made the internet portable. Higher exposure plus platforms that focus on faces, bodies, lifestyle, and public evaluation makes a larger average effect unsurprising even before considering psychological differences.

## Agency and Communion

Agency is the motive to individuate, expand the self, and demonstrate competence or assertiveness; communion is the motive to integrate into a larger social unit through care, cooperation, and empathy. Everyone has both motives, and identity formation requires weaving them together. Across cultures and childhood play, boys show somewhat more agency-oriented interests and girls somewhat more communion-oriented interests, though the difference is neither absolute nor fixed. Social media markets itself as connection and belonging, so it strongly appeals to communion, but its public comparison, unstable status, and shallow ties often frustrate the very need it promises to satisfy.

## Four Reasons Why Girls Are Particularly Vulnerable

Haidt organizes the sex difference around four overlapping pathways rather than a single female “sensitivity.” Girls, on average, use image-heavy social platforms more and are more affected by comparison, relational status, emotional sharing, and sexual harassment. The pathways interact: an image-based status threat can become a group-chat conflict, receive emotional reinforcement, and expose a girl to predation. The argument is probabilistic and population-level, not a rule about every girl.

## Reason #1: Girls Are More Affected by Visual Social Comparison and Perfectionism

Haidt uses the “sociometer” as a model of an internal gauge that monitors social standing and produces anxiety when status appears to fall. Puberty makes appearance and acceptance especially salient, while filters, editing tools, and carefully selected images make girls compare themselves with peers who seem unrealistically beautiful and successful. Girls are also more likely to experience socially prescribed perfectionism—the belief that other people or society demand impossibly high standards—which rises with anxiety and fear of public failure. Even images shown too quickly for conscious awareness can shift body evaluation, so knowing intellectually that feeds are edited does not fully neutralize the comparison machinery. Algorithms can then amplify interest in beauty or weight loss into extreme content; Haidt cites fake 13-year-old accounts receiving tens of thousands of weight-loss videos and describes Instagram's own internal research as identifying social comparison as a particular problem.

### Satisfied with Oneself

The chapter's body-image evidence uses satisfaction with oneself as a concrete outcome of comparison. Exposure to idealized or manipulated images can lower how young women evaluate their own appearance, even when they know the images are curated. Haidt presents this as one route from a platform feature to a change in self-perception, not as proof that every user will develop an eating disorder.

## Reason #2: Girls’ Aggression Is More Relational

Boys are more likely to express aggression physically, while girls in late childhood and adolescence more often harm relationships, reputations, and belonging. Social platforms make gossip, exclusion, anonymous hate pages, subtweets, public tags, and group-chat attacks easy to scale and difficult to escape. The chapter cites evidence that face-to-face bullying declined or stayed flat for girls while cyberbullying rose, and that roughly one in five high-school girls experienced cyberbullying each year in a major U.S. survey. A case in which a 13-year-old was placed in a group chat named for excluding her and then experienced panic attacks shows how a local status conflict becomes continuous, public, and portable. Because adolescence makes “social death” feel especially threatening, relational aggression pushes girls toward defend mode and can intensify anxiety, depression, and suicidal thinking.

## Reason #3: Girls More Easily Share Emotions and Disorders

Emotions travel through networks, and the Framingham studies cited by Haidt found that depression spread from women to their close friends with a large effect while depression in men had no measurable network effect in that dataset. Haidt connects this difference to girls' greater emotional expressiveness and the amount of talking within friendships. He then discusses sociogenic illness—symptoms generated or amplified by social forces—whose historical anxiety and motor variants disproportionately affect adolescent girls. Social media adds prestige bias, conformity, and audience capture: extreme presentations can receive more attention, become models, and spread through a network. The chapter describes social-media-associated outbreaks of tic-like behaviors and rising self-identification with dissociative identity disorder, and treats gender dysphoria as a more complex case in which genuine recognition and reduced stigma may coexist with social clustering and influence; these examples are possibilities in Haidt's argument, not universal diagnoses.

## Reason #4: Girls Are More Subject to Predation and Harassment

Haidt argues that online sexual predation and exploitation are a larger part of girls' adolescent experience, even though boys can also be targeted. Adult men can approach underage girls through platforms that do little age gating, while boys at school may pressure girls to send nude images and use the images as status currency or blackmail. The harms are asymmetric: sharing a girl's nude can trigger shame and cyberbullying, whereas boys' nude images are more often treated as humorous or used to solicit reciprocity. The combination of public self-presentation, high communion needs, and weak platform safeguards keeps many girls in defend mode. This mechanism is not an argument against girls' sexuality or online expression; it is an argument for age separation, privacy, and accountability.

## Quantity over Quality

When every acquaintance, follower, streak, and transient tie requires attention, the number of relationships expands while time and emotional investment per relationship shrink. Freddie deBoer's formulation, adopted by Haidt, is that digital substitutions can superficially satisfy the drive to connect while failing to provide the reinvigorating power of actual connection. The percentage of students reporting a few friends to get together with declined, while loneliness rose for both sexes and more sharply around 2012 for girls. Lisa Damour's rule is “quality over quantity”: a few strong, supportive friendships protect more than a vast sea of fair-weather connections. The collective irony is that a platform promising communion can weaken the real-world conditions that make communion satisfying, including for nonusers living in the same school culture.

### Have a Few Close Friends

Haidt uses the decline in students reporting a few friends to get together with as a counterpoint to rising follower counts. Close friends provide recurring, embodied contact and enough mutual investment to repair mistakes. A large contact list is not equivalent to that kind of friendship, so the chapter's practical standard is to protect a few durable bonds.

### Often Feel Lonely

Loneliness rose for both sexes, with a sharper inflection for girls around the Great Rewiring. The result matters because it shows that more communication can coexist with less belonging. Loneliness is also a feedback loop: disconnection can drive more online seeking, while heavy online use can reduce the real-world encounters that would relieve it.

## In Sum

Girls' pathway through the phone-based childhood is shaped by both exposure and mechanism. Image-based comparison, relational aggression, emotional contagion, and predation are distinct risks, while communion needs can make a platform's promise especially compelling. The evidence supports a causal contribution and possible group-level effects, but it does not erase individual differences or turn every disclosure into social contagion. Protecting a few close friendships and delaying public, algorithmic socialization are the practical implications.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Sociometer**: Use when appearance, likes, and social ranking may be converting comparison into anxiety.
- **Agency and communion**: Identify whether a product is appealing to status/competence or belonging/care, and whether it actually satisfies that motive.
- **Social-media causal triad**: Combine dose–response correlations, experiments, and rollout/natural experiments.
- **Group-level effects**: Evaluate how network adoption changes culture for users and abstainers.
- **Sociogenic transmission / audience capture**: Check whether emotional states or symptoms spread through social learning and reward for extremity.
- **Quality-over-quantity friendship rule**: Protect a few durable, supportive relationships over a large number of low-investment ties.

## Key Concepts

- **Visual social comparison**: Comparing one's face, body, or life with curated images.
- **Socially prescribed perfectionism**: Belief that others demand unrealistically high performance or appearance.
- **Relational aggression**: Harming social ties or reputation rather than primarily using physical force.
- **Cyberbullying**: Networked harassment that follows a child across school, home, and sleep.
- **Emotional contagion**: Transmission of affect through social interaction.
- **Sociogenic illness**: Symptoms or disorders generated or amplified by social influence.
- **Audience capture**: An audience trains a creator toward more extreme content that earns attention.
- **Predation**: Sexual exploitation or harassment facilitated by online access to minors.
- **Communion**: Motive to belong, care, cooperate, and connect.

## Mental Models

- Analyze product effect by subgroup, platform, age, and intensity; averages can hide the mechanism.
- Separate individual effects from network effects before judging a small correlation as unimportant.
- Treat puberty as a status-sensitive period in which visual comparison and audience judgment carry extra weight.
- Ask whether more connections are increasing secure intimacy or merely increasing social labor.

## Anti-patterns

- **Merging all digital use in one variable**: TV, search, private messaging, games, and image-based feeds have different mechanisms.
- **Calling a weak individual correlation proof of no harm**: A group-wide culture change can suppress individual effect estimates.
- **Assuming edited-image literacy cancels comparison**: Comparison can operate outside conscious awareness.
- **Using “connection” as a sufficient benefit test**: Quantity, public performance, and social threat can rise while intimacy falls.

## Worked Example

Alexis Spence secretly opened Instagram at 11 by claiming to be 13, hid the app, and created secondary accounts after her parents tried to monitor it. Initial excitement over gaining followers shifted into a personalized stream from fitness to models, dieting, and pro-anorexia material; within months she was depressed and later hospitalized for anorexia. During a longer period without social media, her mother said Alexis became kind and recognizable again, although recovery was not simple or complete. The case illustrates algorithmic escalation, developmental vulnerability, family-level collective-action pressure, and the limits of using one anecdote to establish population causality.

## Key Takeaways

1. Social media's association with depression is stronger and more consistent for girls, especially heavy users of image-based platforms.
2. Experiments and platform rollouts support a causal contribution, while school-wide network effects may be undermeasured.
3. Visual comparison, socially prescribed perfectionism, relational aggression, emotional contagion, and predation form distinct but interacting pathways.
4. Communion needs can lure girls into platforms that multiply ties while reducing friendship quality.
5. Protect puberty by delaying public accounts, preserving a few close friendships, and treating online safety as a shared responsibility.
