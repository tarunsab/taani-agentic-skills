# Chapter 12: What Parents Can Do Now

**Part**: 4 — Collective Action for Healthier Childhood

## Core Idea

Haidt uses Alison Gopnik's gardener–carpenter distinction to redefine parenting. A carpenter tries to control materials and produce a specified outcome; a gardener creates a protected, nourishing environment and lets unpredictable children grow. Parents have overprotected the physical garden while letting the internet take over the virtual one, so the task is thoughtful cultivation in both realms. In practice that means more unsupervised play, responsibility, mobility, mentorship, and real-world challenge, alongside delayed smartphones and social-media accounts, device boundaries, sleep protection, and attention to problematic use. Recommendations are age-banded because a toddler, middle-schooler, and high-school student need different kinds of autonomy. The chapter's governing rule is to increase freedom with demonstrated competence while coordinating with other families so a healthier choice does not make a child socially isolated.

## The Gardener and the Carpenter

Gopnik argues that modern parents began treating child-rearing like a technical production job as families became smaller, more mobile, and less connected to local wisdom. The carpenter mentality seeks precision, control, and a finished product; it encourages optimization and makes variability feel like failure. Gardening asks for love, stability, protection, and good conditions, followed by space for the child to explore and surprise the adult. Haidt applies the metaphor in both directions: weed harmful digital access and cultivate real-world community, but do not micromanage every outcome. The parent supplies the environment, boundaries, and secure base; the child performs much of the learning.

## For Parents of Young Children (Ages 0 to 5)

### More (and Better) Experience in the Real World

Young children need a secure base, time with loving adults, mixed-age play, and opportunities to help with real household work. Parents should model attention rather than giving continuous partial attention to a phone and child at once. Responsibilities such as setting placemats or helping with laundry make a child feel useful and connected to the family. Interactions do not need to be optimized into lessons; the relationship itself is the developmental setting. Screens should not replace the three-dimensional, five-senses world in which early perception, language, movement, and social systems develop.

### Less (and Better) Experience on Screens

Haidt summarizes the cited medical guidance as no routine screen use in the first 18 months except shared video calls, educational viewing with a caregiver between 18 and 24 months, and limited noneducational time for ages 2–5. Video chat is active, synchronous, and relational; prerecorded video is passive and asynchronous and can disrupt language learning in very young children. Screens should be off during family meals and outings, not used as pacifiers or babysitters, and removed from bedrooms 30–60 minutes before bedtime. Parental controls and habits matter because a child's brain is expecting bodies, objects, movement, and responsive people. Haidt admits that his own family used *Teletubbies* to calm children and says he would do less of it in retrospect.

## For Parents of Children Ages 6–13 (Elementary and Middle School)

### More (and Better) Experience in the Real World

Elementary and middle-school years are sensitive periods for cultural learning, risk assessment, and peer status. Parents should practice letting children out of sight, encourage unmicromanaged sleepovers, organize group walks to school or “drive to five,” keep afternoons open for free play, go camping, and choose device-free sleepaway camps. Child-friendly “playborhoods” can be activated by one or two families who host regular outdoor play or rotate adult presence. The goal is not zero supervision but repeated off-base experience that lets a child discover competence and gives parents evidence that their fear can recede. At this age, delay the smartphone and preserve mixed-age, face-to-face community as the main social learning environment.

### Less (and Better) Experience on Screens

Haidt cites guidance of roughly no more than two hours per day of recreational screen activity for ages 6–12, adjusted for content, context, health, and the child's traits. Parents should use controls and filters, avoid reading private conversations unless necessary, and focus on blocking age-inappropriate access and setting times and places for use. The main metric should be whether the child has enough sleep, in-person friendship, activity, and freedom, not a rigid number divorced from opportunity cost. Shared meals, family movie nights, fixed bedroom cutoffs, a weekly digital Sabbath, and an annual screen-free week structure time and create communal rituals. Delay social-media accounts to 16; talk with preteens about pornography, sexting, cyberbullying, data, and public posting rather than assuming no account means no exposure.

## Recognize Problematic Use

The chapter applies seven warning signs to social media and other screen activities: interference with routines or commitments; strong cravings; lying or deception to use; choosing the screen over in-person interaction; loss of at least eight hours of quality sleep; loss of physical activity; and continued use despite wanting to stop. One sign deserves conversation, while several or an inability to self-correct justify a period without access and professional or specialized guidance. Video games are not automatically harmful, and moderation may be beneficial for many children; the concern is impairment, loss of control, and displacement. Parents should aim for eventual self-governance by 18, not permanent surveillance. Monitoring private messages can overprotect and damage trust, so default to proportionate boundaries and age-appropriate privacy.

## For Parents of Teens Ages 13–18 (High School)

### More (and Better) Experience in the Real World

High school should expand freedom and responsibility, not merely add a smartphone. Parents can help teens master bicycles, buses, trains, subways, and driving, use “third places” away from home and school, cook and run errands, find part-time work, nurture younger children, join exchange programs, take wilderness adventures, and consider a gap year. These experiences give adolescents non-parental role models, agency, useful work, and a reason to see themselves as contributors. The chapter's examples show that a young person can become a leader by helping someone younger even when the help costs effort. The principle is calibrated challenge: risk serious injury only for a reason, but recognize that refusing every meaningful risk also has costs.

### Less (and Better) Experience on Screens

When a teen receives a smartphone, parents should discuss the transition, set family parameters, and keep a consistent evening routine. Phones should leave the bedroom by a fixed time because checking texts and social feeds is often the last activity at night and the first in the morning. Parents should ask whether online life helps or hinders the teen's goals and teach how feeds, metrics, and hooks work. Haidt raises an unresolved question about parental tracking: it helped his family grant earlier freedom, but constant location access can become an invisible fence that weakens trust and competence. The book does not resolve the issue; it recommends noticing whether a tool supports independence or makes it harder to let go.

## In Sum

Parenting in the digital age requires cultivation in two environments. Give children more real-world freedom, responsibility, mobility, mentorship, and sleep, while delaying and structuring access to connected devices and public feeds. Judge screen use by functioning and displacement rather than a single universal minute count, and coordinate with other families so a healthier norm does not isolate one child. The endpoint is self-governance: a young adult who can use tools without being organized by them.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Gardener versus carpenter**: Shape the environment and boundaries; do not try to manufacture a specified child.
- **Secure base and off-base practice**: Provide reliable protection, then increase independent exploration.
- **Age-banded autonomy**: Match screens, freedom, responsibility, and mobility to developmental stage.
- **Opportunity-cost parenting**: Prioritize sleep, in-person life, activity, and competence over an isolated screen-minute target.
- **Problematic-use checklist**: Treat cravings, deception, displacement, sleep loss, inactivity, and inability to stop as diagnostic tells.
- **Coordinated family norms**: Align with friends' parents so limits protect belonging rather than create isolation.

## Key Concepts

- **Gardener mentality**: Create conditions in which many kinds of children can flourish.
- **Carpenter mentality**: Try to shape a child directly into a specified product.
- **Secure base**: Loving, reliable adult from whom a child explores.
- **Playborhood**: Neighborhood intentionally activated for regular child play.
- **Digital Sabbath**: Weekly period with reduced or no digital technology and shared activity.
- **Third place**: Social setting other than home or school, such as a park or pizzeria.
- **Problematic use**: Screen activity interfering with functioning without necessarily meeting full addiction criteria.
- **Self-governance**: Growing ability to set and follow one's own boundaries.

## Mental Models

- Use exposure to reduce parental fear: repeated safe independence teaches the adult that the child can cope.
- Give a child a responsibility that makes them needed, not merely entertained.
- Replace a single screen limit with a portfolio: sleep, friends, movement, commitments, and self-control.
- Let autonomy expand in both physical range and responsibility as competence accumulates.

## Anti-patterns

- **Optimizing every interaction**: It encourages control and anxiety instead of relationship and exploration.
- **Using screens as the default pacifier**: It teaches passive regulation and crowds out sensory and social learning.
- **Surveilling private life by default**: It can reproduce virtual overprotection and undermine trust.
- **Imposing limits alone**: A child may experience a healthier rule as social exile when peers remain connected.
- **Treating a smartphone as maturity**: The device should follow demonstrated responsibility, not replace it.

## Worked Example

Haidt describes his son Max first walking to school with a parent watching from behind, then later traveling alone to a night tennis match. When a subway transfer failed after the match, Max used the skills his parents had taught him, hailed a taxi, and returned safely. The first small exposures changed the parents' anxiety and made the later, larger responsibility possible. The example shows the gardener's feedback loop: give a bounded challenge, observe competence, update trust, and grant a wider range of action.

## Key Takeaways

1. Parent the environment, not a finished product: cultivate real-world experience and weed digital excess.
2. Give far more unsupervised play and independence, beginning with small, agreed responsibilities.
3. Delay smartphones until high school and social-media accounts until 16, coordinating with other families.
4. Protect sleep, meals, movement, in-person friendship, and family rituals with clear device boundaries.
5. Look for functional impairment and loss of control, not merely enthusiasm or a raw hour count.
6. Let tracking and monitoring serve independence rather than silently extending the umbilical cord.
