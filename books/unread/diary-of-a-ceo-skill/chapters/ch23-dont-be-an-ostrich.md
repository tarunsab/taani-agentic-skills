# Chapter 23: Don’t Be an Ostrich

**Pillar**: III — The Philosophy | **Law**: 23 | **Source basis**: Law 23 body

## Core Idea

Bartlett uses the “ostrich effect” for the tendency to avoid difficult information, conversations, or decisions because anxiety makes looking away feel easier in the moment. Titanic passengers who continued playing cards, returning to bed, or leaving lifeboats partly empty illustrate how denial can delay action until the threat becomes undeniable. The same pattern appears in ignored bank accounts, missed health checks, investments viewed only when markets rise, and companies that deny technological change or tolerate low performance. Avoidance is not inert: unresolved conflict moves through a social system, contaminating colleagues, family members, or partners while the original issue remains unspoken. Bartlett’s four-step method is pause and acknowledge, review yourself, speak your truth without blame, and seek the truth by listening to understand. The goal is not to eliminate discomfort, but to accept reality faster than the discomfort can make decisions for you.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Ostrich-effect diagnosis**
  - When to use: When you notice recurring anxiety, delay, distraction, or the same argument repeating without resolution.
  - How: Treat avoidance as information that something needs inspection rather than as proof that the issue will disappear.
- **Four-step discomfort process**
  - **Pause and acknowledge**: Admit that something is not right.
  - **Review yourself**: Inspect feelings, behaviours, unmet needs, and fears; seek help outside your preferred narrative.
  - **Speak your truth**: Share what you found without blame and with personal responsibility.
  - **Seek the truth**: Listen to understand and repeat the process until the real issue is addressed.

## Key Concepts

- **Ostrich effect**: Avoiding threatening information or reality to reduce immediate discomfort.
- **Denial**: A coping response that can temporarily contain anxiety or dangerously distort reality.
- **Blind spot**: A reality a person or organization cannot or will not see about itself.
- **Co-denial**: Mutual avoidance that keeps a failing relationship or system in place.
- **Unresolved conflict**: A problem whose unspoken effects spread through a social system.
- **Traction**: Healthy, reality-facing action, contrasted with distraction.
- **Discomfort**: The unavoidable pain of confronting what is true.

## Mental Models

- **Avoided pain becomes future pain** — Delaying a difficult conversation or diagnosis does not remove the issue; it allows the costs to compound.
- **Conflict moves through systems** — What leaders or partners leave unresolved can reappear as anxiety, withdrawal, poor behaviour, or conflict in adjacent relationships.

## Anti-patterns

- **Waiting for certainty before looking**: Reality is often easiest to change while the evidence is still uncomfortable but manageable.
- **Having the wrong conversation repeatedly**: Repeating surface arguments can be a way of avoiding the underlying unmet need.

## Worked Example

Bartlett’s four-step process begins with naming the persistent emotion or behaviour, then inspecting what it may be signalling. He describes the reviewer as a detective who sees evidence but needs help identifying the culprit. Speaking the findings without blame moves the conflict from an unproductive surface argument to the issue beneath it; listening to understand then tests whether the diagnosis is true. If the truth is still uncomfortable, return to the pause rather than retreat into distraction.

## Key Takeaways

1. Treat anxiety-driven avoidance as a signal, not a solution.
2. Pause before the fear chooses a distraction or delay.
3. Speak directly with ownership rather than accusation.
4. Seek truth from the other person and from evidence, even when it changes your preferred story.
