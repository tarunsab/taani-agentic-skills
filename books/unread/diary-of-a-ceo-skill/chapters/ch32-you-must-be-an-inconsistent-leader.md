# Law 32 — You Must Be an Inconsistent Leader

**Pillar**: IV — The Team  
**Source section**: Law 32, “You Must Be an Inconsistent Leader”

## Core Idea

Bartlett argues that great leaders are consistent about standards and purpose but deliberately variable in how they motivate different people. Sir Alex Ferguson’s strength, as described by Patrice Evra, Gary Neville, Rio Ferdinand and other Manchester United players, was knowing when an individual needed challenge, compassion, personal history or space. A blanket treatment can feel fair while failing to move anyone, because people differ in emotional triggers, insecurities, ambitions and responses to criticism. In this law, leadership is emotional management: become the complementary “jigsaw piece” for each person without losing the team’s shared objective.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Individual motivational fit**: adapt tone, intensity and message to the person receiving it.
- **The Ferguson test**: know the individual well enough to choose between a “hairdryer,” an arm around the shoulder, direct advice or hands-off trust.
- **Calculated emotional range**: leaders may shift emotion or absorb blame when that response best protects the team’s focus, confidence or standards.

## Key Concepts

Evra’s story supplies the chapter’s clearest example. After Evra played what he considered the best game of his life, Ferguson angrily criticised a backward pass at half-time and threatened to remove him; the team returned with renewed focus and won 4–0. The next day Ferguson told Evra he had actually been the best player and explained that the outburst was partly a message to the rest of the team, because Evra could take the fire. The incident illustrates that the same words are not interchangeable across people: their effect depends on trust, context, capability and the leader’s knowledge of the recipient.

Ferdinand describes Ferguson as someone who could attack a referee publicly after a defeat to deflect pressure from players, while Neville recalls personal references to his grandparents’ wartime work as a source of motivation. Bartlett’s broader conclusion is that humans are emotional and shaped by fears, desires, insecurities and experiences, so a purely rational, information-heavy leadership style is inadequate. Adaptation is not arbitrary favouritism: the shared bar and goal remain stable, while the route to commitment changes. The law’s ideal leader is therefore an emotional savant who knows the people well enough to be fluid without becoming manipulative.

## Mental Models

1. **Stable ends, flexible means**: preserve values, standards and outcomes while changing the interpersonal approach.
2. **Motivational jigsaw**: the leader supplies a different complementary shape for each team member.
3. **Message-to-recipient fit**: effectiveness depends on how a person interprets challenge, care, praise and autonomy.

## Anti-patterns

- Applying one communication style to every person in the name of fairness.
- Using anger as theatre without knowing whether the recipient can process it safely or productively.
- Confusing inconsistency of method with inconsistency of standards or hidden favouritism.

## Worked Example

For each direct report, record what reliably increases energy, what causes withdrawal, how they prefer feedback and what personal meaning connects them to the work. Before a difficult conversation, choose the smallest effective intervention—specific challenge, empathy, evidence, autonomy or a reset—and explain the standard being protected. Review the outcome with the person so adaptation becomes a relationship, not a performance imposed on them.

## Key Takeaways

- Treat people differently when their needs and motivators are genuinely different.
- Know the person before choosing the emotional register of a message.
- Be flexible in delivery while staying stable on standards, purpose and respect.

## Limitations

Ferguson’s confrontational examples should not be copied as a universal leadership style; power differences can make “hairdryer” treatment harmful. The useful generalisation is individual fit, psychological safety and clear standards—not theatrical anger or emotional manipulation.
