# Chapter 20: A Small Miss Now Creates a Big Miss Later

**Pillar**: III — The Philosophy | **Law**: 20 | **Source basis**: Law 20 body

## Core Idea

Bartlett extends kaizen from operational improvement to course correction in careers, relationships, friendships, and self-management. Tiger Woods rebuilt his golf swing soon after turning professional because a marginally better foundation mattered more than protecting short-term wins; he endured 18 months without a victory before the new swing helped produce an extraordinary winning run. The aviation “1 in 60 rule” supplies the metaphor: a one-degree deviation becomes a one-mile miss for every 60 miles travelled, so a small neglect can become a large destination error. In relationships, Bartlett uses John Gottman’s finding about contempt and describes a weekly check-in where his partner corrected a blunt phrase; adding one loving word kept a subtle rejection from compounding. The practical solution is frequent, low-drama review: inspect the route, name the small deviation, make a small adjustment, and repeat. The law is not a demand for constant anxiety or perfect control; it is a reminder that timely correction is cheaper than rescuing a life or relationship after years of drift.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **One-degree course correction**
  - When to use: When a project, relationship, or personal habit feels slightly misaligned but not yet broken.
  - How: Check the current direction regularly, identify the smallest meaningful deviation, and correct it before distance amplifies it.
- **Kaizen check-in**
  - When to use: In recurring relationships and responsibilities.
  - How: Schedule an honest review, surface unaddressed small issues, agree on a marginal change, and revisit the effect.

## Key Concepts

- **Compounding miss**: A small deviation that grows through time and distance.
- **Course correction**: A small adjustment that returns a system to its intended direction.
- **1 in 60 rule**: The aviation analogy that one degree off creates a one-mile error per 60 miles.
- **Contempt**: Subtle disrespect or disregard that Bartlett cites as a strong predictor of divorce.
- **Weekly check-in**: A recurring ritual for alignment, repair, and improvement.
- **Drift**: Loss of direction caused by repeated unaddressed deviations.
- **Neglect**: Failing to act on an easy correction because the immediate consequence is invisible.

## Mental Models

- **Today’s seed, tomorrow’s regret** — A small omission is often the beginning of the later crisis, even when no single moment seems responsible.
- **Winning is not always improvement** — Short-term outcomes can conceal a needed rebuild; protect the long-term trajectory rather than only the current score.

## Anti-patterns

- **Waiting for a crisis to review**: By the time a deviation becomes obvious, correction may require a painful reset.
- **Avoiding small conversations**: Silence allows subtle rejection, resentment, or contempt to become the relationship’s default.

## Worked Example

During a weekly relationship check-in, Bartlett’s partner said his response “Sorry, I’m busy with something” sounded blunt and rejecting. He changed it to “Sorry, love, I’m busy with something.” The wording change was tiny, but it addressed the repeated signal before it accumulated into a larger pattern. Bartlett applies the same ritual to directors, friends, and his own diary, using regular review to keep the route aligned.

## Key Takeaways

1. Schedule reviews before there is a crisis.
2. Treat small discomfort as useful navigation data.
3. Prefer a one-degree correction today to a dramatic repair later.
4. Judge progress by direction and alignment as well as by visible wins.
