# Law 30 — The Three Bars for Building Great Teams

**Pillar**: IV — The Team  
**Source section**: Law 30, “The Three Bars for Building Great Teams”

## Core Idea

The culture of a team is protected by deciding whether each person raises, maintains or lowers its average bar. Bartlett uses Sir Alex Ferguson’s Manchester United as the primary example: Ferguson treated club culture and values as more important than any individual star, and moved on famous, productive players when their behaviour conflicted with the “United way.” The practical question is not whether a person is identical to the rest of the team; it is whether their cultural values, standards, attitude and talent would improve the organisation if replicated. The law therefore makes culture a hiring, promotion, training and firing criterion rather than a slogan.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Three bars**: bar raiser, bar maintainer, bar lowerer.
- **Culture-before-star test**: ask whether the organisation would improve if everyone had this person’s values, attitude and level of talent.
- **Fire, hire, train**: remove persistent lowerers, select raisers, and develop people who can meet the standard.

## Key Concepts

Ferguson’s record is used to show the cost of putting an individual above the collective. David Beckham, Roy Keane and Ruud van Nistelrooy were all successful players, yet the book presents their departures as decisions made when distraction, conflict or unacceptable conduct threatened the team ethos. Bartlett acknowledges that removing a valuable employee is psychologically difficult, which is why leaders rationalise delay through the avoidance pattern in Law 23. The harder truth is that allowing a respected but corrosive person to remain can damage many good employees.

The chapter supports this with research on behavioural contagion. A Harvard Business Review study reported that employees were 37 percent more likely to commit misconduct when exposed to a co-worker with a history of misconduct, and calculated a social multiplier of 1.59. Research discussed by Will Felps and Terence Mitchell similarly found that a single negative member could outweigh several positive workers by encouraging copying, disengagement, anxiety and social withdrawal. Bartlett’s memorable operational conclusion is that no one person leaving a good company necessarily kills it, but one person staying sometimes can.

## Mental Models

1. **Replication test**: evaluate the cultural consequence of scaling one person’s behaviour across the team.
2. **Contagion asymmetry**: negative behaviour can spread faster than positive behaviour can repair it.
3. **Culture as an asset**: talent is valuable only while it strengthens the collective standards that let other talent perform.

## Anti-patterns

- Keeping a star because their output appears to compensate for cultural damage.
- Confusing diversity of thought with tolerance for low standards, bullying or misconduct.
- Delaying a decision because firing creates short-term emotional friction.

## Worked Example

For every important hire, promotion or retention review, ask: “If the organisation had this person’s values, attitude and level of talent, would the bar be raised, maintained or lowered?” Separate the answer from similarity of background or worldview. If the person lowers the bar, diagnose whether coaching can change the behaviour within a defined window; otherwise act quickly enough to protect the rest of the team.

## Key Takeaways

- Every hire should raise the bar or clearly strengthen the existing one.
- A high-output person can still be a net negative if their conduct is contagious.
- Protecting culture requires decisive action, not only celebratory values statements.

## Limitations

The bar test is a leadership heuristic, not a substitute for fair performance processes or evidence. It must not be used to enforce sameness, punish dissent or make employment decisions on protected characteristics; Bartlett specifically limits it to values, standards, attitude and talent.
