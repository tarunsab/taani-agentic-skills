# Chapter 17: Let Them Try and They Will Buy

**Pillar**: II — The Story | **Law**: 17 | **Source basis**: Law 17 body

## Core Idea

Bartlett uses the endowment effect to explain why people value something more once they touch it, use it, imagine owning it, or participate in making it. A Christmas mix-up left his niece clutching the “wrong” Buzz Lightyear toy and his nephew happily accepting the Elsa doll; once each child possessed an item, the objective mismatch mattered less than ownership. Apple Stores deliberately turn browsing into an ownership experience: products are powered, connected, touchable, and available for unpressured play, combining mere exposure with the endowment effect. Build-A-Bear does the same by making children choose and create their own animals, while studies cited by Bartlett found that touching or imagining ownership increases value estimates and that Duke basketball ticket winners valued their tickets far above non-winners’ willingness to pay. The mechanism is not only possession but reduced uncertainty, multisensory familiarity, and the feeling that the product already belongs in one’s life. The practical law is to let people test-drive, handle, configure, or experience a product before asking for the purchase, while ensuring the trial is honest and does not create unwanted lock-in.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Endowment effect**
  - When to use: When a customer understands a product abstractly but has not yet felt its personal value.
  - How: Give the person real or imagined possession through a trial, touch, configuration, or use experience before the purchase decision.
- **Ownership experience**
  - When to use: When the product benefits from multisensory interaction or personal setup.
  - How: Remove pressure, make the product easy to explore, and let customers discover solutions and imagine continued ownership.
- **Mere exposure + ownership**
  - When to use: When designing a store, demo, onboarding, or free trial.
  - How: Use repeated contact to increase liking and hands-on possession to increase perceived value; the two effects reinforce one another.

## Key Concepts

- **Endowment effect**: Overvaluing an item because it is owned or believed to be owned.
- **Ownership experience**: A pre-purchase interaction that makes a product feel personally possessed.
- **Mere exposure effect**: Greater liking produced by repeated exposure.
- **Multisensory experience**: Seeing, touching, hearing, or manipulating a product.
- **Trial**: A temporary opportunity to use or configure an offering.
- **Possessiveness**: Attachment that makes a person reluctant to trade away what they have.
- **Value estimate**: The price or worth a person assigns to an item.

## Mental Models

- **Touch changes the question** — A person who is merely evaluating asks whether an item is worth it; a person who feels ownership asks whether they want to give it up.
- **Experience before explanation** — Let the customer form a personal evidence base instead of relying on claims about what the product can do.

## Anti-patterns

- **Hard sell before experience**: Pressure can prevent the customer from building the ownership feeling the trial is meant to create.
- **Trial with artificial scarcity or lock-in**: A genuine ownership experience should clarify fit, not exploit reluctance to return something unsuitable.

## Worked Example

Apple puts working devices on tables, lets customers use them without commission-driven staff pressure, and gives them time to play. Bartlett says this combines mere exposure, which makes the products more familiar and liked, with endowment, which makes them feel more valuable. The Duke ticket study supplies a striking comparison: non-winners would pay about $175, while winners demanded about $2,400 to sell, showing how possession can widen the value gap.

## Key Takeaways

1. Put the product in the customer’s hands whenever the experience can teach value.
2. Let people explore without pressure so ownership can form naturally.
3. Combine familiarity with personal control and configuration.
4. Make trials reversible and honest enough that the customer can judge fit.
