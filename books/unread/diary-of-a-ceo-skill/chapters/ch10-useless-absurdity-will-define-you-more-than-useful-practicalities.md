# Chapter 10: Useless Absurdity Will Define You More Than Useful Practicalities

**Pillar**: II — The Story | **Law**: 10 | **Source basis**: Law 10 body

## Core Idea

Bartlett argues that a brand’s public story is often carried by the apparently useless, absurd, or inefficient feature that makes people talk, not by the practical features that every competitor also offers. His first marketing company’s £13,000 blue slide was rarely used for its intended purpose, but journalists repeatedly photographed it and read it as evidence that the company was young, different, disruptive, and innovative. Third Space’s 100-foot climbing wall works similarly in conversation: it stands in for the scale and energy of an entire gym even though the friend does not sell the machines or changing rooms. Tesla’s zero advertising budget is offered as another example, with playful names and absurd features generating social conversation, while BrewDog’s beer fridges in hotel showers communicate a punk, beer-loving identity without a conventional campaign. The mechanism is symbolic: costly, unusual, or illogical choices signal values more loudly than rational sameness. Bartlett warns that absurdity is difficult to measure and therefore demands conviction, but treats it as a way for a small brand to make its marketing spend travel further.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Useless absurdity**
  - When to use: When a product or company is surrounded by interchangeable practical claims.
  - How: Identify or deliberately create one memorable, surprising feature that embodies the brand’s values and gives people a reason to tell the story.
- **Signal over utility**
  - When to use: When deciding what the public will remember about an experience.
  - How: Ask what the unusual detail says about who the brand is, who it is for, and how it differs; evaluate it as a message, not only as a functional feature.

## Key Concepts

- **Absurdity**: A surprising or apparently impractical element that attracts attention and communicates identity.
- **Useful practicality**: A functional benefit that may be necessary but is shared by many alternatives.
- **Brand signal**: A feature whose social meaning exceeds its direct utility.
- **Public story**: The narrative outsiders repeat about a company, product, or group.
- **Costly signal**: A choice that appears to incur cost or risk and therefore conveys commitment.
- **Status quo**: The conventional pattern that people have little incentive to discuss.
- **Founder conviction**: The willingness to invest in hard-to-measure brand meaning despite pressure for immediate ROI.

## Mental Models

- **The strange detail writes the headline** — The single feature people photograph or retell may define the whole brand more strongly than a catalogue of benefits.
- **Absurdity as compressed identity** — A weird feature can imply youth, scale, humour, rebellion, or innovation without stating those attributes directly.

## Anti-patterns

- **Selling only the category basics**: Rational parity produces little conversation or distinction.
- **Demanding immediate measurable ROI for every signal**: The mechanism works through memory, talk, and association, which may not appear in a direct campaign metric.

## Worked Example

Bartlett’s office had a gaming room, a bar, a basketball hoop, a tree, and a large blue slide. The slide was the detail journalists repeatedly used in stories, and the company’s media exposure grew without an outbound sales team. Bartlett’s point is not to copy the slide literally; it is to design an unmistakable detail whose story makes the larger proposition believable. Tesla’s “Ludicrous” modes and hidden playful features illustrate the same approach at product scale.

## Key Takeaways

1. Find the unusual detail that makes the brand memorable and discussable.
2. Design features as messages about identity, not only as functions.
3. Use absurdity when you can tolerate ambiguity and long-horizon measurement.
4. Do not let practical competence become invisible sameness.
