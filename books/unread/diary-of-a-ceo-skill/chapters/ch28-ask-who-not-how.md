# Law 28 — Ask Who, Not How

**Pillar**: IV — The Team  
**Source section**: Law 28, “Ask Who Not How”

## Core Idea

When a task appears in front of you, the instinct to ask “How can I do this?” can turn leadership into a bottleneck. Bartlett’s better question is “Who is the best person that can do this for me?” The law is not an excuse to avoid understanding a business; it is a shift from personally accumulating every skill to assembling people whose strengths complement one another. In the book’s framing, a company is fundamentally a recruitment company: its outcomes are the combined ingenuity, ideas and execution of the people it assembles, bound together by culture.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Who over how**: locate the person who can own the outcome, rather than defaulting to personal execution.
- **Strength-led work**: concentrate deeply on the work where ability and enjoyment reinforce one another; delegate the rest.
- **Recruitment-company view**: judge a founder by hiring strong people and creating the culture that lets them become more than the sum of their parts.

## Key Concepts

Richard Branson is Bartlett’s central example. Branson described being dyslexic, poor at school and still unable to distinguish gross profit from net profit until a director explained it with a fishing net and fish; his strength was people, trust and delegation, not universal competence. Bartlett connects this story to his own discomfort about not being naturally strong at maths, spelling or operational work, and to his decision to double down on products and delegate work he could not do or did not enjoy. Jimmy Carr’s criticism of all-rounder education supplies a sharper distinction: becoming mediocre at a weakness can be less valuable than becoming exceptional at a natural strength.

The book extends the point beyond personal productivity. Steve Jobs is quoted as saying that smart people should be hired so they can tell the company what to do, not so the company can prescribe every action to them. Founders who overstate their own importance mistake their ideas for the source of the company’s future; Bartlett argues that the destination is defined by the people hired and the culture that makes their contributions compound. This also explains why the law connects directly to the next two team laws: who gets hired establishes the early culture, and cultural standards determine whether the assembled team improves or degrades.

## Mental Models

1. **Ego versus potential**: ego insists “I should do it”; potential asks who can do it best.
2. **Complementary puzzle pieces**: different people need different roles, strengths and support; uniform competence is not the goal.
3. **1 + 1 = 3**: culture can make a group more capable than the sum of isolated individuals.

## Anti-patterns

- Trying to become competent at every function before delegating.
- Hiring people who need to be told exactly what to do instead of people who can extend the founder’s judgment.
- Treating a founder’s personal brilliance as the main determinant of company outcomes.

## Worked Example

For a new product launch, list the outcome and the capabilities it requires, then ask who can own each capability at a higher level than you can. Keep the decisions that depend on your distinctive product judgment, but recruit or delegate the operational, financial, legal and distribution work to people with stronger relevant experience. Give each owner context, a measurable outcome and authority to choose the route; otherwise “who” becomes delegation in name only.

## Key Takeaways

- Ask who can produce the outcome best before asking how to do it yourself.
- Build around distinctive strengths and delegate durable weaknesses.
- The founder’s highest-leverage work is hiring excellent people and binding them with a culture that gets the best from them.

## Limitations

Delegation still requires enough context to set standards, assess quality and notice when an owner is failing. The source celebrates strong delegation but does not give a universal decision rule for when a founder must personally learn a skill; the practical boundary depends on risk, reversibility and the cost of ignorance.
