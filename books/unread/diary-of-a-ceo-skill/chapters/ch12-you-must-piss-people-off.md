# Chapter 12: You Must Piss People Off

**Pillar**: II — The Story | **Law**: 12 | **Source basis**: Law 12 body

## Core Idea

Bartlett argues that a brand that matters must produce an emotional response, and that trying to make everyone mildly comfortable often creates indifference. He uses the rise of profanity in self-help book titles as an example of a saturated category searching for a way to interrupt the wallpaper filter; the tactic worked because it was conspicuous and divisive, not because swearing is inherently persuasive. Jane Wurwand’s rule for Dermalogica is stated as a deliberate ratio: be willing to offend or alienate roughly 80 per cent in order to strongly activate 20 per cent. That minority can love the brand, while another group hates it; both reactions create connection, whereas people who feel nothing do not engage. Bartlett also gives a boundary: emotional hooks have a shelf life, and once an effective tactic becomes popular and overused it habituates into wallpaper. The law therefore means choose a clear emotional position and accept polarisation, not manufacture offence without a meaningful brand point.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Make people feel something—either way**
  - When to use: When a message is blending into a crowded category.
  - How: Choose a bold, emotional, or disruptive point of view that can produce love or dislike; avoid a bland message that produces no response.
- **80/20 emotional activation**
  - When to use: When deciding whether to soften a distinctive proposition to reach everyone.
  - How: Prefer a message that strongly turns on a meaningful minority over one that leaves the entire audience indifferent, provided the brand can sustain the trade-off.

## Key Concepts

- **Indifference**: The absence of love or hate and, in Bartlett’s account, the least profitable emotional outcome.
- **Emotional hook**: A provocative element designed to create a felt response.
- **Polarisation**: A message dividing the audience into strong positive and negative reactions.
- **Semantic satiation**: The loss of meaning that eventually follows overused emotional language.
- **Brand**: A proposition that triggers a recognizable emotional response, not merely a product.
- **Differentiation**: Being definable through a clear position rather than being middle-of-the-road.

## Mental Models

- **Love/hate/indifference triage** — Ask whether a message will make a meaningful audience feel something; indifference is the signal that it has become wallpaper.
- **Shelf-life of provocation** — The tactic that once felt daring will lose force when competitors copy it and audiences learn to expect it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Mediocrity by consensus**: Smoothing every sharp edge can make a product palatable but not memorable or loved.
- **Provocation without identity**: Offence alone is not a brand strategy; it must express a point of view people can connect with.

## Worked Example

Bartlett observed that none of the top Amazon books a decade earlier used profanity in their titles, while by 2018 many did, following the success of Mark Manson’s cuss-covered title. The first wave cut through noise, but later repetition weakened the tactic. Wurwand’s marketing principle adds the strategic decision: accepting that 80 per cent may dislike a bold message can be worthwhile if 20 per cent becomes intensely engaged and the brand remains distinct.

## Key Takeaways

1. Optimize for a clear emotional response, not universal mild approval.
2. Know which audience you are trying to turn on and what identity the message expresses.
3. Expect successful provocations to decay as they become common.
4. Refresh the emotional device once it becomes wallpaper.
