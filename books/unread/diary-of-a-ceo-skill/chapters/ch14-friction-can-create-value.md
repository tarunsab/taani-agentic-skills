# Chapter 14: Friction Can Create Value

**Pillar**: II — The Story | **Law**: 14 | **Source basis**: Law 14 body

## Core Idea

Bartlett’s counterintuitive claim is that making an experience easier is not always the best way to increase perceived value; some audiences read effort, delay, or inconvenience as evidence that an outcome is powerful or carefully made. Red Bull’s medicinal taste is presented as a signal that the drink delivers performance, while a performance-nutrition brand worried that an unusually pleasant taste made customers doubt its efficacy. General Mills discovered the same expectation problem with Betty Crocker cake mix: removing powdered egg made the product less convenient, but adding “an egg” restored the baker’s sense of contribution and value. A hot stone with raw steak adds customer effort while improving control, reducing returns, and making the process visible and engaging. Travel aggregators similarly show a longer search with multiple sites to signal thoroughness, even though the underlying search could be faster. The law is contextual rather than universal: friction creates value when it confirms the story customers expect, gives them control or contribution, or reduces uncertainty; needless obstacles that contradict the promise remain destructive.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Expectation-matched friction**
  - When to use: When customers doubt a product because it seems too easy, fast, pleasant, or effortless for the promised result.
  - How: Add a small, meaningful action, pause, or visible process that confirms competence, effort, efficacy, or control.
- **Subtraction technique**
  - When to use: When convenience has removed the customer’s sense of authorship or contribution.
  - How: Restore one step that the customer values, as General Mills did by requiring an egg in the cake mix.

## Key Concepts

- **Friction**: Effort, delay, inconvenience, or participation in an experience.
- **Expectation**: The customer’s prior belief about how a category should feel or perform.
- **Perceived efficacy**: The belief that a product works, often inferred from sensory or procedural cues.
- **Subtraction technique**: Removing convenience to increase involvement and perceived value.
- **Operational transparency**: Visible process that makes effort and progress credible.
- **Customer control**: The ability to shape an outcome, such as cooking a steak to a preferred doneness.
- **Perceived value**: A judgment formed from expectations and the experience that meets them.

## Mental Models

- **Effort as evidence** — When the promised outcome is demanding, a small amount of effort can serve as evidence that the outcome is real or earned.
- **The right kind of inconvenience** — Add friction only when it improves control, participation, credibility, or meaning for the intended audience.

## Anti-patterns

- **Universal convenience**: Removing every step can remove ownership, effort, and confidence in the result.
- **Friction without a story**: An arbitrary delay or obstacle feels like poor service when it does not reinforce a valued expectation.

## Worked Example

The original Betty Crocker mix required only water, so homemakers could feel they had taken a shortcut and had not really baked. General Mills removed powdered egg and instructed customers to add an egg. The product objectively became less convenient, but the added action made the baker feel more valuable and sales rose. Bartlett uses the same logic for raw steak on a hot stone: customer participation improves control, keeps the wait active, and increases satisfaction.

## Key Takeaways

1. Ask what customers expect the experience to signal before optimizing for speed.
2. Use small, purposeful effort to create contribution, control, or credibility.
3. Make hidden work visible when visibility reassures the customer.
4. Remove friction when it is merely bureaucratic; add it only when it creates a valued meaning.
