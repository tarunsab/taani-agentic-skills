# Chapter 5: You Must Lean In to Bizarre Behaviour

**Pillar**: I — The Self | **Law**: 5 | **Source basis**: Law 5 body

## Core Idea

“Leaning in” means moving toward information, ideas, or technologies that feel strange, threatening, or hard to understand instead of dismissing them. Bartlett contrasts this with the music-store CEO who insisted people loved music while failing to notice that they no longer wanted the inconvenience of CDs once iTunes offered the underlying outcome more directly. He explains premature rejection through cognitive dissonance: evidence that threatens identity, intelligence, livelihood, or a public commitment is painful, so people rationalize, attack, or stop listening. The same pattern appeared in the fashion brand that rejected social media and in the criticism directed at Bartlett’s young social-media company; he treats intense criticism of an unfamiliar innovation as a signal worth investigating, not proof of failure. The “Shimon Peres solution” is to hold two apparently conflicting truths at once—when a friend makes a mistake, the friend remains a friend and the mistake remains a mistake—rather than force a binary choice. The practical discipline is to reserve judgment, study the unfamiliar thing, and ask whether discomfort comes from genuine risk or from not understanding what threatens one’s existing story.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Lean-in person**
  - When to use: When encountering an innovation, social movement, competitor, or idea that makes you feel stupid, threatened, or dismissive.
  - How: Pause judgment, research the thing on its own terms, and ask what you do not understand before deciding whether it is useful or harmful.
- **Cognitive dissonance**
  - When to use: When evidence conflicts with behaviour, identity, loyalty, or a public position.
  - How: Notice the discomfort; do not automatically reduce it through rationalization, attack, or avoidance. Let the conflict prompt inquiry.
- **Shimon Peres solution**
  - When to use: When two seemingly incompatible judgments create pressure to choose one side.
  - How: Keep both statements separate and true where appropriate; a person can remain a friend and still have made a mistake, and an old way can retain merit while a new way is worth exploring.

## Key Concepts

- **Lean out**: Refuse to listen or learn because new information feels threatening.
- **Cognitive dissonance**: Mental tension produced by conflict between thoughts, behaviour, evidence, or identity.
- **Identity threat**: Evidence that destabilizes a person’s view of themselves or their place in the world.
- **Incumbent**: An established person, company, or convention with something to lose from change.
- **Innovation signal**: Unfamiliarity, criticism, or weirdness that may indicate a meaningful departure from convention.
- **Nuance**: The ability to preserve more than one relevant truth without collapsing into tribal binaries.

## Mental Models

- **Discomfort is diagnostic** — The feeling that an idea is stupid or wrong may be evidence of a gap in understanding or a threat to identity, not evidence about the idea itself.
- **Hold the dissonance** — Resist the urge to make every contradiction disappear immediately; sustained inquiry can produce a better judgment.

## Anti-patterns

- **Public certainty too early**: A strong public claim makes later evidence harder to accept because the brain starts defending consistency.
- **Attacking what you do not understand**: Criticism can relieve discomfort while leaving the underlying reality unexamined.

## Worked Example

In 2012 Bartlett tried to persuade a major fashion brand to launch social-media pages. The marketing director feared public comments, loss of control, and the risk of virality; the meeting ended before the presentation did. Bartlett’s company grew while the brand later filed for bankruptcy. He later applied the opposite response to Web3 and blockchain: rather than accept the surrounding hype or criticism, he researched the technology beneath the bad behaviour and founded thirdweb after concluding that some underlying functions could become easier, faster, and cheaper.

## Key Takeaways

1. When you do not understand something, lean in more before judging it.
2. Treat intense discomfort as a prompt to inspect identity and incentives.
3. Keep apparently conflicting truths separate instead of choosing a convenient binary.
4. Reserve judgment long enough to learn what the market, evidence, or unfamiliar users are saying.
