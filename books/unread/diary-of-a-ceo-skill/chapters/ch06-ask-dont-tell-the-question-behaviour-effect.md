# Chapter 6: Ask, Don’t Tell — The Question/Behaviour Effect

**Pillar**: I — The Self | **Law**: 6 | **Source basis**: Law 6 body

## Core Idea

Bartlett argues that a well-designed question can motivate behaviour more effectively than a statement because the question makes the recipient actively answer and confront the action. Reagan’s 1980 debate question—whether voters were better off than four years earlier—turned economic evaluation into a personal judgment rather than a lecture of facts. Bartlett cites a review of more than 100 studies over 40 years finding that asking about a future behaviour can change whether it happens, sometimes for up to six months. The effect is strongest with binary yes-or-no questions, with questions beginning “will” rather than “can,” “could,” or “would,” and when “yes” fits the person’s desired identity or social ambitions. A yes answer creates cognitive dissonance if behaviour does not follow, while a binary format removes the elaborate excuses that let someone escape commitment. The technique can be used on oneself, with a friend, or in service interactions, but the question should invite honest ownership rather than become a coercive trick.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Question/behaviour effect**
  - When to use: When trying to encourage a concrete future action or habit.
  - How: Ask a clear question, preferably yes/no, about whether the person will perform the action; make the action congruent with who they want to become.
- **Will over can/could/would**
  - When to use: When wording a motivational question.
  - How: Use “Will I…?” or “Will you…?” to imply ownership and action; “can” emphasizes ability, while “could” and “would” leave more room for hypothetical escape.

## Key Concepts

- **Active response**: The mental work elicited by a question rather than passively receiving a statement.
- **Binary commitment**: A yes-or-no answer that narrows the space for rationalization.
- **Cognitive dissonance**: Discomfort between the desired self and an answer or action that contradicts it.
- **Ownership**: The sense that the person has chosen and must enact the behaviour.
- **Future behaviour**: An action a question can make more likely by creating intention and commitment.
- **Excuse space**: The verbal room that allows a person to defer or explain away a choice.

## Mental Models

- **Question as mirror** — A good question makes a person inspect their own desired identity and answer to it.
- **No wiggle room** — Binary wording converts vague intention into a visible commitment that behaviour can either honour or contradict.

## Anti-patterns

- **Lecturing with instructions**: Statements such as “Please recycle” or “I will eat vegetables” can be weaker than a question that requires an active answer.
- **Using conditional language**: “Could” and “would” can preserve possibility without creating a decision.

## Worked Example

Bartlett was running up a steep hill in Porto and asked himself, “Will you keep running—without stopping—until you’ve reached the top?” Saying yes killed the excuses he might have used to stop and created a promise he did not want to break. He applies the same pattern to a friend considering a promotion or a healthier diet. The Reagan example shows the public version: the question made voters perform the comparison and then act on their own conclusion.

## Key Takeaways

1. Ask for a behaviour instead of merely telling someone what to do.
2. Prefer a specific yes-or-no question that begins with “will.”
3. Tie the action to the person’s desired identity or ambition.
4. Use the technique to create ownership, not to replace informed consent or honest judgment.
