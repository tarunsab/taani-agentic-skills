# Chapter 16: Use Goldilocks to Your Advantage

**Pillar**: II — The Story | **Law**: 16 | **Source basis**: Law 16 body

## Core Idea

The Goldilocks effect is Bartlett’s application of anchoring: presenting an inexpensive, a middle, and an expensive option can make the middle option appear safest, most reasonable, and best value. He explains that people often lack an objective measure of “true” value, so they use nearby prices and features as context cues. An estate agent demonstrated the tactic by showing Bartlett a property that was too small and one that was obviously overpriced around the middle property he hoped to sell; the extremes made the standard option feel sensible. Panasonic’s microwave range and an experiment adding a “Rome without coffee” option are used to show that a deliberately unattractive alternative can increase preference for the target. The middle option is not necessarily the cheapest or objectively best; it benefits from a story of balanced quality, risk, and price. The practical use is to design a transparent range whose options genuinely correspond to different needs, rather than manipulating people with fake comparisons.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Goldilocks effect**
  - When to use: When selling a standard offer whose value is hard to judge in isolation.
  - How: Place it between a clearly limited/economy option and a premium option that is materially more expensive; let the contrast make the standard choice feel balanced.
- **Anchoring**
  - When to use: When people are making a judgment with incomplete information.
  - How: Recognize that the first or surrounding number, feature, or alternative can influence later evaluation, even when it is not intrinsically relevant.

## Key Concepts

- **Goldilocks effect**: Preference for a middle option created by two contrasting extremes.
- **Anchoring**: Reliance on an initial or nearby cue when making a judgment.
- **Economy option**: A lower-priced or lower-feature alternative that can make the standard option look safer.
- **Premium option**: An expensive alternative that can make the standard option look efficient.
- **Context cue**: Information surrounding an option that helps people infer its value.
- **Perceived value**: A judgment shaped by comparison rather than price alone.
- **Trade-off**: The quality, risk, and cost balance a customer believes the options represent.

## Mental Models

- **Middle as safe bet** — The target option inherits the imagined benefits of the extremes while avoiding their perceived risks.
- **No option in isolation** — If value is uncertain, the range tells a story before the product does.

## Anti-patterns

- **Offering only one option**: A single price supplies little context and makes the customer do all the value construction alone.
- **Fake decoys**: An option designed only to mislead can destroy trust if customers notice the manipulation or if the differences are not real.

## Worked Example

For a property trip, the agent showed Bartlett a small, expensive first house, a spacious second house only slightly more expensive, and a very expensive third house. Bartlett chose the second. Panasonic used a similar range: when a premium microwave was added above the existing prices, the mid-priced microwave became the popular option and the company’s share rose. The contrast did not change the middle product; it changed the comparison frame.

## Key Takeaways

1. Give customers a meaningful range when a standard option needs context.
2. Use extremes to clarify trade-offs, not to hide defects or fabricate value.
3. Remember that “best value” is a story produced by comparison, not the same as “lowest price.”
4. Review your own decisions for anchors before assuming your preference is purely intrinsic.
