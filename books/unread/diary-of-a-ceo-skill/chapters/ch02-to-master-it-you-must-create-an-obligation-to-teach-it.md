# Chapter 2: To Master It, You Must Create an Obligation to Teach It

**Pillar**: I — The Self | **Law**: 2 | **Source basis**: Law 2 body

## Core Idea

Bartlett attributes his change from a terrified, mute 14-year-old speaker to someone speaking on major stages to one practice: creating an obligation to teach. At 21 he committed to learning an idea, distilling it into a tweet or video every day, and sharing it at a fixed time, turning an interest into a public duty. The public commitment created “skin in the game”: feedback, audience attention, and reputation became losses he wanted to avoid, which raised the psychological stakes and accelerated repetition. Teaching also exposed the boundary of his understanding, because he had to make an idea clear enough for someone else to receive it rather than hide behind jargon. His revised Feynman technique is a loop of learning, teaching a child in simple language, sharing for feedback, and reviewing whether people understood. The law applies beyond content creation: any public, consistent practice can turn knowledge into usable skill, provided the obligation produces honest feedback rather than performative noise.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Obligation to teach**
  - When to use: When you want to master a topic, skill, or form of communication.
  - How: Choose one idea, learn it, explain it publicly and consistently, and let the audience’s response expose what still needs work.
- **Skin in the game**
  - When to use: When good intentions are not generating enough repetition.
  - How: Put money, attention, reputation, or a social commitment at stake so avoiding loss strengthens the desired behaviour.
- **Revised Feynman technique**
  - When to use: When you suspect you recognize a concept without truly understanding it.
  - How: (1) Learn the topic thoroughly; (2) teach it to a child using simple words; (3) share it in a feedback-rich medium; (4) review comprehension and return to learning if the explanation failed.

## Key Concepts

- **Public obligation**: A commitment that makes learning and sharing a recurring responsibility.
- **Social contract**: The implicit agreement with an audience that motivates continued delivery.
- **Skin in the game**: Having something to lose, raising the stakes of action.
- **Loss aversion**: The tendency for avoiding a loss to motivate behaviour more strongly than pursuing an equivalent gain.
- **Distillation**: Reducing an idea to its essential meaning without hiding behind extra words.
- **Feedback loop**: Sharing, receiving response, improving, and sharing again.

## Mental Models

- **Mastery is release** — Retaining information is not enough; explaining and transferring it is evidence that the knowledge has become usable.
- **Constraint reveals comprehension** — A strict format such as a short post forces the learner to separate the idea from its verbal decoration.

## Anti-patterns

- **Private consumption without output**: Reading or watching can create the feeling of learning without the pressure to explain, perform, or receive correction.
- **Overcomplication**: Bigger words and longer explanations can mask a weak mental model instead of proving understanding.

## Worked Example

At 21 Bartlett promised himself that at 7 p.m. every day he would write a tweet or make a video containing one idea, then publish it at 8 p.m. The routine built knowledge, writing, speaking, and delivery skill while comments and platform analytics supplied feedback. It also created a growing audience whose attention and trust he would risk losing if he stopped. Bartlett describes the same logic as buying a small amount of a company to learn it, or reporting daily workouts to friends: money or reputation makes the lesson consequential.

## Key Takeaways

1. Turn learning into a recurring public obligation.
2. Explain ideas simply enough that another person can reconstruct them.
3. Use feedback to locate the gap between knowing and communicating.
4. Give the behaviour something meaningful to lose, while keeping the commitment sustainable.
