# Law 31 — Leverage the Power of Progress

**Pillar**: IV — The Team  
**Source section**: Law 31, “Leverage the Power of Progress”

## Core Idea

People are most engaged when they feel they are moving forward. Bartlett uses Sir David Brailsford’s marginal-gains philosophy to show how tiny, repeatable improvements can generate both performance and the belief that progress is possible. The motivational mechanism is partly perceptual: a small win makes a difficult mission feel tractable, which invites another small win and creates a narrative that the team is “on the move.” Leaders therefore create energy by making progress meaningful, clear, autonomous, friction-light and visible, rather than waiting for a distant breakthrough.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Marginal gains**: improve many small aspects of performance instead of waiting for one heroic intervention.
- **Smallification**: reduce an intimidating task to easy, achievable micro-goals.
- **Five progress levers**: create meaning, set clear actionable goals, provide autonomy, remove friction, and broadcast progress.

## Key Concepts

Before Brailsford, British Cycling was widely regarded as a failure; under his direction, the team improved details such as hand hygiene, tyre grip, saddle comfort, sleep conditions and wind-tunnel performance. The book links this attention to small inputs with a striking run of Olympic, world-championship and Tour de France success. Brailsford’s explanation is that perfection is too distant to motivate consistently, while a small improvement by next week feels achievable and gives people evidence that they can improve again tomorrow. The goal is not to fetishise triviality; it is to create sustainable stickability.

Teresa Amabile’s analysis of nearly 12,000 diary entries is used to support the claim that incremental progress is more frequently associated with positive emotion and high motivation than any other workday event. Bartlett pairs this with Nir Eyal’s account of procrastination as avoidance of psychological discomfort and Karl Weick’s argument that oversized problems overwhelm action. Small wins lower the emotional cost of starting, reveal patterns, attract allies and reduce resistance. Recognition helps, but the chapter’s distinction is that recognition works best when it is attached to real achievement and forward motion.

## Mental Models

1. **Progress flywheel**: meaning and a clear next step produce action; action produces a win; the win produces energy and more ideas.
2. **Micro-goal bridge**: scale a challenge down until the next action feels possible rather than threatening.
3. **Visibility multiplier**: a public progress ritual turns one team’s evidence of movement into permission for adjacent teams to move too.

## Anti-patterns

- Setting only an inspiring end-state and leaving people without near-term milestones.
- Assuming praise can substitute for meaningful work, ownership or completed progress.
- Letting bureaucracy, missing resources or slow decisions hide the team’s daily wins.

## Worked Example

Translate a quarterly objective into a few weekly milestones, then ask each owner to choose the next smallest action that advances one milestone. Make obstacles explicit in a check-in and remove them quickly. End the week with a concise update showing what changed, why it matters and what comes next; include customer or human evidence when metrics alone would make the work feel abstract.

## Key Takeaways

- Small, sustainable gains can outperform bursts of heroic effort.
- Make the work meaningful and the next action clear enough to start.
- Progress becomes a team asset when autonomy, friction removal and visible broadcasting reinforce it.

## Limitations

The source uses high-performing teams to illustrate marginal gains, but not every problem yields to incremental optimisation; some situations require a strategic reset or a major investment. Perceived progress should be tied to genuine outcomes, not cosmetic activity or misleading dashboards.
