# Chapter 13: Shoot Your Psychological Moonshots First

**Pillar**: II — The Story | **Law**: 13 | **Source basis**: Law 13 body

## Core Idea

A psychological moonshot is Bartlett’s term for a relatively small intervention that produces a large improvement in perceived value or satisfaction. His hairdresser’s “one last snip” did not improve the haircut, but the pause, inspection, and fictitious final cut signalled care and attention to detail. Uber’s redesign shows the same logic at system scale: rather than only making transport faster, Uber Labs worked on the psychology of waiting through the peak-end rule, idleness aversion, operational transparency, uncertainty anxiety, and the goal-gradient effect. Domino’s Pizza Tracker reduced uncertainty; TESSEI’s bright uniforms and visible cleaning theatre made rapid train cleaning credible; and McDonald’s kiosks made the process feel faster, more novel, more private, and closer to completion. The common mechanism is to improve the customer’s mental model of progress, control, ownership, or care without pretending that reality does not matter. Bartlett’s decision rule is to inspect perception and psychological friction before spending heavily on physical improvements, while keeping the intervention truthful rather than deceptive.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Psychological moonshot**
  - When to use: When a customer’s dissatisfaction is driven by perception, uncertainty, or experience rather than a simple physical defect.
  - How: Find a tiny, credible change that makes progress, care, control, value, or completion more visible and emotionally satisfying.
- **Five Uber principles**
  - **Peak-end rule**: Design the emotional high/low and the ending because people remember those moments disproportionately.
  - **Idleness aversion**: Give waiting customers something meaningful or engaging to do.
  - **Operational transparency**: Show the process, calculation, and progress behind the experience.
  - **Uncertainty anxiety**: Replace vague waiting with a clear status or estimate, even when the news is negative.
  - **Goal-gradient effect**: Make proximity to completion or reward visible so effort and motivation rise near the finish.

## Key Concepts

- **Perceived value**: The value a person experiences, not only the objective specification.
- **Psychological friction**: Anxiety, ambiguity, idleness, or effort that makes an experience feel worse.
- **Peak-end rule**: Judging an experience through its peak and ending rather than its average.
- **Idleness aversion**: The preference for justified activity over unexplained waiting.
- **Operational transparency**: Making hidden progress and calculations visible.
- **Uncertainty anxiety**: Distress caused by not knowing what is happening or when it will end.
- **Goal-gradient effect**: Increasing effort as a goal appears nearer.
- **Ownership experience**: Letting customers feel involvement and control before purchase.

## Mental Models

- **Perception before construction** — Before rebuilding reality, ask which psychological signal would make the existing reality more legible and satisfying.
- **Make the invisible visible** — Progress, care, process, and completion are often valued more when the customer can see them.

## Anti-patterns

- **Waging an expensive war on reality first**: Faster, bigger, or more feature-rich may miss the psychological problem causing dissatisfaction.
- **Placebo without integrity**: Perception work should clarify or improve the experience, not hide a material failure or make a false promise.

## Worked Example

Domino’s customers called when a pizza was late, but the calls interrupted the kitchen and delayed orders further. The Pizza Tracker reused existing order-management software to show the five-step process, replacing uncertainty with a visible status. Bartlett says calls fell, satisfaction and retention rose, and the company saved and made substantial money. The intervention changed the customer’s experience of waiting even though it did not make every pizza physically faster.

## Key Takeaways

1. Diagnose the psychological source of dissatisfaction before adding cost or complexity.
2. Design peak moments, endings, progress signals, and explanations deliberately.
3. Reduce uncertainty and idle waiting by showing what is happening.
4. Use small perception changes to reveal genuine value; do not use them to conceal bad reality.
