# Chapter 11: Avoid Wallpaper at All Costs

**Pillar**: II — The Story | **Law**: 11 | **Source basis**: Law 11 body

## Core Idea

Bartlett uses habituation to explain why people stop noticing repeated words, sounds, images, smells, and experiences, even when those stimuli were initially powerful. Aron Ralston’s ability to focus on action while trapped under a boulder is an extreme example of the brain filtering information it no longer treats as the most relevant; rats in a familiar maze similarly move on autopilot after the environment is learned. Semantic satiation is the language version: repetition can temporarily strip a word of meaning, turning it into noise. Mere exposure is the complementary effect—familiarity can increase liking—so effective communication must find the optimal level of exposure where a message is familiar enough to feel safe but novel enough to be noticed. Bartlett calls overused phrases and calls to action “wallpaper,” and shows the principle with his replacement of generic “like and subscribe” language with a specific statistic, a reciprocity request, and a promise of better future guests. He also stresses that repetition follows an inverted-U: exposure first builds meaning, then familiarity becomes satiation, so creators must refresh a message before the audience tunes it out.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Habituation filter**
  - When to use: When an important message is being ignored despite repeated exposure.
  - How: Replace neutral, predictable presentation with a specific, unusual, emotionally relevant, or varied stimulus that restores attention.
- **Semantic generation → semantic satiation**
  - When to use: When deciding how long to keep a phrase, hook, or campaign.
  - How: Repeat while meaning and response rise; when the message remains familiar but loses action-producing force, change its wording or shape.
- **Mere exposure and optimal exposure**
  - When to use: When balancing novelty with liking.
  - How: Give people enough repeated contact to build familiarity, but vary the presentation before repetition turns the stimulus into wallpaper.

## Key Concepts

- **Habituation**: The brain’s adjustment to repeated stimuli by ignoring or downgrading their significance.
- **Semantic satiation**: Temporary loss of a word’s meaning through excessive repetition.
- **Semantic generation**: The rising phase in which repeated exposure increases familiarity and meaning.
- **Mere exposure effect**: Increased preference produced by repeated exposure.
- **Wallpaper**: Overused language or imagery that the brain no longer processes with meaning.
- **Optimal exposure**: The sweet spot between unfamiliarity that is hard to like and familiarity that is easy to ignore.
- **Attentional hook**: An opening device that interrupts habituation and earns attention.

## Mental Models

- **Novelty gets attention; familiarity gets liking** — Effective stories move between the two rather than choosing only repetition or only surprise.
- **The inverted-U message curve** — More exposure is helpful until it passes the point where the audience can predict it without processing it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Default call to action**: Common phrases can be literally heard without being noticed.
- **Assuming memorability equals effectiveness**: A phrase can remain memorable while losing its ability to create emotion or action.

## Worked Example

Bartlett’s channel had millions of views but roughly 70 per cent of viewers did not subscribe. “Please like and subscribe” barely moved the rate, so he replaced it with a specific non-subscriber statistic, a request grounded in reciprocity, and a promise that a larger channel could attract larger guests. He reports that the revised wording increased the viewer-to-subscriber rate by 430 per cent after one use. The example demonstrates the pattern: specificity and a meaningful reason can bypass a phrase the audience has learned to ignore.

## Key Takeaways

1. Audit language, visuals, sounds, and formats for habituation.
2. Use specific, unexpected, unsaturated wording when attention matters.
3. Repeat to build familiarity, then refresh before meaning turns into wallpaper.
4. Design the first presentation and every later variation as part of one attention system.
