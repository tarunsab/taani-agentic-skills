# Chapter 26: Your Skills Are Worthless, but Your Context Is Valuable

**Pillar**: III — The Philosophy | **Law**: 26 | **Source basis**: Law 26 body

## Core Idea

Bartlett argues that a skill has no intrinsic market price; its value depends on where it is needed, how rare it appears there, and how much value the buyer believes it can create. After leaving his marketing company, he researched psychedelics and was then invited to help a biotech company prepare for a multi-billion-dollar IPO; the same marketing skills that had earned ordinary fees in crowded consumer sectors were offered $6–8 million in stock options because the context was high stakes and the expertise was scarce. The lesson is not only “change jobs,” but question the professional label that limits where a capability can travel. Joshua Bell’s subway experiment supplies the vivid analogy: a world-class violinist playing a $3.5 million instrument for commuters earned $52.17 in 45 minutes, vastly less than in a prestigious concert hall. Bartlett’s friend moved graphic design from local flyers to luxury and blockchain clients in Dubai and increased projected revenue dramatically. The practical strategy is to combine complementary, rare skills and transplant them into a context where they affect a valuable outcome competitors cannot easily produce.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Context arbitrage**
  - When to use: When income has plateaued despite strong performance.
  - How: Identify industries or problems where the same skill is rarer, closer to a high-value decision, or linked to larger returns; reposition the skill there.
- **Complementary rarity**
  - When to use: When competing against specialists who are better at one isolated skill.
  - How: Combine several useful capabilities that are individually solid but jointly unusual and valuable to the target industry.
- **Label escape**
  - When to use: When a job title narrows the opportunities you can see.
  - How: Describe yourself by curiosity and transferable capabilities rather than by one profession’s boundary.

## Key Concepts

- **Context**: The market, industry, problem, and stakes in which a skill is used.
- **Perceived rarity**: How uncommon a skill appears in a particular context.
- **Value generated**: The outcome a buyer believes the skill can influence.
- **Professional label**: A role name that can provide belonging but also become a constraint.
- **Complementary skill**: A capability that increases the value of another capability when combined.
- **Market value**: What a person or organization is willing to pay in a given context.
- **Context arbitrage**: Increasing the price of an existing skill by moving it to a higher-value context.

## Mental Models

- **Violin in the subway versus concert hall** — Talent does not create the same value signal everywhere; the setting determines who notices and what they can pay.
- **Rarity × consequence** — A skill becomes more valuable when fewer people have it in the target context and when its effect on the outcome is large.

## Anti-patterns

- **Waiting only for an internal promotion**: The current market may have normalized the skill and therefore its price.
- **Treating a label as identity**: A title can become a professional shackle that hides transferable combinations.
- **Selling a skill where it is common**: Capability alone does not create scarcity or bargaining power.

## Worked Example

Bartlett researched psychedelic biotech during lockdown, then received an invitation from a major shareholder to market a company preparing for an IPO. In consumer goods, e-commerce, and technology, similar marketing services had been common and paid at a fraction of the biotech offer. In biotech, digital storytelling and social-platform expertise were rare while the company’s valuation stakes were enormous, so the company offered potential stock options of $6–8 million plus salary. The context—not a sudden change in Bartlett’s underlying skill—created the price difference.

## Key Takeaways

1. Audit where your existing skills are rare and economically consequential.
2. Move across contexts before assuming you need a wholly new capability.
3. Combine complementary skills to become difficult to substitute.
4. Describe your value by the problem you can change, not only by your job title.
