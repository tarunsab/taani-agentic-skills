# Chapter 21: You Must Out-Fail the Competition

**Pillar**: III — The Philosophy | **Law**: 21 | **Source basis**: Law 21 body

## Core Idea

Bartlett reframes failure as feedback: a failed experiment creates knowledge, and knowledge expands the chance of a later success. He cites IBM’s Thomas J. Watson, who said doubling the failure rate could increase success, and distinguishes useful attempts from mistakes that teach nothing. Booking.com learned customer preferences through constant testing and eventually ran around 1,000 experiments at a time; Amazon accepted a graveyard of failed products because occasional long-tailed winners such as Prime, Kindle, and AWS could pay for many losses. Jeff Bezos’s Type 1/Type 2 distinction prevents over-deliberation: irreversible decisions deserve care, while reversible two-way doors should be made quickly by high-judgment people. Bartlett’s father-and-son brand comparison shows the cost of delay: the son acted on a reversible social-growth experiment immediately, while the father’s ten-month delay closed the opportunity. To build a pro-failure culture, he recommends removing bureaucracy, fixing incentives, promoting fast experimenters and removing blockers, measuring failure, and sharing what failed.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Failure = feedback = knowledge = power**
  - When to use: When fear of failure is suppressing experimentation.
  - How: Define a testable hypothesis, run the smallest useful experiment, record the outcome, and circulate the learning so the next attempt starts further ahead.
- **Type 1 / Type 2 decisions**
  - When to use: Before applying a heavy approval process to a decision.
  - How: Treat irreversible or nearly irreversible choices as Type 1 and deliberate carefully; treat reversible two-way-door choices as Type 2 and decide quickly with a small, capable group.
- **Five-part pro-failure culture**
  - How: Remove bureaucracy; align incentives; promote and fire for the desired behaviour; measure accurately; share failures and hypotheses.

## Key Concepts

- **Experiment**: An unproven test whose outcome is not known in advance.
- **Failure rate**: The number or proportion of experiments that do not produce the intended result.
- **Type 1 decision**: A consequential, hard-to-reverse choice.
- **Type 2 decision**: A changeable, reversible choice.
- **Bureaucracy**: Rules, sign-offs, and hierarchy that slow action and suppress ideas.
- **Misaligned incentives**: Rewards and risks that encourage behaviour different from the stated culture.
- **Long-tailed return**: A distribution in which a few exceptional wins pay for many ordinary losses.
- **Failure library**: Shared organizational memory of failed hypotheses and outcomes.

## Mental Models

- **Opportunity cost of caution** — Delaying a reversible test loses time, learning, and sometimes the market window; avoiding loss can create a larger loss.
- **51 per cent certainty** — When the probability and impact are understood well enough, decide without waiting for impossible certainty.

## Anti-patterns

- **Slogan-only experimentation**: “Fail faster” on a wall means nothing when embarrassment, firing, or no recognition make failure unsafe.
- **Type 1 process for Type 2 work**: Heavy approval on reversible experiments creates slowness and risk aversion.
- **Hiding failures**: Unshared learning forces teams to repeat the same unsuccessful work.

## Worked Example

Bartlett brought a social-growth discovery to a father’s brand and a son’s brand on the same day. The father’s organization demanded presentations and layers of sign-off; ten months later the platform loophole had closed. The son called the team together and launched immediately, adding 10 million followers at 95 per cent lower cost. Bartlett estimates the son’s experiments produced many failures, but roughly one in ten was great enough to pay for the rest—an example of speed and asymmetric upside.

## Key Takeaways

1. Increase the number of informative attempts, not careless errors.
2. Match decision speed and approval weight to reversibility.
3. Reward execution of a well-designed experiment, not only a successful market outcome.
4. Share failure details so the organization compounds learning instead of repeating loss.
