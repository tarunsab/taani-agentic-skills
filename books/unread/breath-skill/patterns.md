# Patterns

## Nasal Baseline
**When to use**: Rest, sleep, and manageable exercise when the nose is open.
**How**: Breathe quietly through the nose; investigate persistent obstruction rather than forcing it.
**Trade-offs**: Nasal breathing filters and pressurizes air and adds sinus nitric oxide, but structural blockage may require clinical care.

The book treats the nose, throat, and lungs as one airway. Nasal airflow helps keep posterior tissues open, while chronic mouthbreathing can reinforce collapse and sleep disruption. Brief mouth use during illness or intense effort is not the same as an all-day baseline. Nestor’s two-person study supports the direction of the pattern, but not a universal treatment effect.

## Resonant Breathing
**When to use**: Calming, sleep preparation, or autonomic coordination.
**How**: Inhale for about 5.5 seconds and exhale for about 5.5 seconds, without a pause, for ten or more cycles.
**Trade-offs**: Portable and low effort; the timing is a guide, not a target to force.

The book links this rhythm to prayer and mantra, the Pavia study, and later work with anxiety and lung injury. Longer exhalation and slow rate are associated with parasympathetic settling and cardiovascular coherence. Start with shorter, comfortable cycles if 5.5 seconds feels strained. Stop if the practice causes dizziness or panic.

## Full-Exhale / Breathing Coordination
**When to use**: Shallow chest breathing, poor recovery, or a sense of stale air.
**How**: Exhale softly while counting; let the voice fade to a whisper and then silent mouth movement; refill gently.
**Trade-offs**: Trains diaphragm range, but forceful emptying can create distress.

Stough’s model says the exhale creates room for a fuller inhale and assists the thoracic pump. Counting turns respiratory effort into a progressive exercise that can be repeated until the range becomes more automatic. The method is presented as access to functioning capacity, not regeneration of damaged lung tissue. Complex respiratory disease belongs with a clinician.

## Buteyko “Less”
**When to use**: Habitual overbreathing, low carbon dioxide, or a breathing pattern linked to asthma/panic in the book’s evidence.
**How**: Reduce volume gently through the nose; use a Control Pause and brief, safe pauses as feedback.
**Trade-offs**: May improve tolerance and efficiency; evidence is mixed and extreme restriction is risky.

The key distinction is volume, not just a slower clock. Buteyko’s healthiest subjects breathed about five to six liters per minute at rest, while his sickest patients breathed much more. The chapter reports promising asthma and panic results but also notes that mechanisms remain uncertain and some claims were dismissed as pseudoscience. Do not use the method to stop medication or manage severe attacks alone.

## Functional Chewing and Oral Posture
**When to use**: Supporting jaw, palate, tongue, and airway function.
**How**: Use appropriately resistant food or clinician-selected chewing proxies; keep lips together, teeth lightly touching, tongue on the palate, and head upright.
**Trade-offs**: Mechanical input may support development/remodeling; hard clenching, pain, and unsupervised appliances can injure.

The source connects soft diets to narrow arches and uses Price, Corruccini, Mew, and Belfor to argue that structure remains responsive. Expansion and oral posture are presented as functional alternatives to simply extracting and retracting teeth. The evidence is a mixture of historical comparison, case series, animal work, and personal scans. Dental assessment matters more than copying a device or online exercise.

## Tummo / Conscious Sympathetic Stress
**When to use**: Only for an instructed, bounded stress-and-recovery practice.
**How**: Fast deep breaths, a partial-exhale hold, a recovery inhale, and repeated rounds; modern versions may add cold exposure.
**Trade-offs**: May train autonomic flexibility; can provoke panic, tetany, nausea, or fainting, and long-term effects are uncertain.

The book’s mechanism is a deliberate sympathetic surge followed by parasympathetic recovery, with carbon dioxide and stress hormones moving sharply. The Radboud endotoxin study is presented as evidence that trained volunteers can influence immune and autonomic responses. Personal stories are more dramatic than the research base. Never practice near water, driving, walking, or other hazards.

## Chemoreceptor Exposure
**When to use**: As a research or clinical model for panic and respiratory alarm.
**How**: Use measured breath awareness or supervised exposure; do not improvise carbon-dioxide gas.
**Trade-offs**: May widen tolerance to air hunger; can itself trigger severe panic.

The chapter’s S. M. case shows that carbon dioxide can trigger fear without functioning amygdalae. This supports a body-generated alarm pathway, not a simple “all anxiety is thoughts” model. Feinstein’s work resembles exposure therapy, but the clinical evidence remains early. Ordinary breathholding during sleep or distracted work is not therapeutic training.

## Facilitated Holotropic Work
**When to use**: Structured psychological exploration with trained facilitators and sitters.
**How**: Sustained heavy breathing, music, protected setting, observation, and integration.
**Trade-offs**: Subjectively powerful; studies are sparse, mostly self-report, and sessions can be physically or psychologically intense.

The book distinguishes Holotropic Breathwork from Tummo: its main aim is psychological processing rather than autonomic temperature or immune control. Nestor reports altered imagery and tetany and questions how much derives from breathing versus set and setting. The method should not be reduced to a home playlist and forceful hyperventilation. Facilitation and a safe exit are part of the pattern.
