# Chapter 7: Chew

## Core Idea

“Chew” returns to the structural origin of airway problems and argues that food texture is a respiratory variable. Nestor traces the shift from traditional, fibrous, resistant foods to milling, canning, sugar, and soft processed meals, then follows researchers who found narrower palates, crowded teeth, and more obstructed airways when hard diets were replaced by soft ones. Weston Price’s international observations, Robert Corruccini’s cross-cultural and animal comparisons, and the historical work of James Sim Wallace are used to show that the pattern can appear within generations, although critics objected to anecdotal samples and overextended conclusions. The chapter then critiques extraction-and-retraction orthodontics for making some already small mouths smaller, and presents John and Mike Mew’s oral-posture and expansion approach. Theodore Belfor supplies the proposed mechanism: chewing loads the masseter, stimulates stem-cell activity around sutures, and can remodel the plastic maxilla even in adulthood. Nestor’s one-year Homeoblock experience and follow-up scans are offered as a personal result—new facial bone, wider airways, and easier breathing—not as a universal treatment. The practical theme is to restore functional inputs through appropriate chewing and posture while using trained dental professionals for appliances or palatal expansion.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Chew-airway connection**
  - **When to use:** When crowded teeth, a narrow palate, snoring, or a recessed jaw are treated as unrelated.
  - **How:** Consider food texture, chewing load, tongue strength, facial growth, and airway room as one developmental chain.
- **Functional expansion versus extraction/retraction**
  - **When to use:** When orthodontic treatment is being designed for a small arch and airway.
  - **How:** Ask whether the plan creates room and supports function or simply removes teeth and pulls the remaining structure backward.
- **Oral posture**
  - **When to use:** During rest, sitting, and standing.
  - **How:** Lips together, teeth lightly touching, tongue on the roof of the mouth, head upright, spine in the book’s J-shaped alignment, and slow nasal abdominal breathing.
- **Mewing**
  - **When to use:** As the Mews’ tongue-posture exercise, not as proven self-treatment.
  - **How:** Rest the back of the tongue against the palate and move the contact forward while keeping the oral posture stable.

## Key Concepts

- **Masseter:** Powerful chewing muscle below the ears.
- **Maxilla:** Plastic membrane bone forming the center of the face and upper jaw.
- **Sutures:** Skull seams that remain capable of movement and bone production.
- **Palatal expansion:** Widening the upper palate to create oral and airway space.
- **Monobloc:** Pierre Robin’s early mouth-expanding appliance.
- **Biobloc:** John Mew’s modified expansion device.
- **Homeoblock:** Belfor’s appliance designed to simulate chewing stress and expand function.
- **Cranial dystrophy:** Mike Mew’s term for the forward-head and collapsed oral-posture pattern.
- **Mewing:** Tongue-thrusting posture popularized by Mike Mew.

## Mental Models

- **Texture is training:** Food that requires work exercises the system that must house the airway.
- **Give the teeth a playing field:** Straight teeth can be an outcome of sufficient arch width, not only a target achieved by removing teeth.
- **Structure is plastic but not magic:** Adult remodeling is possible in the book’s cases, yet appliances, age, diagnosis, and clinician skill change the result.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assume “healthy” processed food supplies the same stimulus:** Nutrition and mechanical chewing are separate inputs.
- **Treat extraction as a neutral space-saving fix:** The chapter’s critique is that removing teeth and retracting the arch may reduce airway room.
- **Clench or force the tongue:** A therapeutic load is not the same as jaw strain, pain, or temporomandibular injury.
- **Self-prescribe an appliance:** Expansion devices require assessment, fitting, maintenance, and monitoring.

## Worked Example

After a year wearing Belfor’s Homeoblock, Nestor’s scans showed 1,658 cubic millimeters of new bone around the cheeks and right eye socket, additional bone along the nose and upper jaw, and wider, firmer airways. Sinus pus and granulation seen earlier were gone, and he reported easier nasal breathing. The first weeks involved excess saliva, throat constriction, and tooth pain, which later became tolerable. The case illustrates the book’s claim that adult facial bone can respond to repeated mechanical input, but it is one person’s result and does not establish that every appliance or posture exercise will produce the same changes.

## Key Takeaways

1. Mouth and airway development reflect both nutrition and mechanical demand.
2. Chew harder, less processed foods when appropriate, but do not confuse this with a universal raw-food prescription.
3. Keep lips together, tongue on the palate, and head and spine aligned at rest.
4. Evaluate orthodontics by airway function as well as tooth alignment.
5. Treat Mewing, Homeoblock, Biobloc, and palatal expansion as clinician-dependent interventions with mixed evidence.
