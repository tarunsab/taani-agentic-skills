# Chapter 1: The Worst Breathers in the Animal Kingdom

## Core Idea

This chapter argues that modern breathing problems begin partly in the shape and development of the mouth, face, sinuses, and throat. Nestor uses his own crowded mouth, narrow palate, crooked teeth, deviated septum, and obstructed sinuses as a case study, then places that anatomy beside ancient skulls with broad jaws, large nasal openings, straight teeth, and wide airways. Marianna Evans and Kevin Boyd’s measurements of more than one hundred skulls are presented as evidence that modern facial growth has shifted backward and downward compared with many preindustrial specimens. Nestor calls this harmful change dysevolution: evolution means change, not guaranteed progress, and modern environments can reward traits that undermine health. He traces the transition from raw, laborious food to tenderized, cooked, farmed, milled, canned, and highly processed food, arguing that reduced chewing and altered nutrition allowed the brain to expand while the mouth and airway narrowed. The chapter therefore treats nasal congestion, snoring, sleep apnea, and malocclusion as connected outcomes of developmental history rather than isolated defects.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Dysevolution**
  - **When to use:** When a modern trait is widespread but seems biologically disadvantageous.
  - **How:** Ask what environmental change made the trait common, then separate “more recent” from “better.”
- **Facial growth as airway architecture**
  - **When to use:** When dental alignment, palate shape, nasal obstruction, and sleep symptoms are discussed separately.
  - **How:** Inspect the relationship among jaw position, palate width, sinus volume, tongue space, and airway room.
- **Developmental inputs**
  - **When to use:** When anatomy is treated as fixed destiny.
  - **How:** Consider early feeding, chewing load, food texture, posture, and chronic obstruction as inputs that shape a growing system.

## Key Concepts

- **Malocclusion:** Misaligned teeth or jaws, including overbites, underbites, and crowding.
- **High-arched palate:** A V-shaped palate that rises rather than widening, reducing nasal and oral space.
- **Forward facial growth:** The broad-jawed, forward-projecting pattern Nestor contrasts with modern recessed faces.
- **N-perpendicular:** One of the facial-angle measurements used in the skull comparisons.
- **Frankfort plane:** A reference plane used to compare facial symmetry and growth.
- **Dysevolution:** Harmful change produced by the interaction of modern environments and human biology.
- **Airway obstruction:** Reduced space or flow in the nasal, oral, or pharyngeal passage.

## Mental Models

- **The skull as a fossilized breathing record:** Compare jaw width, nasal apertures, and airway space across populations instead of treating teeth as a cosmetic snapshot.
- **Use versus disuse:** A structure that receives less mechanical or respiratory input may develop differently or lose function.
- **The mouth is not an isolated room:** Food texture, teeth, tongue, palate, sinuses, and throat form one developmental neighborhood.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treat crooked teeth as only an inherited cosmetic problem:** The chapter presents heredity as incomplete when it excludes food texture, chewing, and airway development.
- **Assume evolution always improves health:** Dysevolution is the warning that adaptation and progress are not synonyms.
- **Blame one nutrient or one behavior too quickly:** The historical evidence Nestor reports points toward interacting diet, development, and environmental inputs.

## Worked Example

Nestor’s “patient” is himself. A soft commercial diet and little chewing were followed by a small dental arch, extractions, braces, a tongue that no longer fit comfortably, and chronic nasal problems. At Stanford, an endoscope and X-ray showed a high-arched palate, severe left-sided obstruction, and unusual sinus structures. The case is not offered as a universal diagnosis; it demonstrates how a lifetime of seemingly ordinary interventions can accumulate into less airway space. It also establishes the experimental question for the next chapter: what happens when an already compromised airway is forced to use the mouth alone?

## Key Takeaways

1. Widespread does not mean normal or harmless; modern malocclusion and snoring may be historically recent.
2. Facial structure can be read as part of the respiratory system, not only as dental appearance.
3. Soft food reduces the mechanical demand that once helped develop broad jaws and airways.
4. Investigate developmental and environmental inputs before treating airway problems as fixed destiny.
5. The chapter’s skull comparisons support a direction of change, but they do not prove that every modern symptom has one cause.
