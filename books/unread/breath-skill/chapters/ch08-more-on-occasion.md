# Chapter 8: More, on Occasion

## Core Idea

Part Three introduces Breathing+, a group of practices that intentionally push physiology toward states that would be concerning if they happened involuntarily. Nestor contrasts ordinary slow, less, nasal breathing with deliberate overbreathing, prolonged holds, cold exposure, and psychologically intense sessions. The autonomic nervous system supplies the main model: slow, deep breaths and longer exhales favor parasympathetic restoration, while short, fast breaths recruit sympathetic readiness. Tummo, or Inner Fire Meditation, uses conscious sympathetic stress followed by breathholding and recovery; the book connects it to Tibetan practice, Alexandra David-Néel, Wim Hof, laboratory studies, and reports of immune, temperature, and inflammatory changes. Holotropic Breathwork uses sustained heavy breathing, music, and a facilitated setting to provoke altered states and psychological material, but its evidence is largely subjective and its mechanism remains incomplete. Nestor reports that overbreathing can lower carbon dioxide and brain blood flow, producing tetany, hallucination, and a felt death-and-rebirth cycle; he also records the objection that modern long-term stress may make additional sympathetic stimulation unwise. The chapter’s judgment is therefore conditional: a controlled, instructed disruption may train flexibility, but extreme breathing is not a universal baseline or a safe solo experiment.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Autonomic two-way switch**
  - **When to use:** When a breathing practice is intended to change arousal, recovery, digestion, or stress.
  - **How:** Use slow breathing and long exhales for settling; understand fast breathing as sympathetic activation that must be followed by recovery.
- **Conscious stress**
  - **When to use:** In the book’s Tummo model of making stress voluntary rather than being passively overwhelmed by it.
  - **How:** Enter a brief, bounded stress state, observe it, then return deliberately to calm; the ability to stop matters as much as the intensity.
- **Tummo / Inner Fire**
  - **When to use:** Only in a controlled, safe setting with appropriate instruction.
  - **How:** Cycles of deep fast breathing, a partial exhale hold, a recovery inhale, and repeated rounds; cold exposure is part of the modern Hof version.
- **Holotropic Breathwork**
  - **When to use:** For facilitated experiential psychotherapy or self-exploration, not ordinary relaxation.
  - **How:** Sustained heavy breathing, a protected setting, music, a sitter/facilitator, and integration afterward.

## Key Concepts

- **Breathing+:** Nestor’s umbrella for intense breathing, holds, and related practices.
- **Sympathetic nervous system:** Mobilizes the body for action.
- **Parasympathetic nervous system:** Supports rest, digestion, restoration, and reproduction.
- **Vagus nerve:** Network connecting the brain to major organs and regulating autonomic communication.
- **Tummo:** Tibetan “inner fire” practice; modern versions often combine fast breathing, holds, and cold.
- **Holotropic Breathwork:** Grof’s extended heavy-breathing therapy for altered states and psychological processing.
- **Tetany:** Involuntary muscle contractions associated in the chapter with hyperventilation.
- **Set and setting:** The surrounding environment and expectations that shape an altered-state experience.

## Mental Models

- **Stress as a dose:** A short, voluntary stressor may train recovery; chronic or unbounded stress degrades it.
- **Pilot, not passenger:** Breathing gives conscious access to an otherwise automatic system, but control requires a reliable stop and recovery.
- **Physiology plus context:** The same breathing rate can feel different in a safe facilitated room, a cold lake, or a dangerous everyday setting.

## Anti-patterns

- **Practice near water, while driving, walking, or alone where fainting could injure you:** The book repeats this warning because loss of consciousness is a real possibility.
- **Treat heat, visions, or immune changes as proof of cure:** The experiences may involve autonomic and cerebral mechanisms, expectation, context, or several factors at once.
- **Use extreme sympathetic stress as daily relaxation:** Olsson argues that a chronically adrenaline-heavy society may make this counterproductive.
- **Skip facilitation and integration:** Holotropic sessions can involve panic, vomiting, tetany, traumatic material, or disorientation.

## Worked Example

With instructor Chuck McGee, Nestor performs repeated deep, fast breaths on a mat, then holds after a partial exhale and takes a recovery breath before repeating. He experiences sweating, a racing heart, tingling, intestinal movement, and later a spreading warmth and quiet. The book uses this felt sequence to connect Tummo with sympathetic activation, vagal recovery, and the Radboud endotoxin experiment in trained volunteers. It then contrasts Holotropic sessions, where participants breathe for much longer with music and sitters; Nestor experiences tetany and altered imagery, while other participants show dramatic behavioral and emotional release. Because the Holotropic studies mostly measure reported experience, the chapter keeps its therapeutic interpretation tentative.

## Key Takeaways

1. Deliberate overbreathing is a specialized stressor, not a replacement for gentle regulation.
2. A complete cycle includes activation, a controlled pause, and recovery.
3. Vagus and autonomic models explain why breathing can affect several organs at once.
4. Context, expectation, facilitation, and safety are part of the method.
5. The strongest limitation is the gap between vivid personal reports and sparse long-term research.
