# Chapter 5: Slow

## Core Idea

“Slow” is the chapter’s answer to the common instruction to breathe deeply and quickly. Nestor follows Christian Bohr’s discovery that carbon dioxide helps loosen oxygen from hemoglobin, so overbreathing can lower carbon dioxide, constrict blood vessels, and reduce oxygen delivery even when a pulse oximeter still reads normally. The body loses most fat through exhaled carbon dioxide and water vapor, while the same gas also helps match blood flow to active tissues. Olsson’s demonstrations and Nestor’s bike tests make the principle concrete: at roughly six breaths per minute, carbon dioxide rose, blood pressure and heart rate fell, and oxygen saturation stayed stable; progressively slower nasal breathing improved Nestor’s cycling distance. The chapter then connects the same rhythm to Buddhist, Hindu, Taoist, Christian, and other prayers, and to the Pavia study in which about 5.5 breaths per minute synchronized heart, circulation, and nervous-system measures. Resonant or Coherent Breathing is presented as a private, portable regulation practice that can resemble meditation or prayer without requiring either. The useful boundary is that slow is not automatically less: a person can breathe slowly while still taking too much air, so timing and volume must be considered separately.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Bohr effect**
  - **When to use:** When oxygen saturation looks adequate but tissue delivery, anxiety, or exercise efficiency is in question.
  - **How:** Maintain enough carbon dioxide through quiet, slower breathing so hemoglobin can release oxygen where metabolism is producing carbon dioxide.
- **Resonant/Coherent Breathing**
  - **When to use:** For regular calming, circulation, or nervous-system coordination.
  - **How:** Inhale softly for about 5.5 seconds and exhale softly for about 5.5 seconds, without a pause, for at least ten cycles.
- **Longer stroke over more strokes**
  - **When to use:** When exercise feels inefficient because respiratory rate rises faster than workload.
  - **How:** Gradually lengthen breaths and observe effort, recovery, and oxygen saturation rather than chasing maximal ventilation.

## Key Concepts

- **Carbon dioxide:** Metabolic gas that helps regulate blood pH, vessel diameter, and oxygen unloading.
- **Bohr effect:** Increased carbon dioxide/acidity encourages hemoglobin to release oxygen.
- **Hemoglobin:** Blood protein that transports oxygen.
- **Alveoli:** Lung bulbs where oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange with blood.
- **Resonant breathing:** Slow breathing near the heart-and-circulation resonance range.
- **Coherence:** Coordinated heart, circulation, and nervous-system function.
- **Aerobic respiration:** Efficient oxygen-dependent energy production.
- **Hypocapnia:** Carbon-dioxide deficiency from excessive ventilation.

## Mental Models

- **Oxygen is cargo; carbon dioxide is the unloading signal:** More air is not the same as more usable oxygen.
- **Rowing rather than splashing:** Fewer, longer respiratory strokes can move the same workload with less wasted effort.
- **Prayer as an inherited breathing technology:** Repeated vocal phrases may preserve a physiological rhythm even when the original mechanism is forgotten.

## Anti-patterns

- **“Breathe as deeply as possible”:** Heavy breathing can purge carbon dioxide and cause light-headedness, cramps, headache, or fainting.
- **Treat an oxygen bar as cellular fuel:** The chapter argues that small rises in arterial oxygen do not necessarily improve tissue delivery.
- **Confuse slow with optimal:** Slow breaths can still have excessive volume; the book’s later “less” chapter supplies the missing volume control.
- **Make the number a performance test:** The 5.5 rhythm is a useful anchor, not a reason to strain or ignore symptoms.

## Worked Example

Olsson breathes at about one-third of the average rate, moving from roughly 18 breaths per minute to six. His exhaled carbon dioxide rises by about 25 percent, while oxygen remains around 97 percent; blood pressure drops several points and heart rate settles. Nestor’s cycling distance then improves from the mouthbreathing baseline by small increments, eventually reaching 7.7 miles in the same thirty-minute session—about a mile farther than the prior week. The chapter presents this as a home-lab demonstration of efficiency, not as a universal athletic promise. The Pavia prayer study and the later work with anxiety, depression, and 9/11 survivors broaden the case, but the book still reports a mixture of small trials, personal data, and historical interpretation.

## Key Takeaways

1. Slow, quiet breathing can preserve carbon dioxide and improve oxygen unloading.
2. Use approximately 5.5 seconds in and 5.5 seconds out as a gentle coherence anchor.
3. Judge a practice by lower effort, steadier recovery, and regulation—not by maximum air intake.
4. Breathing rhythm may explain part of the calming effect of prayer, chanting, and mantra.
5. Keep slow and less separate: the next question is how much air each breath contains.
