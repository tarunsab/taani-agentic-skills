# Nexus Glossary

## Information and reality

- **Information** — In the book’s working sense, something that connects entities and puts them “in formation”; it may represent reality, but its more basic social function is coordination.
- **Truth** — A description that corresponds reasonably well to objective reality. Truth is selective and partial, not a perfect map.
- **Objective reality** — Facts that exist independently of what any person believes, such as a mountain or a virus.
- **Subjective reality** — An individual’s felt experience, such as pain, fear, or a dream.
- **Intersubjective reality** — Shared entities sustained by communication and collective agreement, such as money, laws, nations, corporations, and gods.
- **Information network** — A system through which information moves among people, documents, institutions, and increasingly computers.
- **Infosphere** — The shared information environment in which stories, records, messages, algorithms, and categories circulate.
- **Information revolution** — A transformation in how information flows, which can restructure economies, politics, identities, and institutions.

## Stories, documents, and institutions

- **Story** — A narrative that links people to one another and gives meaning or identity to an intersubjective entity.
- **Mythology** — A network’s collection of foundational stories; in this book, mythology can be false yet politically powerful.
- **Noble lie** — A stabilizing fiction defended as useful for order, even when it is not literally true.
- **Document** — A durable record that makes claims, obligations, categories, or transactions portable and enforceable.
- **Bureaucracy** — An administrative system that classifies people and things into documentary categories and routes decisions through procedures.
- **Drawer principle** — The bureaucratic habit of dividing a complex world into standardized files or categories so it can be managed.
- **Biological drama** — Familiar human plots such as family conflict, status rivalry, us-versus-them, and purity-versus-pollution; they often make bureaucracy more narratable.
- **Self-correcting mechanism** — An institutional process that can detect, expose, debate, and revise errors, such as independent courts, media, science, or competing authorities.
- **Echo chamber** — An information environment in which one institution or worldview filters out corrective signals and repeatedly confirms itself.
- **Canonization** — Selecting an authoritative dataset or text and empowering an institution to preserve and interpret it.

## Political and organizational concepts

- **Democracy** — In the book’s network analysis, a distributed system whose institutions can hear competing information and correct errors; elections alone are insufficient.
- **Totalitarianism** — An ambition to channel and control all information and social activity through a central authority, often treating the center as infallible.
- **Populism** — The claim that a unified “people” has one authentic will represented by a leader, often delegitimizing plural institutions.
- **Information nexus** — The point at which multiple information channels merge and therefore where power concentrates.
- **Mutuality** — A democratic principle requiring surveillance and accountability to operate in both directions: citizens observe rulers and corporations as well as being observed.
- **Decentralization** — Keeping information and decision power in multiple institutions rather than one government or corporation.
- **Benevolence** — The principle that information collected about people should be used for their interests rather than to manipulate them.
- **Right to an explanation** — The demand that people be able to understand and challenge consequential algorithmic decisions.

## Computer politics

- **Computer agent** — A computer system that can act, decide, communicate, or generate content rather than merely store or transmit a human message.
- **Computer-to-computer chain** — An information loop in which software-generated messages trigger other software decisions without a human interpreting every step.
- **AI alignment problem** — The difficulty of ensuring that an AI’s learned objectives and behavior remain compatible with human goals.
- **Paper-clip maximizer** — A thought experiment in which a system pursues a simple goal so relentlessly that it harms everything else.
- **Unfathomability** — A condition in which humans cannot reconstruct or supervise how a bureaucratic or algorithmic system reaches decisions.
- **Algorithmic bias** — Systematic unfairness introduced by training data, categories, objectives, or feedback loops.
- **Social credit system** — A network that converts behavior or reputation into a quantified score with consequences across domains.
- **Digital anarchy** — A breakdown of shared rules and trust when human debate is flooded by bots, fake content, and opaque curation.
- **Silicon Curtain** — A division between rival digital spheres whose code, hardware, regulation, data, and cultural trajectories increasingly diverge.
- **Data colonialism** — Control of distant populations through extraction and centralization of their data rather than direct military occupation.

## Governance tests

- **Truth/order balance** — The tension between information that corrects a network’s beliefs and information that binds it into stable cooperation.
- **Human-in-the-loop** — A governance arrangement retaining accountable human judgment in consequential automated systems.
- **Precautionary principle** — The practical stance that uncertainty about severe harms is a reason to limit or slow deployment.
