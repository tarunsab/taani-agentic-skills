# Chapter 9: Democracies: Can We Still Hold a Conversation?

**Part:** Part III — Computer Politics

## Core Idea

Liberal democracy is valuable because it contains self-correcting mechanisms, but the computer network may make correction either impossible or irrelevant. Harari uses the Industrial Revolution as a warning: societies eventually developed more benevolent industrial institutions, but only after imperialism, totalitarianism, world wars, genocide, and ecological damage. In the digital age, democracy must preserve privacy, plural information channels, mutual accountability, human-readable explanations, and a public conversation not dominated by bots or opaque algorithms. None of the dangers is technologically inevitable, but failure to regulate the network could produce either digital totalitarianism or digital anarchy.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Democracy as a self-correcting information network.
- Four democratic principles for the digital age: benevolence, decentralization, mutuality, and room for change and rest.
- Labor-market volatility as a political risk.
- Unfathomability and the right to an explanation.
- Digital anarchy and the regulation of bots.

## Key Concepts

- **Benevolence:** data access should serve the person’s interests rather than manipulate the person.
- **Decentralization:** no government or corporation should possess every relevant database and channel.
- **Mutuality:** surveillance of citizens should be balanced by transparency about governments and corporations.
- **Unfathomability:** humans cannot supervise what they cannot understand.
- **Digital anarchy:** free conversation loses its democratic function when shared rules, facts, and trust collapse.

## Source Sections

### The Democratic Way

The industrial era eventually showed that liberal democracy, despite its flaws, could learn from the disasters of imperialism and totalitarianism better than more rigid systems. The reason is not moral perfection but strong self-correction. A computer network is too new and unpredictable to manage by confident forecasts, so preserving correction is the safest general strategy. The author’s digital principles are the following:

- **Benevolence:** if a physician, lawyer, or therapist collects sensitive information, professional duty requires using it for the client’s benefit; data-heavy platforms should face an analogous fiduciary expectation. Services could be paid for in money or provided as public infrastructure rather than financed by unrestricted extraction of personal information.
- **Decentralization:** separate medical, police, banking, insurance, and other databases even when merging them would be efficient. Some inefficiency protects liberty, and multiple independent channels let institutions check one another.
- **Mutuality:** if governments and corporations know more about individuals, individuals must also know more about the agencies that monitor them. Algorithms should expose corruption, tax evasion, lobbying, and political influence as well as rank citizens.
- **Change and rest:** surveillance must not freeze identity into caste or racial categories, and it must not monitor people without pause. People need privacy, the ability to change, and time outside constant measurement.

These principles are constraints on power, not a promise that privacy and safety can be perfectly maximized at once. The practical test is whether an information system makes its tradeoffs visible and leaves room for correction.

### The Pace of Democracy

Automation may destabilize democracy through labor-market turmoil even if it does not eliminate all jobs. The Weimar Republic moved from less than 3 percent Nazi support in 1928 to Hitler’s dictatorship within five years of the financial crisis, while unemployment approached 25 percent. Earlier automation repeatedly destroyed jobs and created others, so the likely problem is continual retraining, financial insecurity, and psychological stress rather than a jobless endpoint. The difficulty is that nobody knows which skills will matter: chess proved easier to automate than busy-restaurant dishwashing, pattern-recognition doctors may be easier to replace than nurses, and computers may outperform humans in some forms of creativity and emotional analysis.

AI can identify emotional patterns without having emotions, and studies discussed in the book found ChatGPT more emotionally aware than average humans in some tests and more empathic than unpaid doctors in an online-advice comparison. Yet people may want a relationship with a conscious being, not merely a correct response; sports, chess, and religious ceremonies retain human value partly because participants feel and care. Even that distinction may erode if humans treat chatbots as conscious or grant them legal-person status. The political risk is therefore a volatile sequence of job loss, retraining, and reinvention, not simply a final replacement event.

### The Conservative Suicide

Traditional conservatism is a commitment to pace: preserve institutions that work and change them cautiously because social systems are more complex than any one reformer understands. The book argues that several conservative parties in the 2010s and early 2020s, especially the U.S. Republican Party under Donald Trump, became revolutionary by attacking elections, scientists, civil servants, and the established order. The storming of the U.S. Capitol is presented as the opposite of Edmund Burke’s horror at the storming of the Bastille. This transformation forced progressives to defend institutions they once wanted to overhaul.

Rapid technological and economic change may make moderate conservation seem impossible, encouraging a right-wing revolution to preempt a left-wing one. But the different outcomes of the Great Depression in Germany and the United States show that economic shock is mediated by institutional depth: Roosevelt’s New Deal changed U.S. democracy without destroying it. Democracies that remain loyal to correction can adapt, as the United States, Japan, and Italy did during the computer revolution. Flexibility—including abandoning myths that waste human potential, as the entry of women into the workforce demonstrates—is a strategic asset.

### Unfathomable

Democratic correction fails when citizens, judges, journalists, and lawmakers cannot understand the system they must supervise. Human bureaucracies could be cruel or biased but remained composed of people whose interests and prejudices could be investigated; the Brown v. Board of Education case illustrates how racial motives could be identified and legally challenged. An algorithmic social-credit score might encode a new category that no human can recognize or correct. The COMPAS risk-assessment case makes the problem concrete: Eric Loomis was sentenced partly on an opaque proprietary assessment, and neither he nor the court could inspect the methodology or fully evaluate its possible data bias.

The Wisconsin Supreme Court permitted the use of COMPAS while advising judges to be cautious, but supplied no workable account of what caution meant. An opaque system can therefore receive a formal warning without acquiring real accountability. The issue is not only secrecy; complex learned patterns may remain unintelligible even to their creators. Democracies need institutions and narratives that make computer bureaucracy comprehensible enough to contest.

### The Right to an Explanation

Algorithms increasingly influence prison sentences, education, employment, welfare, loans, medical care, insurance, news, and dating. The European Union’s GDPR is discussed as a model for a right to an explanation and a human authority able to hear a challenge. But AlphaGo’s famous move 37 shows why explanation is difficult: the move won, revealed a region of go that human tradition had not explored, and remained opaque even to the engineers who built the system. A black-box decision may be alien because it weighs thousands of data points and discovers patterns no individual can reconstruct.

The imagined bank letter illustrates both sides. A smartphone model, battery level, and hundreds of other features may affect the score, yet one salient reason cannot explain the aggregate result. Algorithms can sometimes outperform humans precisely because humans rely on a few conscious reasons while subconscious biases influence many others. Expert teams using computers can audit an algorithm more systematically than one can audit a human judge, but this creates a recursive institutional problem: the auditor also needs oversight. There is no purely technological solution; independent bureaucratic bodies must certify, reject, and explain algorithmic systems.

### Nosedive

The Black Mirror episode “Nosedive” makes social-credit dynamics narratable. Lacie tries to raise her score from 4.2 to 4.5 so she can afford an apartment, courts high-score friends, suffers cascading penalties after a series of mishaps, and ends in jail with a score below 1. Traditional elements such as sibling tension and status competition remain, but the algorithm becomes the true driver and removes the breaks that once interrupted status anxiety. Art can therefore help citizens understand computer bureaucracy when political language and technical documents cannot.

The EU AI Act’s prohibition on certain social-credit systems is offered as an example of democratic choice, not proof that every such system is impossible. Regulation must be paired with independent technical scrutiny and stories that reveal how the system changes behavior. Otherwise people may replace institutional trust with conspiracy theories and charismatic leaders.

### Digital Anarchy

Democracy needs both free public conversation and enough order and trust to reach legitimate decisions. Social media weakened older gatekeepers such as newspapers and parties, making participation more open while also requiring new rules. Bots add a new problem because nonhuman voices can join the conversation: studies cited in the book estimate that bots generated a significant share of election-era tweets and that humans were more likely to trust AI-produced disinformation than human-produced disinformation in one experiment.

Generative bots can mass-produce persuasive arguments, deepfakes, and simulated friendship, learning the vulnerabilities of the people they influence even though they do not vote or care about politics. Algorithms also orchestrate which human voices are amplified, making public rules subject to inscrutable machine choices. If citizens cannot distinguish people from bots or agree on basic facts and debate rules, free speech turns into anarchy. A likely response to such collapse would be a demand for dictatorship in exchange for certainty.

### Ban the Bots

Daniel Dennett’s analogy is to counterfeiting money: societies protect trust in currency by outlawing counterfeit notes, so they might protect trust in humans by outlawing nonhuman agents that pretend to be human. The proposed rule would prohibit deepfakes of real people and, more broadly, deceptive human impersonation by bots, while allowing clearly identified AI doctors and other helpful agents to participate. The argument is that a bot has no human right to speech that is violated by disclosure or exclusion. This proposal has difficult implementation questions and must be distinguished from censoring genuine human views.

The book also proposes that unsupervised algorithms should not set the rules of key public debates. Platforms may use algorithms for scale, but human institutions should vet the principles that silence, amplify, or deliberately provoke. Transparency about curation, business models, and political connections is a minimum. Regulation will age quickly as technology changes, yet the historical record shows that every democratic venue—from town halls to newspapers—has required rules.

### The Future of Democracy

Large-scale democracy was once impossible because communication was too slow; it may now become impossible if communication is too sophisticated for humans to understand. The breakdown of shared facts and bipartisan conversation in the United States, along with similar polarization in the Philippines and Brazil, shows that the warning is already practical rather than purely futuristic. Ideological conflict existed in the 1960s, but institutions then retained more common ground about elections, courts, and some legislative cooperation. The cause of current breakdown is uncertain, and that uncertainty itself reflects an opaque network whose algorithmic interactions humans cannot fully explain.

The future is not predetermined by computers, but a democracy that cannot discover what is broken cannot repair itself. The central policy challenge is therefore to make the network legible, plural, accountable, and bounded before people trade liberty for order.

## Mental Models

1. **Four-guardrail audit:** test benevolence, decentralization, mutuality, and change/rest.
2. **Conversation health:** assess shared facts, rules of debate, willingness to listen, and acceptance of correction.
3. **Explanation ladder:** distinguish a list of inputs from an intelligible account of how the system found its pattern.
4. **Bot transparency:** treat human/nonhuman identity as part of the truth conditions of public speech.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating elections as sufficient evidence of democratic health.
- Solving safety by merging every database into one efficient center.
- Promising an explanation that merely prints thousands of features without human meaning.
- Allowing platforms to hide curation choices behind “the algorithm.”

## Worked Example

For a public-benefits AI, publish its purpose, data sources, decision categories, known error rates, appeal route, and responsible institution. Keep welfare data separate from policing and commercial profiles, permit independent auditors to compare the system with outcomes, and give recipients time and privacy outside the scoring process. If bots participate in the policy debate, label them and prevent them from masquerading as citizens.

## Key Takeaways

- Democracy’s best defense is not prediction but self-correction.
- Digital surveillance should be benevolent, decentralized, mutual, and bounded by rest and change.
- Automation threatens democracy through continual transition and insecurity.
- Unfathomable algorithms undermine accountability even when they are accurate.
- Public conversation needs rules for bots, curation, transparency, and shared facts.
