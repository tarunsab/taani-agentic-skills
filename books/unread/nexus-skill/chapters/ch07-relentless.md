# Chapter 7: Relentless: The Network Is Always On

**Part:** Part II — The Inorganic Network

## Core Idea

Human surveillance has always been ambivalent: it can oppress, but it can also find a missing child, prevent a crime, or deliver public services. Digital networks transform surveillance by placing powerful agents in everyone’s pocket, allowing them to record, compare, and infer continuously. The Romanian Securitate’s tens of thousands of agents and hundreds of thousands of informers already produced more reports than humans could read; digital systems remove much of that bottleneck. The danger is not only loss of privacy but the removal of rest, change, and the human gaps that once limited coercion. A network that never sleeps can make status and punishment permanent while overwhelming the people expected to govern it.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Human limits as a brake on surveillance.
- Under-the-skin inference and behavioral prediction.
- Peer-to-peer surveillance and surveillance capitalism.
- Quantified reputation and social credit.
- Rest and change as conditions of freedom.

## Key Concepts

- **Sleepless agent:** a digital system that observes and acts continuously without human fatigue.
- **Under-the-skin surveillance:** inference about emotions, intentions, or bodily states from biometric and behavioral data.
- **Surveillance capitalism:** extraction of behavioral data for prediction, ranking, and commercial control.
- **Social credit:** converting reputation into a score that travels across social and institutional domains.

## Source Sections

### Sleepless Agents

Computers can process a person’s digital traces far faster than a human investigator and can compare them with millions of other lives. Eye tracking, biometric sensors, medical devices, and future brain interfaces could allow systems to infer not just what someone says but what they feel or intend. Harari treats the smartphone as an already powerful compromise between convenience and privacy, while noting that under-the-skin interfaces could deepen the asymmetry. The capacity to know more about a person than the person knows about themselves changes the meaning of consent.

### Under-the-Skin Surveillance

Surveillance becomes more intrusive when algorithms infer emotional or physiological states rather than merely recording visible actions. A system that detects fear, attraction, fatigue, or ideological commitment could manipulate a person before they consciously recognize the state. These inferences are also probabilistic and fallible, so a confident score can turn an uncertain pattern into an official identity. The more consequential the inference, the stronger the need for contestability and limits on secondary use.

### The End of Privacy

Cameras, spyware, facial recognition, and voice analysis expand observation into streets, homes, and phones. The book uses the FBI’s data fusion after January 6, the facial-recognition search for Gui Hao, and Brøndby stadium’s monitoring to show how dispersed images can become actionable identity records. In Iran, AI-assisted enforcement of hijab rules turned SMS notices, vehicle immobilization, arrests, and legal referrals into a large automated punishment network after the death of Mahsa Amini. The issue is not merely whether one camera is accurate, but whether a state can combine countless traces into continuous control.

### Varieties of Surveillance

Surveillance is not a single state-versus-citizen relation. Domestic abusers can use stalkerware, employers can monitor workers, platforms can rank attention, and citizens can watch one another. Some forms genuinely improve safety or service delivery, while others create coercive dependence. Evaluate the purpose, direction, visibility, and reversibility of each system instead of treating “surveillance” as uniformly good or bad.

### The Social Credit System

Money already converts resources into a portable score, but reputation is more diffuse and socially meaningful. A social-credit system attempts to quantify trustworthiness and attach the number to loans, travel, employment, education, or status. The official rationale may be anti-corruption or safer exchange, yet comprehensive scoring can merge many status competitions into one never-ending race and make privacy impossible. Once a score follows a person everywhere, opting out becomes nearly as difficult as refusing money.

### Always On

Human communities have pauses: people sleep, leave the market, stop watching one another, and forget some offenses. A digital network can remain active through the night, update a score instantly, and preserve every interaction. Constant measurement can therefore intensify small errors and prevent the reflective distance needed to correct them. Freedom requires not only limits on what a network knows but protected periods in which the network is not judging.

## Mental Models

1. **Rest test:** ask what happens when a person is offline, tired, changing, or temporarily unreachable.
2. **Inference ladder:** distinguish recorded behavior, inferred state, predicted intent, and imposed punishment.
3. **Direction test:** measure whether citizens can inspect and challenge the agencies that inspect them.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating more observation as automatically more safety.
- Confusing a biometric prediction with a fact about a person.
- Combining databases merely because doing so increases efficiency.
- Designing reputation systems without an exit, appeal, or forgetting mechanism.

## Worked Example

For a workplace monitoring program, separate necessary safety data from behavioral profiles, restrict retention, tell workers what is inferred, and provide an independent appeal route. Do not let a fatigue or sentiment score silently determine promotion or dismissal. Preserve off-hours and the ability to change roles without a permanent algorithmic identity.

## Key Takeaways

- Digital agents remove the human fatigue and scale limits that once constrained surveillance.
- Under-the-skin inference can turn uncertain predictions into social facts.
- Surveillance can come from governments, corporations, peers, and intimate partners.
- Social credit systems make reputation portable, quantified, and difficult to escape.
- Privacy, rest, change, and mutual accountability are democratic safeguards.
