# Chapter 1: What Is Information?

**Part:** Part I — Human Networks

## Core Idea

The book does not attempt a universal definition of information for physics, biology, or mathematics. Its historical question is what information does in human networks: it connects entities, coordinates behavior, and creates new realities, while only sometimes representing the world accurately. Truth is a selective description of objective reality, not a perfect copy, and all accounts omit something. The crucial warning is that connectivity can be powerful without being truthful, so a successful network must be judged by both the relationships it creates and the mechanisms it uses to test its claims.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Objective, subjective, and intersubjective reality.
- Information as connection and formation.
- The distinction between representation, coordination, and creation.
- The difference between connectivity and truth.

## Key Concepts

- **Objective fact:** something that exists independently of belief.
- **Subjective fact:** an individual’s experience or interpretation.
- **Intersubjective fact:** a shared entity sustained by communication and agreement.
- **Selective truth:** an account can be more or less accurate without containing everything.

## Source Sections

### What Is Truth?

Harari begins with cases in which information saves lives: the pigeon Cher Ami carrying a message from the Lost Battalion, Noah’s dove and its olive branch, sailors reading stars, and the NILI network using shutters and pigeons. These examples invite the idea that information is valuable because it represents a state of affairs accurately. Yet the story of Borges’s map—a map as detailed as the territory would be useless—shows why truth must select and simplify. A map, a historical account, or a message is more truthful when it preserves relevant structure without pretending to reproduce reality in full.

### What Information Does

Information’s more fundamental function is connection: it puts people, objects, or processes into formation. Astrology can connect celestial patterns to human decisions; music can coordinate feeling without describing a fact; DNA and adrenaline carry instructions inside organisms; and a shared belief can connect strangers even when its claims are false. The moon landing and conspiracy theories demonstrate that rival descriptions can organize different communities around the same event. The result is a warning against defining information only as representation: information can create a social reality by changing what connected actors do.

### Information in Human History

Human history is full of information that is factually doubtful yet socially consequential. The Bible may contain historical or scientific errors, but the stories, texts, and institutions around it have connected billions of people into religious networks. The fundamental feature of such information is not that it is true but that it links humans and enables coordinated action. Truth requires special effort and institutions because an efficient network can be built around a false story, as Nazi and Stalinist networks demonstrate.

## Mental Models

1. **Three-reality test:** before debating a claim, identify whether it concerns objective conditions, subjective experience, or an intersubjective agreement.
2. **Function-before-fact:** ask what a message or category enables people to do before asking whether it mirrors reality.
3. **Map discipline:** seek selective, useful accuracy rather than the fantasy of a complete description.

## Anti-patterns

- Equating information with truth by definition.
- Assuming that a widely shared belief is therefore objectively true.
- Confusing a network’s ability to coordinate with its ability to reason correctly.
- Treating one accurate message as proof that an entire information system is reliable.

## Worked Example

For a workplace performance score, separate the observable work from the score and from the behavior the score creates. The underlying work may be objective, the employee’s stress subjective, and the ranking system intersubjective. Even if the score predicts one outcome, it can still create a new status hierarchy and distort behavior through the connections it establishes.

## Key Takeaways

- Information should be studied by its effects in networks, not only by its representational accuracy.
- Truth is selective and can be improved, but no account is a one-to-one copy of reality.
- Objective, subjective, and intersubjective facts operate differently.
- A network can be highly connected and highly wrong.
- Truth-seeking must be designed as a special activity inside information systems.
