# Chapter 11: The Silicon Curtain: Global Empire or Global Split?

**Part:** Part III — Computer Politics

## Core Idea

AI is a global problem because a reckless state, corporation, terrorist group, or dictator can create harms that cross borders. The new computer network could centralize information into a few digital empires, producing data colonies, or it could split the world into rival digital spheres separated by a Silicon Curtain. Different code, hardware, rules, and identities could make cooperation harder just when AI, cyberwar, climate change, and biological threats require it most. Harari’s constructive alternative is neither a single empire nor cultural sameness, but global rules that preserve national differences while limiting technologies capable of harming everyone.

## Frameworks Introduced

- AI risk as a global rather than national network problem.
- Digital empire and data colonialism.
- Silicon Curtain and information cocoons.
- Mind–body politics in rival digital cultures.
- Cyberwar escalation and the limits of deterrence.
- Global cooperation as shared rules plus long-term self-preservation.

## Key Concepts

- **Digital empire:** a power center that controls data, algorithms, infrastructure, and attention across borders.
- **Data colonialism:** extraction of raw data from dependent populations while high-value models and profits remain in the hub.
- **Silicon Curtain:** a code-, hardware-, and regulation-based split between digital spheres.
- **Information cocoon:** a closed digital environment whose users develop divergent realities and identities.
- **Global bond:** cooperation among different nations through communication and shared rules.

## Source Sections

### The Rise of Digital Empires

A single society’s choices can no longer contain AI risk: a dictator could give an AI authority over nuclear weapons, a terrorist group could use it to design and spread a pathogen, or an information attack could flood other countries with fake news, money, and humans. Small states such as Qatar, Tonga, Tuvalu, Kiribati, and the Solomon Islands currently retain leverage in a postimperial system because power is distributed among many players. AI could reverse that distribution by rewarding the states and corporations able to collect the most data and run the strongest models. The resulting empire might be more centralized than industrial empires because information travels rapidly and algorithms occupy little space.

### Data Colonialism

Industrial empires extracted cotton, rubber, and other raw materials while keeping high-value manufacturing in the imperial hub. Data colonialism would extract personal histories, behavioral traces, medical records, images, and attention, then return finished algorithms to populations that do not control them. Digital data can travel almost at the speed of light, unlike land, oil, or factories, so one hub could concentrate a decisive share of algorithmic power. Poorer states may lose unskilled jobs to automation and lack the resources to retrain workers, while leading AI powers capture most of the gains.

The book uses China and North America’s projected share of future AI gains, and the dependence of Pakistan and Bangladesh on textile employment and exports, to illustrate the distributional risk. A platform can also colonize attention and reputation: a foreign social-credit score might affect flights, visas, jobs, scholarships, and local trust. Blocking Facebook, YouTube, TikTok, WeChat, or other apps is therefore not only censorship policy; it is a struggle over sovereignty and control of a country’s information flows.

### From Web to Cocoon

The Silicon Curtain is made of code running through phones, servers, software, and hardware. China and the United States already use different platforms, regulations, and political priorities: Chinese systems serve state goals and support wider surveillance, while U.S. systems are led more by private firms and offer stronger protections for some offline privacy. Hardware restrictions, chip-export controls, and competing infrastructure can make the two spheres technically incompatible. As code shapes behavior and behavior reshapes code, digital divergence may become cultural and political divergence.

The web once symbolized increasing connection, but separate information cocoons could end a shared human reality. Qataris, Tongans, or Tuvaluans may live under a foreign digital sphere whose rules shape their identities and opportunities. A split may reduce some risks of a single empire, yet it also makes mutual understanding and common regulation more difficult.

### The Global Mind-Body Split

Digital cocoons may disagree about whether identity is grounded in a biological body or an online mind and avatar. Harari traces this through Jewish and early Christian embodiment, later Christian dualism, ascetic practices, and Luther’s emphasis on faith rather than bodily self-torment. The same conflict could reappear in debates about virtual work, online identity, AI personhood, ecological responsibility, and whether material infrastructure matters. A sphere that downplays bodies may accept disembodied AIs as persons and neglect sewage, forests, and other physical supports; a body-centered sphere may do the opposite.

These are explicitly speculative scenarios, not forecasts. Their value is to show that digital separation could produce incompatible concepts of personhood and rights, making future disputes about AI status or avatars as consequential as earlier religious conflicts. If two spheres cannot understand each other’s basic identity categories, ordinary human-rights and environmental negotiations may become much harder.

### From Code War to Hot War

Cyber weapons are more versatile and stealthy than nuclear bombs: they can target grids, research facilities, elections, scandals, sensors, or a single phone without a visible launch trail. Attribution is uncertain, so limited attacks are tempting and escalation can proceed without a clear red line. Cyber conflict also lacks nuclear deterrence’s relative predictability because neither side knows which malware, logic bombs, or compromised command systems will function.

An actor that believes a temporary advantage is slipping away may launch a first strike, making the arms race especially dangerous. Even without world war, rival digital empires could repress their populations, block ecological cooperation, and resist AI regulation. The shift from code war to hot war is therefore a systems risk produced by opacity and miscalculation, not only by hostile intent.

### The Global Bond

Cooperation does not require nations to become identical; it requires the ability to exchange information and discover shared interests. Patriotism and global cooperation can reinforce one another because a pandemic, like an out-of-control AI, threatens compatriots through events elsewhere. Rejecting a foreign vaccine merely because it is foreign would be anti-patriotic when the aim is to protect one’s own citizens. The same reasoning applies to sharing AI safety information.

Globalism in this account means two modest commitments: nations accept common rules for their interactions, and they sometimes place long-term human survival above short-term national advantage. The World Cup illustrates both: teams remain national competitors but agree on shared rules and ban performance-enhancing drugs that would destroy the competition. States may continue competing in AI while agreeing to limit autonomous weapons and manipulative algorithms for mutual self-preservation.

### The Human Choice

AI agreements will be harder to verify than nuclear agreements because laboratories are easier to hide and civilian and military applications overlap. A mail-delivery or pesticide drone could be modified into an autonomous weapon, so rivals may doubt one another’s compliance and be tempted to defect. Realist theories that treat states as seeking maximum power resemble populist and Marxist accounts that reduce all relations to domination, but the book counters with cooperation in biological ecosystems and the long human expansion from bands to tribes, religions, trade networks, and states.

War is shaped by technology, economics, and culture rather than fixed human nature. Nuclear weapons raised the price of great-power war; knowledge-based economies reduced the gains from conquering land and raw materials; post-1945 cultures became less enthusiastic about imperial conquest. State budgets shifted from overwhelmingly military spending toward health and education, although the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine and rising military budgets show that the change is reversible.

Historical narratives guide leaders’ choices, so pessimistic stories can become self-fulfilling. Putin’s belief that Ukraine was an artificial enemy project helped prioritize conquest over health care or global AI cooperation. The author’s final claim is that conflict is not inevitable, but precisely because alternatives exist, people bear responsibility for choosing institutions that keep power in human hands.

## Mental Models

1. **Global-externality test:** ask whether a local AI failure can cross borders through data, software, weapons, markets, or pathogens.
2. **Empire/cocoon fork:** determine whether centralization or incompatibility is the dominant risk.
3. **Code-to-culture loop:** trace how software and hardware shape norms, identity, and political institutions.
4. **Deterrence audit:** test attribution, predictability, escalation windows, and incentives for first strike.
5. **Patriotic cooperation:** frame shared rules as protection of citizens, not abandonment of national identity.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming wise national regulation can contain a global AI externality.
- Treating data as a neutral resource rather than a source of sovereignty and bargaining power.
- Equating global cooperation with one empire or one culture.
- Assuming post-1945 reductions in war are permanent laws of history.

## Worked Example

For a cross-border AI standard, identify dual-use capabilities, verification gaps, data dependencies, and the consequences of a rival’s defection. Preserve national control over legitimate local choices while requiring shared rules for deceptive bots, autonomous weapons, and dangerous biological design. Build channels for technical information exchange and independent auditing so that uncertainty does not automatically become a first-strike incentive.

## Key Takeaways

- AI risk crosses borders and cannot be solved by domestic policy alone.
- Data and algorithms can create a more concentrated form of empire.
- Rival digital spheres may generate incompatible realities and identities.
- Cyberwar is opaque, versatile, and difficult to deter through nuclear-style certainty.
- Global cooperation is compatible with patriotism when common rules protect all communities.
- Human history supports change and cooperation, but neither is guaranteed.
