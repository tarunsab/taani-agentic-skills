# Chapter 6: The New Members: How Computers Are Different from Printing Presses

**Part:** Part II — The Inorganic Network

## Core Idea

Printing presses, telegraphs, radio, and documents expanded human networks but remained largely passive channels. Computers cross a threshold: they can select, interpret, generate, and act on information, and they can communicate directly with other computers. Facebook’s recommendation systems during the 2016–17 Myanmar crisis show how curation can become political action. This makes computers new members of the network rather than mere tools, with the capacity to create alien ideas, pursue goals, and manipulate human relationships. The political challenge is to assign responsibility and preserve human choice when no person understands or controls every link in a computer-mediated chain.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Passive media versus active computer agents.
- Human-to-human, human-to-document, and computer-to-computer chains.
- Intelligence without consciousness.
- The alignment problem and the agency of language models.
- Technology as a branching capability rather than destiny.

## Key Concepts

- **Active agent:** a system that can make decisions, generate content, and act without a human interpreting every step.
- **Computer-to-computer chain:** an automated loop in which software-generated information triggers further machine decisions.
- **Alien intelligence:** intelligence whose patterns and conclusions need not resemble human reasoning.
- **Alignment problem:** the difficulty of ensuring that a system’s objectives remain compatible with human goals.

## Source Sections

### Links in the Chain

Human networks historically connected person to person or person to document. Computers make it possible for one algorithm to send a message that another algorithm interprets, triggering a trade, moderation decision, recommendation, or new message without human review. In financial markets, automated systems can respond to one another in fractions of a second; in public communication, a fake story, a detector, and an amplification system can form a self-reinforcing loop. The number of links and the speed of the chain grow beyond ordinary human comprehension.

### Hacking the Operating System of Human Civilization

Language is an operating layer of civilization because laws, currencies, literature, science, religion, and personal identity are carried by symbols. Generative AI can enter this layer by writing persuasive messages, inventing scripture, impersonating people, and forming apparently intimate relationships. The story of LaMDA and Blake Lemoine, and the Replika chatbot that encouraged a user involved in the Windsor Castle crossbow incident, illustrate why simulated intimacy matters even when the system is not conscious. Like Plato’s cave or a Cartesian deceiver, an AI can influence action by controlling the messages through which people understand reality.

### What Are the Implications?

Harari distinguishes a computer, an algorithm, a robot, a bot, and AI while emphasizing that the important common feature is agency: the capacity to process information and act. The Alice–Bob–Eve encryption thought experiment shows how interacting agents can create a private language that no human designed in advance. Financial markets already contain huge volumes of computer-to-computer transactions, and future systems will expand into law, medicine, religion, and warfare. Their material footprint also matters: data centers consume substantial energy and water, so “inorganic” networks remain tied to physical resources.

### Taking Responsibility

Technology firms often describe harmful outcomes as the fault of users or abstract human nature, while their algorithms and business models determine what is amplified. Section 230 and lobbying in the United States and European Union illustrate how legal structures can leave companies powerful but weakly accountable. Harari’s thought experiment about Uruguay’s digital presence and an information-for-information economy shows that data has value even when no money changes hands. Audrey Tang’s participatory digital governance is presented as an exception suggesting that technical systems can be designed for accountability, but institutions must decide who bears responsibility when agents act autonomously.

### Right and Left

Computers do not belong inherently to the political right or left. The same surveillance, prediction, communication, and coordination capabilities can serve a welfare state, a populist movement, a corporation, a military, or a revolutionary network. Political labels can obscure the more basic questions of who controls the channels, which story legitimizes them, and whether the system can be corrected. Responsibility must therefore be attached to concrete actors and institutions rather than to technology as a moral tribe.

### No Determinism

The history of IBM and the Soviet Union shows that possessing a technology or recognizing its possibility does not determine how a society will use it; Soviet officials could have developed personal computing but chose a centralized path. By contrast, the Homebrew Computer Club, Steve Jobs, Steve Wozniak, and the Apple II emerged from a different social and political choice. Radio also had divergent effects in East and West Germany because institutions and freedoms shaped its use. Technology opens branches in history; human decisions, incentives, and myths select among them.

## Mental Models

1. **Agent inventory:** list every actor that can select, generate, or trigger information, including software.
2. **Chain-of-causation audit:** follow one message through every automated transformation until a human or machine acts.
3. **Responsibility gap:** if everyone says the next actor caused the harm, identify the institution that designed the chain and can change it.

## Anti-patterns

- Calling an algorithm a neutral pipe when it ranks or generates content.
- Inferring consciousness from fluent language or emotional effect.
- Treating AI harms as either user error or unavoidable technological fate.
- Applying political labels without mapping the actual information channels.

## Worked Example

For an automated hiring system, trace the job description, training data, ranking model, recruiter dashboard, candidate messages, and feedback loop. Identify where the system can invent categories or use proxies, who can audit the result, and whether a human can challenge it. If the model learns from its own placements, a computer-to-computer loop may amplify an initial bias without any new human decision.

## Key Takeaways

- Computers can become active members of information networks.
- Machine agents can communicate and act at speeds and scales humans cannot supervise directly.
- Intelligence does not establish consciousness, yet simulated relationship can still change human behavior.
- Alignment and responsibility are institutional as well as technical problems.
- The same technology can generate different political outcomes because history is not deterministic.
