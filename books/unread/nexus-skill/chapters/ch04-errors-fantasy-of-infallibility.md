# Chapter 4: Errors: The Fantasy of Infallibility

**Part:** Part I — Human Networks

## Core Idea

Every information network is fallible, but large networks often protect their authority by claiming access to an infallible source. Religious canons, bureaucratic expertise, political leaders, and scientific systems each develop different ways to manage the conflict between a fixed authority and changing evidence. Printing and literacy can multiply both corrective knowledge and destructive myths, as the history of witch hunts demonstrates. The durable solution is not a perfect text or a perfect machine but institutions that admit error, expose their methods, and make revision possible.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Infallibility claims as a response to human fallibility.
- Canonization, interpretation, and institutional authority.
- Echo chambers and the self-sealing information network.
- Science as an institutionalized self-correcting mechanism.
- The limits of correction when order is threatened.

## Key Concepts

- **Infallibility fantasy:** the belief that a person, text, party, or technology cannot be wrong.
- **Canon:** a selected authoritative dataset whose boundaries and interpretation are institutionally managed.
- **Self-correction:** organized error detection, criticism, replication, and revision.
- **Echo chamber:** a network that converts authority into repeated confirmation by filtering dissent.

## Source Sections

### Taking Humans Out of the Loop

The Baining example of Tanotka and Baninge shows how a community can rely on a spirit messenger and still receive a human interpretation of its message. Greek oracles likewise appeared superhuman while remaining vulnerable to human mediation and bribery. Removing a visible human from the communication chain can hide fallibility rather than eliminate it. A system becomes safer only when the human and institutional steps remain inspectable.

### The Infallible Technology

Sacred books promised a technology that could preserve an authoritative message unchanged, bypassing the unreliability of memory and human messengers. Yet a fixed text immediately creates compilation, copying, translation, and interpretation problems. The apparent solution to human error therefore shifts power toward the people who decide what belongs in the text and how it should be read. Infallibility is often a claim about the network’s authority, not a demonstrated property of the artifact.

### The Making of the Hebrew Bible

The Dead Sea Scrolls reveal a world of competing Jewish texts, including works such as Enoch that later communities treated differently from Genesis. The Septuagint and later Masoretic tradition show that copying and translation generated variation before the canon acquired a more settled form. Rabbis and scribes gradually selected, preserved, and interpreted the authoritative corpus. The attempt to democratize revelation by fixing it in writing consequently created a powerful curatorial institution.

### The Institution Strikes Back

Once a text is authoritative, readers still need decisions about ambiguous commands, variant copies, and conflicts between passages. Rabbinic interpretation and the Talmud supplied procedures for arguing about questions such as the meaning of Sabbath rules or the prohibition on cooking a kid in its mother’s milk. The text did not remove human judgment; it moved judgment into an interpretive institution. The institution could protect a living tradition, but it could also monopolize access to truth.

### The Split Bible

Christianity rejected the authority of the rabbis while needing to decide which writings counted as the New Testament. Athanasius and the councils at Hippo and Carthage helped establish a canon among competing texts and communities. The contrast between 1 Timothy’s restrictive teaching and the more empowering Acts of Paul and Thecla shows how canon choices can shape gender roles for centuries. A new scripture can therefore create a new network, but its curators inherit the old problem of fallibility.

### The Echo Chamber

Medieval churches controlled much of the copying and circulation of books, which allowed one institution to police heresy and reinforce its own interpretation. The Sermon on the Mount could be read as a call to peace, yet the same Christian information network also justified crusades and inquisitions. Sparse books made institutional filtering especially powerful. When a network hears mainly its own authority, it can become certain precisely because corrective signals have been excluded.

### Print, Science, and Witches

Printing greatly increased the number and speed of available texts: millions of volumes appeared in the decades after Gutenberg, compared with the far smaller hand-copied output of the preceding millennium. But print was not inherently scientific; it also spread the Malleus Maleficarum and theories of organized witch conspiracies. A new medium expands the reach of whatever stories and institutions use it. More copies can therefore multiply error and persecution alongside discovery.

### The Spanish Inquisition to the Rescue

Witch-hunts killed tens of thousands of people across Europe, with especially severe episodes in places such as Bamberg and Würzburg. Torture, rumor, and printed stories created an intersubjective reality in which imaginary witches appeared to threaten society. Alonso de Salazar Frías investigated the claims in Spain and found that careful conversation, writing, and evidence undermined the supposed conspiracy. The case shows that a truth-seeking official can emerge inside a coercive institution, but only if the network permits doubt.

### The Discovery of Ignorance

The scientific revolution depended on recognizing that inherited authorities could be wrong and that important questions might have no accepted answer. Ignorance became a legitimate starting point for investigation rather than a defect to conceal. This shift made it possible to replace sacred certainty with provisional models, experiments, and public criticism. Science did not eliminate mythology or error; it built a stronger social method for exposing them.

### Self-Correcting Mechanisms

Scientific institutions distribute correction across observations, rival researchers, peer criticism, replication, and public records. No scientist is presumed infallible, so a claim can be revised without requiring the whole network to collapse. This differs from a canon that treats disagreement as heresy or a party that treats criticism as treason. Self-correction is an institutional achievement, not an automatic consequence of possessing facts.

### The DSM and the Bible

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual demonstrates that even a professional classification can be revised: homosexuality was removed from the DSM in 1974 after changes in evidence and professional judgment. The comparison with the Bible highlights different correction architectures. A scientific manual can formally change its categories, while a sacred canon usually relies on reinterpretation or abandonment because its text is not designed for amendment. The point is not that science is bias-free, but that it makes revision more visible and legitimate.

### Publish or Perish

The history of Dan Shechtman’s quasicrystals illustrates how a disputed finding can move from ridicule to acceptance through evidence and replication. Scientific incentives can produce conformity, career pressure, and bad research, yet the norm of publishing results exposes claims to a wider community. A network can therefore contain both error-generating incentives and correction-generating procedures. The relevant test is whether the latter can eventually outweigh the former.

### The Limits of Self-Correction

Correction can threaten the order and identity of a network, so institutions often resist it when the cost of admitting error is high. Science functions partly because other institutions maintain social order while scientists revise theories; a laboratory cannot itself supply every political safeguard. Even a strong method can be captured, underfunded, or excluded from consequential decisions. The next political question is who controls the channels that allow correction to reach power.

## Mental Models

1. **Authority migration:** when a network removes one human source of error, inspect which institution inherits interpretive power.
2. **Correction budget:** the more a network can do, the more independent error detection it needs.
3. **Echo-chamber test:** identify which dissenting signals cannot enter and what penalty keeps them out.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a fixed text or automated output as self-authenticating.
- Assuming print, data, or replication is inherently truth-producing.
- Confusing disagreement with disloyalty.
- Rejecting a correction method because its practitioners remain fallible.

## Worked Example

For a clinical AI, require a versioned model, a record of training data and known errors, independent audits, a route for clinicians and patients to challenge decisions, and a process for withdrawing or revising the model. The aim is not to make the system infallible; it is to ensure that a mistake can become a correction rather than a hidden rule.

## Key Takeaways

- Fallibility cannot be engineered away by moving authority into a text or machine.
- Canonization creates both stability and curator power.
- Print can amplify science, superstition, and persecution.
- Science’s strength lies in visible, distributed correction rather than perfect practitioners.
- As power grows, institutions must protect dissent and revision from the network’s need for order.
