# Nexus Cheatsheet

## Fast diagnostic

1. What network is being analyzed?
2. Who and what are its members: people, documents, institutions, algorithms, bots?
3. Is the information representing reality, or connecting and coordinating actors?
4. Which parts are objective, subjective, or intersubjective?
5. What story makes the network cohere?
6. Where do channels merge—the information nexus?
7. Does the network privilege order over truth?
8. Who can challenge a decision, inspect evidence, and revise the record?
9. Are correction channels independent, plural, and understandable?
10. Does surveillance flow both upward and downward?
11. Can people rest, change status, and refuse unwanted intimacy?
12. Is a computer acting as an agent, or merely relaying a human instruction?
13. Are the system’s goals aligned across tactics, strategy, and ultimate values?
14. What happens when bots, algorithms, and humans form a feedback loop?
15. Could a local failure cross borders through data, software, weapons, or pathogens?

## Four democratic guardrails

- Benevolence: use personal data for the person’s interests; treat access as a fiduciary responsibility.
- Decentralization: keep databases and institutions separate enough to check one another.
- Mutuality: make governments and corporations legible and accountable to the people they monitor.
- Change and rest: preserve privacy, reversibility, dissent, and time outside constant measurement.

## Red flags

- One archive, one model, or one leader claims infallibility.
- A platform rewards outrage while denying responsibility for its curation.
- A consequential score cannot be explained or challenged.
- Human and machine agents are indistinguishable in public conversation.
- A network becomes more centralized as its power and error cost increase.
- Political actors describe all relationships as pure power struggles.
- A new technology is treated as destiny rather than a branching capability.

## Practical response

Map the network; separate truth from coordination; identify the nexus; add independent reviewers; expose objectives and data provenance; require contestable explanations; label nonhuman agents; preserve human responsibility; and negotiate shared rules for cross-border risks. Use these as design questions, not as a claim that one institutional form is universally sufficient.
