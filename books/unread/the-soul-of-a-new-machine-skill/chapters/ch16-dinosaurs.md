# Chapter 16: Dinosaurs

## Core Idea

“Dinosaurs” looks backward from the completed Eagle to the integration and succession work that had been easy to overlook. Blau realizes that West’s contribution was not just a CPU design: he coordinated compatibility, cost, performance, peripherals, product-line politics, and the many connections between boards and organizations. After the Product Board accepts Eagle, West briefly relaxes, but the chapter also punctures his aura of omnipotence with a sailing-engine failure that he must diagnose like anyone else. The old Eclipse culture—informal, competitive, playful, and autonomous—now appears dinosaur-like as Data General grows, adds executives and regulations, and threatens to delegate the work into smaller official pieces. West leaves for business development in Japan after naming Rasala, Wallach, and Alsing as people who can carry parts of the work; his departure feels like a funeral because the culture depended on personal presence as much as on procedures. Rosen, Peck, Wallach, Alsing, and later Rasala leave or move on, while the group is disbanded in fall 1980 despite promotions, options, and publications. The post-project blues show that a successful technical organization still needs a succession plan and a next meaningful challenge.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Integrator role**: Identify the person or interface that holds together local designs, product economics, compatibility, manufacturing, and organizational commitments.
- **Finishing school**: A high-autonomy project can develop successors by giving people whole responsibilities and exposing them to system-level judgment.
- **Protect autonomy until safe transfer**: Preserve a team’s freedom while the work is interdependent, then make explicit how decisions and knowledge will survive a leader’s exit.
- **Postpartum continuity**: Plan the next challenge, reflection, or community before the current project ends; otherwise the group may experience success as loss.
- **The dinosaur test**: A culture that once enabled innovation can become maladapted to scale, but formalization can also destroy the capability it was meant to preserve.

## Key Concepts

- **Integrator**: The role that reconciles many local technical and business constraints into one working product.
- **Product Board**: The organizational review at which Eagle’s larger product case is accepted.
- **Eclipse Group**: Data General’s older engineering culture and community, later treated as an organizational relic.
- **Succession**: Transfer of judgment, relationships, and culture, not just assignment of tasks.
- **Postpartum blues**: The emptiness and disorientation that follow the end of an absorbing project.
- **Dinosaur**: A once-effective form that cannot assume its environment will remain unchanged.

## Mental Models

- Draw a system’s **integration graph**, not only its block diagram: include people, suppliers, compatibility promises, product economics, and downstream handoffs.
- Think of culture as **tacit infrastructure**. It can be taught by shared work, but it becomes fragile when its rules live only in one leader’s behavior.
- Treat project completion as a **state transition**: build → hand off → renew or close. Without the transition plan, the team experiences the end as a failure even when the product succeeds.

## Anti-patterns

- **Ignoring the integrator**: Local excellence does not automatically produce a coherent product.
- **Assuming informal culture scales unchanged**: Practices that work in a small basement can become opaque or exclusionary in a larger organization.
- **Replacing autonomy with task delegation**: Breaking a whole responsibility into official fragments can remove the judgment that made the system work.
- **Leaving succession implicit**: Naming replacements is not enough if the relationships, decision rules, and meaning of the work are not transferred.

## Worked Example

Blau sees a cable bearing the “Tom West special” and recognizes that West has coordinated far more than the CPU: the Eagle must fit a product line, cost target, compatible software and peripherals, and an organization capable of manufacturing it. West then leaves for business development, carrying the project’s clock upstairs and naming Rasala, Wallach, and Alsing as successors. The departures that follow—Rosen, Peck, Wallach, Alsing, and later Rasala—show that individual promotion or stock options cannot preserve the same community. The group’s disbanding and the resulting post-partum blues make the operational lesson clear: a project needs both an integrator during construction and a designed continuity after success.

## Key Takeaways

1. Integration is a distinct form of technical and organizational work.
2. Informal autonomy can teach successors, but only if the tacit system is made legible before departure.
3. Growth can make a once-effective culture look obsolete while still destroying the capability it contained.
4. A next game, handoff ritual, or new mission is part of finishing the current project.
