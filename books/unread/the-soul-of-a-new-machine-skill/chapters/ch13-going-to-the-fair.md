# Chapter 13: Going to the Fair

## Core Idea

The National Computer Conference gives the Eagle team a view of the industry that their basement culture has kept at a distance. At the fair, machines, peripherals, software, OEM relationships, marketing claims, and corporate scale appear as one interdependent ecosystem; Data General’s tall sign and DEC’s VAX make competition concrete. The chapter then widens from industry observation to social argument, asking whether the computer revolution will be benevolent, dangerous, banal, or cruel, and whether productivity, privacy, jobs, and centralized power will improve or worsen. The engineers are not detached from those questions: Eagle is general-purpose and could be ruggedized for military use, while Holland refuses direct weapon design and Blau feels the moral discomfort of making a flexible tool whose uses he cannot control. Yet the team also leaves the fair for Greenwich Village and Times Square, enjoying the freedom to say, in effect, that they build machines rather than determine every use. The distinction is useful but incomplete; general-purpose technology distributes responsibility across designers, companies, customers, and institutions.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Ecosystem before component**: Evaluate a technical product within its surrounding vendors, software, peripherals, customers, and marketing channels; a fast CPU is not the whole offering.
- **Use/control distinction**: Separate the design of a general-purpose capability from the institutions that deploy and control it, while recognizing that the separation does not erase professional responsibility.
- **Professional virtue and restraint**: Technical competence needs judgment about foreseeable uses, power, and limits; refusal can be a legitimate engineering decision.
- **Visibility paradox**: Public spectacle makes an industry look unified and inevitable, while the people who built one machine may remain invisible or morally unrepresented.

## Key Concepts

- **National Computer Conference**: The industry fair where the team sees competitors and the expanding computer ecosystem.
- **OEM**: An original-equipment manufacturer that incorporates or resells a computer component or system.
- **Chronocentrism**: The tendency to treat one’s own period as uniquely transformative, a bias the chapter applies to computer enthusiasm.
- **National Data Bank**: A proposed form of centralized information power used in the chapter’s privacy discussion.
- **General-purpose machine**: A flexible system whose eventual uses are not exhausted by the designers’ immediate intentions.
- **Ruggedized computer**: A machine adapted for harsher environments, including potential military deployment.

## Mental Models

- Draw a technology’s **use chain**: component → product → software/peripherals → customer → institution → social effect. Responsibility and risk can enter at every link.
- Treat industry fairs as **maps of dependencies**, not merely showcases. The booths reveal who supplies whom and which claims are being translated into sales.
- Ask two separate questions: **Can we build it?** and **What power does building it place into other hands?** Neither question substitutes for the other.

## Anti-patterns

- **Technological inevitability**: Assuming that a computer revolution has one fixed moral direction prevents examination of actual institutions and uses.
- **“I just build ’em” as a complete ethic**: A designer need not control every use, but can still assess foreseeable harm and choose what work to accept.
- **Confusing market size with social value**: A large industry and impressive trade-show floor do not prove that every application is useful or economical.
- **Making the component disappear**: Treating peripherals, software, customers, and military or commercial channels as someone else’s problem produces an incomplete risk picture.

## Worked Example

The team arrives by bus while still debugging, then sees DEC’s VAX, Data General’s display, storage and communications products, software vendors, and OEMs arranged as one commercial world. The fair’s scale makes Eagle’s intended market legible, but the public debate around computers raises a different question: a flexible machine can support ordinary business, centralize personal data, or be adapted to military work. Holland declines to design weapons directly, while Blau worries about the implications; other engineers take refuge in the narrower identity of builders. The episode shows a practical boundary—designers cannot specify every future use—but also the need to preserve ethical judgment at the boundary.

## Key Takeaways

1. Product success depends on an ecosystem of complementary technologies and organizations.
2. General-purpose capability creates distributed responsibility rather than no responsibility.
3. A project team should surface foreseeable uses and risks before launch, even when it cannot control deployment.
4. Industry visibility and moral clarity are different achievements.
