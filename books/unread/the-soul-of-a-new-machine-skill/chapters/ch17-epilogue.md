# Epilogue

## Core Idea

The Epilogue shows what happens when a collective technical achievement crosses the threshold into a marketed product. Meetings about patents become painful because individual contribution is difficult to separate from the glue work and shared effort that made Eagle function. The machine finally leaves the building and is launched at the Roosevelt Hotel on April 29, 1980, with 128 terminals; a crash is corrected, the boards are presented in off-white and blue, and Marketing renames it the MV/8000. Public accounts then attribute the machine to people who did not build it, while some builders themselves begin to ask whether the product is really theirs. West is present in the background as the story’s leader but is already moving into business development, and Alsing’s account of him supplies a human correction to the official narrative. The launch brings relief and awkwardness rather than uncomplicated triumph: the machine’s commercial identity belongs to Data General, while the makers’ identity remains tied to the intense conditions of its construction. The handoff therefore tests the book’s central claims about authorship, motivation, and the difference between making a thing and owning its future use.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Collective authorship**: Treat the product as the result of interdependent design, debugging, documentation, manufacturing, and organizational work; use individual credit only where the evidence supports it.
- **Public handoff**: A launch is a boundary event in which responsibility, language, and ownership move from builders to marketing, sales, customers, and operators.
- **Productization translation**: Marketing names and narratives translate a technical artifact into a market promise, often losing the local history of how it works.
- **Creator/product separation**: A maker can feel proud of a product without controlling its name, use, or future institutional meaning.

## Key Concepts

- **Patent contribution meeting**: A painful attempt to map individual inventive credit onto collective work.
- **Roosevelt Hotel launch**: Eagle’s public debut on April 29, 1980.
- **128 terminals**: The launch configuration that makes the handoff a visible operational event rather than a symbolic announcement.
- **MV/8000**: Marketing’s new name for Eagle.
- **Glue**: The integration work that binds separate contributions into a functioning system and is difficult to assign to one person.

## Mental Models

- Model launch as an **ownership gradient**: builder → integrator → manufacturing → marketing → customer. Each transition changes incentives and language.
- Preserve two records: **who made the system work** and **what the organization promises about the product**. They may overlap without being identical.
- Treat a public name as an **interface**, not a technical history; it is optimized for recognition and sales, so it should not be used as evidence of authorship.

## Anti-patterns

- **Attributing a product to titles**: Organizational rank or launch visibility is not proof of technical contribution.
- **Assuming launch preserves ownership**: Once the machine enters the market, its makers may lose control over naming, use, and interpretation.
- **Using marketing as the engineering record**: A renamed product still needs the actual design, test, and handoff history behind it.
- **Ignoring incentive transition**: The motivations that sustain a small team—responsibility, craft, and another game—do not automatically survive commercialization.

## Worked Example

At the Roosevelt Hotel launch, Eagle operates with 128 terminals and a crash is corrected in public view; the physical boards and the new MV/8000 name turn a local engineering object into a corporate product. The people who built it are now in suits, while a reporter’s account and marketing language redistribute responsibility. Alsing’s description of West keeps the human chain visible, but even he wonders after the launch, “did I do that?” The event demonstrates the gap between collective making and public ownership: success can be real while the builders’ relationship to the product becomes uncertain.

## Key Takeaways

1. A launch changes who speaks for a system and what counts as its identity.
2. Collective technical work needs deliberate attribution records because “glue” is easy to erase.
3. Marketing translation is useful for a product but unreliable as a history of construction.
4. The end of the build is also the beginning of a different incentive and responsibility system.
