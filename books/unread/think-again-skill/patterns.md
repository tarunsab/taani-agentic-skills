# Patterns

## Hypothesis–Experiment Loop
**When to use**: A plan is uncertain, testable, and still reversible.
**How**: State the strategy as a hypothesis, identify what would support or refute it, run a small test, measure, and pivot when the evidence disagrees.
**Trade-offs**: Small experiments may miss long-term effects, but attachment to an untested plan can be far more costly.

This is Grant’s central translation of scientist mode into action. Italian founders who treated strategy, customer interviews, and prototypes as theory, hypothesis, and experiment pivoted more often and produced more revenue than founders who stayed wedded to the first plan. The loop is not indecision: it creates a deadline and evidence for a decision. Use it before identity or sunk costs make revision feel like failure (Ch 1, Epilogue).

## Prediction With Exit Conditions
**When to use**: You are making a time-bound forecast or committing to a belief.
**How**: Record the probability, supporting conditions, disconfirming signals, and the evidence that would change your mind; update and review.
**Trade-offs**: The record exposes errors and can feel uncomfortable, but it separates calibration from hindsight.

Superforecasters revise more frequently because they treat predictions as hunches and search for disconfirming evidence. An explicit exit condition prevents a bad forecast from becoming an identity defense. Review the quality of the update, not only whether the outcome happened (Ch 3).

## Challenge Network
**When to use**: A decision, draft, or leader is surrounded by agreement.
**How**: Recruit thoughtful critics, preferably disagreeable givers; explain what kind of pushback is useful; protect their status and act on the signal.
**Trade-offs**: Criticism can sting and may slow consensus, but flattery and conformity hide weaknesses.

A challenge network is not a collection of contrarians. Its value comes from care, expertise, and a shared aim of improving the work. Pixar’s “pirates,” peer reviewers, and Grant’s book critics show how dissent produces better options when psychological safety is present (Ch 4, Ch 10).

## Task-Conflict Debate
**When to use**: People need to test competing ideas without damaging the relationship.
**How**: Say “Can we debate?”, focus on the task, ask how the mechanism works, critique the artifact, and reset if language becomes personal.
**Trade-offs**: Moderate early conflict improves creativity and decisions; unmanaged emotional conflict creates defensiveness and silence.

The Wright brothers argued intensely while keeping the object of disagreement separate from the person. Asking how exposes explanatory gaps more reliably than asking only why, which can make people defend identity or principle. The boundary is relationship safety, not politeness at the expense of truth (Ch 4).

## Steel Man and Common Ground
**When to use**: You need to persuade someone who has a valid premise but rejects your conclusion.
**How**: State what the other side gets right, name shared goals, then offer a few cohesive reasons for the disputed mechanism or trade-off.
**Trade-offs**: Concessions can feel like lost ground, but they signal truth-seeking and reduce the urge to defend.

Harish Natarajan moved a preschool-subsidy audience by accepting the value of preschool and the seriousness of poverty before questioning access and allocation. Project Debater supplied more facts, but its failure to grant valid points made it sound like an onslaught. The steel man is for learning as well as persuasion; do not use it as a rhetorical trick (Ch 5).

## Minimal Compelling Case
**When to use**: The audience is invested, skeptical, and likely to resist persuasion.
**How**: Choose the smallest number of strong, connected reasons and explain them fully; remove weak or unrelated arguments.
**Trade-offs**: Receptive or uninvolved audiences may benefit from more reasons, while resistant audiences gain ammunition from every weak link.

Research on negotiations and alumni giving showed that two different lines of argument could be less effective than either one alone. The audience’s awareness of being persuaded activates defense, and rejecting one weak reason can contaminate the rest. The rule is conditional, not a universal preference for brevity (Ch 5).

## Motivational Interview
**When to use**: A person is ambivalent, defensive, or resisting advice.
**How**: Ask open questions, reflect what you hear, affirm values and capacity, follow change talk, ask permission before information, summarize, and check execution.
**Trade-offs**: It takes time and cannot guarantee change; it fails ethically when used as covert manipulation.

The method works through ownership and autonomy rather than pressure. Arnaud Gagneur’s vaccine conversations and Betty Bigombe’s peace work show how careful listening lowers defensiveness and reveals reasons for change. Grant’s failed work with CEO Jeff adds the limit: motivation without a concrete implementation plan is incomplete (Ch 7).

## Counterfactual Origin Question
**When to use**: A stereotype, rivalry, or certainty seems inherited rather than examined.
**How**: Ask what the person would believe if born in another place, time, family, culture, or social category; compare that contingency with the strength of the belief.
**Trade-offs**: Reflection can soften certainty but cannot by itself repair systemic harm or guarantee empathy.

Red Sox and Yankees fans became less hostile when they considered that their allegiance was an accident of birth. The question changes the perceived origin of the belief rather than demanding that a person immediately like the rival. Use it alongside safe, meaningful intergroup contact (Ch 6).

## Complexity Frame
**When to use**: A public or private issue is being reduced to two camps.
**How**: Map the spectrum, name caveats and contingencies, distinguish skepticism from denial, and include more than one credible route to action.
**Trade-offs**: Complexity can be less catchy, but it improves credibility, curiosity, and the quality of joint reasoning.

In Coleman’s lab, a many-viewpoint frame helped opposing strangers find common ground more reliably than a two-sided frame. Complexifying is not false balance: evidence can still be stronger on one side. The purpose is to replace identity defense with a more accurate problem map (Ch 8).

## Multiple-Draft Critique
**When to use**: Quality depends on revision, craft, or a changing standard.
**How**: Produce several versions, invite specific and kind critique of the work, revise, and update the criteria for excellence.
**Trade-offs**: Revision requires time and tolerance for confusion, but a polished first draft can conceal shallow thinking.

Ron Berger’s students learned that drafts are evidence of learning, not embarrassment. Gallery critiques and focused reviews made feedback public while keeping the author safe. The pattern transfers to writing, design, scientific explanations, and team plans (Ch 9).

## Learning-Zone Scorecard
**When to use**: An organization wants experimentation without complacency.
**How**: Combine psychological safety with process accountability; review assumptions, alternatives, uncertainties, and outcomes; distinguish luck from a smart experiment.
**Trade-offs**: It can slow decisions and expose leaders, but results-only evaluation rewards hidden error and stale routines.

Safety without accountability becomes a comfort zone, while accountability without safety becomes an anxiety zone. The combination makes challenge and admission of error useful. Separate initial decision makers from later evaluators when sunk-cost self-justification is likely (Ch 10).

## Career Checkup
**When to use**: A role, relationship, or life plan reaches a plateau or feels sacred because of past investment.
**How**: Ask when the aspiration formed, how you have changed, what is no longer learning, which possible selves appeal, and what small experiment could test them.
**Trade-offs**: A checkup may uncover costly change, but escalation of commitment can waste far more future time.

Ibarra’s sequence—possible selves, fit hypotheses, and experiments—keeps a pivot evidence-based. The same practice works for relationships and daily job crafting when a full exit is impossible. The goal is not constant novelty; it is staying open to a better fit (Ch 11).
