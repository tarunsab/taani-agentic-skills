# Chapter 1: A Preacher, a Prosecutor, a Politician, and a Scientist Walk into Your Mind

## Core Idea

Grant argues that adapting to a changing world depends on people rethinking, not on organizations magically “adapting” by themselves. We often enter preacher mode to defend sacred beliefs, prosecutor mode to find flaws in others, or politician mode to win approval from an audience; all three can reward certainty while hiding gaps in our own reasoning. Scientist mode offers a different posture: treat strategies and opinions as hypotheses, use evidence and experiments, and revise when results contradict expectations. A field experiment with Italian startup founders found that those taught to frame business plans as theories and products as tests pivoted more often and generated much more revenue than controls. Intelligence and expertise do not guarantee this flexibility; smart people can deploy their reasoning to rationalize confirmation and desirability bias. The rethinking cycle therefore needs humility, doubt, and curiosity, plus an identity based on values rather than on being right.

## SECOND THOUGHTS

Knowledge is expanding faster, while beliefs become more entrenched the longer we hold them. Grant describes outdated facts as mental fossils and notes that people readily seek second opinions about another person’s medical diagnosis while accepting their own judgments about hiring, money, or relationships without equivalent scrutiny. The Bernie Madoff example shows how ordinary social evidence can overpower skepticism: Stephen Greenspan trusted a family adviser, his sister’s returns, and apparent double-digit performance, then lost a large share of his retirement savings when the fund collapsed. The lesson is not to distrust every relationship or outcome, but to analyze a strategy systematically, seek independent perspectives, and test with limited stakes before committing. A personal second opinion is a habit of rethinking rather than a sign of indecision.

## A DIFFERENT PAIR OF GOGGLES

In scientist mode, a business strategy is a theory, customer interviews help form hypotheses, and a minimum viable product or prototype is an experiment. The Italian startup training study randomly assigned more than a hundred founders to ordinary instruction or the same instruction with this scientific framing. After a year, the control startups averaged under $300 in revenue, while the scientific-thinking group averaged over $12,000, brought in revenue more than twice as fast, and pivoted more than twice as often. The control group remained attached to its original plan; the experimental group treated failed hypotheses as information about when to rethink the model. The chapter also warns that professional scientists can become preachers, prosecutors, or politicians when they protect pet theories, popularity, or status instead of discovery.

## THE SMARTER THEY ARE, THE HARDER THEY FAIL

Mental horsepower can make motivated reasoning more effective rather than more truthful. In the gun-law data study, mathematically skilled participants interpreted neutral skin-rash data well, but used their quantitative ability selectively when the same pattern was labeled with an ideological issue: they were more accurate when the data supported their politics and worse when it contradicted them. Confirmation bias makes us see what we expect; desirability bias makes us see what we want; the “I’m not biased” bias makes it harder to notice either. Scientist mode means leading with questions, testing one’s own arguments, and actively searching for disconfirming evidence. Intellectual curiosity and openness—not ambition, forcefulness, charm, or polish—were the traits most consistently associated with highly regarded presidents, and the same flexibility distinguished eminent scientists and creative architects.

## DON’T STOP UNBELIEVING

Grant describes rethinking as a cycle: humility about what we do not know opens doubt, doubt produces curiosity, curiosity sends us looking for information, and discovery renews humility. The overconfidence cycle starts with pride, hardens into conviction, filters evidence through confirmation and desirability, and turns validation into more arrogance. BlackBerry illustrates the trap: Mike Lazaridis was a brilliant engineer who generated hypotheses to invent wireless email, but as CEO he protected the keyboard-and-email identity of the product after the iPhone demonstrated a broader smartphone future. Apple’s recovery depended on employees persuading Steve Jobs to preserve Apple’s identity as a computer company while experimenting with a phone, a continuity frame that made change less threatening. The appropriate mode is not absolute: preaching, prosecuting, or politicking can sometimes serve a purpose, but the default for updating our own beliefs should be scientist mode.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Preacher, prosecutor, politician, scientist**: Four mental modes that shape how we defend beliefs, attack reasoning, seek approval, or search for truth.
  - When to use: Diagnose the mode you are in before a difficult decision or disagreement.
  - How: Move from sermons, rebuttals, and audience management to hypotheses, tests, data, and revision.
- **The rethinking cycle**: Intellectual humility → doubt → curiosity → discovery → renewed humility.
  - When to use: When a conclusion is becoming a fixed identity or when new evidence feels threatening.
  - How: Name what you do not know, find disconfirming evidence, and update the view.
- **The overconfidence cycle**: Pride → conviction → confirmation/desirability bias → validation → more pride.
  - When to use: When confidence is being reinforced mainly by agreement, status, or past success.
  - How: Add independent critics, counterevidence, small experiments, and explicit conditions for changing course.

## Key Concepts

- **Scientist mode**: A frame of mind that treats ideas as testable hypotheses rather than truths to defend.
- **Preacher mode**: Protecting sacred beliefs by delivering a sermon about their merits.
- **Prosecutor mode**: Building a case against another person’s reasoning to prove it wrong.
- **Politician mode**: Shaping views or arguments to win the approval of constituents and audiences.
- **Confirmation bias**: Interpreting evidence in ways that favor existing expectations.
- **Desirability bias**: Interpreting evidence in ways that favor preferred outcomes.
- **Active open-mindedness**: Looking for reasons one might be wrong and revising in light of evidence.
- **Continuity frame**: Showing what will remain stable so change does not threaten identity.

## Mental Models

- **Hypothesis, not verdict**: Use this when making a plan whose success is uncertain.
- **Second opinion**: Use this for your own decisions whenever you would seek one for another person’s diagnosis.
- **Continuity plus change**: Preserve core values or identity while experimenting with strategy and method.

## Anti-patterns

- **Confusing intelligence with flexibility**: Reasoning skill can become a weapon for defending a preferred conclusion.
- **Treating past success as current evidence**: A product that once fit the market may now conceal a different opportunity.
- **Protecting the audience instead of the truth**: Approval can turn a tentative belief into a political commitment.

## Worked Example

### BlackBerry and Apple

Lazaridis initially used scientist-like questioning to create the BlackBerry: thumb typing, synchronized email, server relays, and encryption were hypotheses for engineers to test. After the iPhone appeared, he kept defending the features that had made BlackBerry successful and did not seriously test the competing hypothesis that users wanted a general-purpose computer with apps and a touchscreen. The company’s market share fell from nearly half of the U.S. smartphone market in 2009 to under 1 percent by 2014. Apple’s engineers faced the opposite problem: Steve Jobs rejected the phone idea because he feared carriers, crashes, and cannibalizing the iPod. They preserved the continuity of Apple as a computer company while experimenting with how to add calling, and the iPhone later became a major share of Apple’s revenue.

## Key Takeaways

1. Before defending a view, identify whether you are preaching, prosecuting, politicking, or testing.
2. Form opinions as hypotheses and write down what evidence would change them.
3. Use small experiments and independent perspectives before making irreversible commitments.
4. Do not treat intelligence, expertise, or past success as immunity from motivated reasoning.
5. Define identity by values so methods and beliefs can evolve.
