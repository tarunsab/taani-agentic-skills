# Chapter 10: That’s Not the Way We’ve Always Done It

## Core Idea

Rethinking is a collective capability shaped by organizational culture, not only a private mental habit. Performance cultures reward execution, results, and standard procedures, which can make people normalize anomalies and hide mistakes; learning cultures make humility, questions, experimentation, and revision routine. Grant argues that a learning culture requires both psychological safety and accountability for the quality of the decision process. Psychological safety without accountability can become a comfort zone, while accountability without safety creates an anxiety zone; together they form a learning zone. Process accountability evaluates how alternatives were considered, so a good process with a bad outcome can be a smart experiment and a bad process with a good outcome can be luck. NASA, the Gates Foundation, and Amazon show how leaders can convert these principles into questions, rituals, memos, decision rights, and protected dissent.

## I ERR, THEREFORE I LEARN

Amy Edmondson initially found that teams reporting more medical errors also reported more psychological safety, but the apparent relationship reversed when covert observation showed that psychologically safe teams were simply more willing to report mistakes. Unsafe teams hid errors, preventing diagnosis and allowing the same failures to recur. Psychological safety is not lowered standards, unconditional praise, or comfort without challenge; it is a climate of respect and openness in which people can raise concerns without fear of reprisal. Google’s team research cited by Grant identified it as the most important differentiator of high performance and well-being. The core mechanism is learning from visible failure instead of protecting status through silence.

## SAFE AT HOME GATES

At the Gates Foundation, asking managers to solicit criticism produced a short-lived rise in psychological safety because some managers disliked or could not use the feedback. A more durable intervention had managers share a past experience of benefiting from criticism and a current development goal, publicly modeling imperfect, ongoing learning. Over time, teams offered more useful feedback and created regular practices such as “ask me anything” conversations and development check-ins. Melinda Gates’s “Mean Reviews” video made senior criticism visible and human, reduced power distance, and encouraged employees to approach leaders. Vulnerability worked because it was repeated and backed by action, not because a single disclosure automatically made a culture safe.

## THE WORST THING ABOUT BEST PRACTICES

Declaring a routine a “best practice” freezes it and discourages questions about where it fails. NASA’s shuttle disasters and Luca Parmitano’s near-drowning involved anomalies that had occurred before and were normalized as harmless: foam loss, O-ring risk, or water in a helmet attributed to a drink bag. Ellen Ochoa carried a card of questions such as what supports an assumption, what the uncertainties are, and what happens if it is wrong. Process accountability adds a second scorecard: evaluate the depth of alternatives considered, not only whether the outcome looked successful. Amazon’s six-page memo forces this reasoning before consequential decisions, while “disagree and commit” allows a group to run a reversible bet without demanding certainty in advance.

## Decision rights and smart experiments

Organizations are more likely to rethink when initial decision makers are not the only people evaluating later outcomes. California banks kept extending loans after defaults when the same executives had signed off on the first loan; higher turnover made it easier to identify and write off bad loans. NASA historically let cost and schedule managers waive technical requirements, making self-justification easy. In a learning culture, an outlier such as Ellen’s no vote on a shuttle launch is treated as information and a responsibility to investigate, not disloyalty. NASA delayed the launch, repeated the problem, and eventually identified a cryogenic connection fault; the delay protected later missions. The aim is not to eliminate judgment or experimentation, but to give decisions enough safety, process scrutiny, and independence to learn before harm becomes irreversible.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Psychological safety**: Respectful openness to raising concerns, questions, and mistakes without reprisal.
  - When to use: When people are silent, hiding errors, or deferring to authority.
  - How: Leaders model humility, normalize imperfection, respond constructively, and protect dissent.
- **Learning zone**: Psychological safety plus accountability for rigorous work and improvement.
  - When to use: When teams need both freedom to experiment and pressure to think carefully.
  - How: Pair permission to speak with process review, clear standards, and follow-through.
- **Process accountability**: Evaluate how options were considered, not only the final result.
  - When to use: Before results are known or when luck can mask a weak strategy.
  - How: Require explanations of assumptions, alternatives, uncertainties, and trade-offs.
- **Better practices over best practices**: Keep routines provisional and subject to improvement.
  - When to use: In changing environments or after anomalies and near misses.
  - How: Debrief, test alternatives, and treat current routines as hypotheses.
- **Disagree and commit**: Allow a documented bet despite disagreement when the experiment is worthwhile.
  - When to use: When requiring proof before testing would block progress.
  - How: State the disagreement, choose a bounded experiment, and review the process and result.

## Key Concepts

- **Performance culture**: A culture centered on execution and results.
- **Learning culture**: A culture centered on growth, questions, experimentation, and revision.
- **Psychological safety**: Freedom to speak up and admit errors without punishment.
- **Accountability**: Responsibility for standards and consequences of work.
- **Learning zone**: The combination of safety and accountability.
- **Process accountability**: Review of reasoning and alternatives, not just outcomes.
- **Normalization**: Treating an abnormal signal as ordinary because it happened before.
- **Reversible experiment**: A bounded test whose consequences can be undone or contained.
- **Decision authority**: Who makes, approves, waives, or later evaluates a decision.

## Mental Models

- **Outcome versus process scorecard**: Good outcome + shallow process = luck; bad outcome + deep process = smart experiment.
- **Comfort/anxiety/learning matrix**: Safety without accountability is comfort; accountability without safety is anxiety; both is learning.
- **How do you know?**: A nonjudgmental question that reveals unsupported assumptions.

## Anti-patterns

- **Punishing visible error while rewarding hidden error**: It drives reporting underground and prevents learning.
- **Best-practice freeze**: Treating a routine as final because it once worked.
- **Results-only evaluation**: Mistaking luck for a sound method and failure for a bad experiment.
- **Same person approves and defends**: Decision owners may rationalize sunk commitments.
- **One-time vulnerability theater**: A single request for feedback without follow-through can reduce trust.

## Worked Example

### Ellen Ochoa and the shuttle launch

After the Columbia disaster, Ellen Ochoa carried a 3 × 5 card with questions about assumptions, uncertainties, disadvantages, and what would happen if an analysis were wrong. When sensors malfunctioned during a later shuttle countdown, she was the only mission manager to vote against launching. The administrator asked to understand her reasoning, the launch was delayed, and repeated symptoms eventually revealed a faulty connection in a cryogenic environment rather than a sensor failure. NASA’s emerging culture treated dissent as a responsibility, not a personal attack. The result was not immediate certainty; it was a safer process that allowed the organization to learn before the next irreversible event.

## Key Takeaways

1. Build psychological safety by repeatedly modeling humility and acting on feedback.
2. Pair safety with process accountability so learning does not become complacency.
3. Ask how people know, what is uncertain, and what happens if the assumption is wrong.
4. Replace best-practice worship with better-practice experiments and debriefs.
5. Separate decision making from later evaluation when self-justification is likely.
6. Treat a good process as valuable even when a bounded experiment fails.
