# Epilogue: Keep the Conclusion Open

## Core Idea

The epilogue resists finality as a symbol of the book’s subject: a conclusion should be a starting point for new questions, not a sealed verdict. Grant describes rethinking as something generations and individuals can do, rather than waiting for old believers to disappear. He updates a detail about tyrannosaur feathers and uses the revision to model the ordinary pleasure of correcting a belief without losing the larger project. He also qualifies his central advocacy of scientist mode: it is the strongest default for rethinking our own views, but preaching, prosecuting, or politicking may sometimes persuade particular audiences or provide needed security. Franklin Roosevelt’s call for “bold, persistent experimentation” supplies the final model for leadership under uncertainty. The appropriate conclusion is therefore provisional: act, observe, admit what fails, and keep the method open to revision.

## The conclusion as a new draft

Grant originally wanted a blank epilogue, but his challenge network argued that an endpoint for the writer could be a starting point for the reader. He instead shows how the ending itself changed through feedback, mirroring the multiple drafts in Ron Berger’s classroom. Fact-checking also changed his picture of tyrannosaur plumage: a typical T. rex is now understood as mostly scaly, while another tyrannosaur could supply the feathered example. The details matter less than the practice of revising a claim while preserving curiosity and humor. A conclusion should leave the reader with a question that can generate another experiment or conversation.

## When scientist mode is not the only mode

Grant remains uncertain about the situations in which preaching, prosecuting, and politicking are productive. When people are forming their own views, the evidence favors scientist mode because humility, doubt, and curiosity improve updating. When influencing others, a passionate moral case may mobilize a receptive audience, prosecution may persuade people who are not strongly invested in control, and simple messages may rally one’s own political tribe. The correct mode depends on the audience, the complexity of the problem, and whether the goal is security, coordination, discovery, or revision. The qualification does not weaken the book’s central default; it makes the framework itself subject to the kind of nuance it recommends.

## Bold, persistent experimentation

During the Great Depression, Franklin Delano Roosevelt argued that the country required bold, persistent experimentation and that a method should be tried, admitted as a failure when necessary, and replaced. Critics preferred a leader with a precise, concrete, definitive plan, but Grant presents FDR’s trial-and-error posture as a form of confident humility suited to a complex crisis. The example does not prove that every vague or changing plan is wise, nor settle which reforms caused recovery; it shows the value of visible learning under uncertainty. Leaders can give people hope by being candid about what is unknown, learning from others, and adapting plans rather than pretending the first plan was final.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Open-ended conclusion**: Treat a final synthesis as a springboard for further questions and revision.
  - When to use: After a project, decision, book, or policy claim appears complete.
  - How: Ask what changed, what remains uncertain, and what experiment or conversation comes next.
- **Bold, persistent experimentation**: Act under uncertainty, inspect results, and change methods without disguising failure.
  - When to use: In crises and complex systems where proof is unavailable in advance.
  - How: Try a bounded method, monitor it, admit failure, and try another.
- **Contextual mode selection**: Choose among scientist, preacher, prosecutor, and politician modes based on the goal and audience.
  - When to use: When deciding how to communicate a plan or change another person’s mind.
  - How: Preserve truth-seeking, but consider whether the audience needs discovery, coordination, motivation, or reassurance.

## Key Concepts

- **Open-endedness**: Keeping a conclusion available to further evidence and thought.
- **Visible rethinking**: Showing how a belief or plan changed so others can learn from the process.
- **Bold experimentation**: Persistent action with willingness to replace a failed method.
- **Contextual persuasion**: Adjusting communication mode to audience, complexity, and purpose.
- **Confident humility**: Acting with hope while admitting uncertainty and revising publicly.

## Mental Models

- **Conclusion as draft**: Use the final paragraph or decision as the next question’s first draft.
- **Try–learn–replace**: Use when no plan can be proven in advance but inaction is costly.
- **Do your rethinking out loud**: Use transparent updates to make revision socially safer for others.

## Anti-patterns

- **Finality theater**: Presenting a complex, changing problem as settled because the format demands an ending.
- **Mode absolutism**: Assuming scientist mode or any other mode is always the best communication tool.
- **Experiment without accountability**: Treating “try something” as permission to avoid defining goals, evidence, or review.

## Worked Example

### FDR’s “try something” principle

Grant first describes a seemingly weak crisis message: try a method, admit failure, and try another. He then reveals that the speaker was FDR in 1932, not a contemporary governor, and that the principle became a hallmark of his leadership during the Great Depression. The message offered a method for learning rather than a guarantee of success, and Americans later rewarded the broader experimental posture with repeated elections. The example preserves an important limit: historians still debate which reforms produced recovery, so the illustration supports a method of public rethinking rather than a simple causal claim about policy.

## Key Takeaways

1. Let conclusions generate the next question instead of ending inquiry.
2. Use scientist mode as the default for your own beliefs, but select communication modes contextually.
3. Make uncertainty and revision visible when others need permission to learn.
4. In complex crises, use bold, persistent experimentation with explicit review.
