# Chapter 5: Dances with Foes

## Core Idea

Grant reframes debate as a dance rather than a war: the aim is to reach a better understanding of the truth with a partner whose steps differ from yours. Harish Natarajan’s debate with IBM’s Project Debater shows why a speaker with fewer facts can change more minds than a machine with an enormous knowledge base. Expert negotiators prepare common ground, offer a small number of strong and connected reasons, ask more questions, and avoid defend-attack spirals. These moves model confident humility and invite the other person to generate reasons for change rather than forcing them to accept yours. When hostility rises, a conversation about the conversation can reset the process; when confidence is moderate and openly provisional, the audience has room to think. The methods are conditional, not magic: more reasons can help receptive or uninvolved audiences, but can give skeptical, invested people more material to reject.

## THE SCIENCE OF THE DEAL

Grant’s “logic bully” episode with a business-school-bound former student taught him that airtight data can still make a conversation worse when it overwhelms the listener. In recordings of labor and contract negotiations, expert negotiators spent more planning time identifying common ground, presented fewer reasons, and asked questions more often than average negotiators. At least one in five of their comments ended in a question, which let the partner contribute information and take a step toward the solution. Their restraint was not weakness; it kept a weak argument from diluting a strong one and avoided provoking a defend-attack spiral. A scientist-like negotiator searches for a mutually better option instead of treating agreement as a concession.

## DANCING TO THE SAME BEAT

Harish began the preschool-subsidy debate by acknowledging that he and Project Debater agreed about the harm of poverty and the value of some research. That common ground made his objection about the remedy rather than a denial of the problem. He also steel-manned the opposing case by accepting that high-quality preschool can help children before questioning whether a subsidy reaches the poorest children or is the best use of limited political resources. An audience poll shifted toward Harish even though most listeners learned more facts from the computer. Conceding a valid point signals that the speaker is not merely preaching, prosecuting, or politicking, and it gives the other side a reason to consider changing too.

## DON’T STEP ON THEIR TOES

Project Debater supported preschool subsidies with many studies and multiple lines of argument, while Harish focused on two concerns: trade-offs in public spending and unequal access to high-quality programs. Research cited by Grant suggests that piling on reasons is most likely to backfire when an audience cares about the issue, is skeptical of the speaker’s position, and is generally strong-willed. Different reasons can alert a resistant audience that it is being persuaded, making it search for the weakest point and reject the case. A single question to basketball season-ticket holders—whether they planned to attend—raised attendance more than an exhortation from the team, because the fans supplied their own reasons and retained ownership of the choice. Persuasion often works by helping people persuade themselves.

## DR. JEKYLL AND MR. HOSTILE

When an executive dismissed Grant’s evidence about compensation and insisted that personal experience proved the company was different, Grant escalated from logic bully to name-calling. In a later exchange with a hostile heckler, he waited, asked what evidence would change the man’s mind, and sent the requested study; he did not know whether the belief changed, but he opened the conversation. Treating debate as a dance creates a third option when attacked: sidestep by discussing the process rather than attacking or retreating. Expert negotiators comment on their own feelings, test their understanding of the other person’s feelings, and ask whether the other side sees any merit in a proposal. If the answer to “What evidence would change your mind?” is “nothing,” continuing is not persuasion but a waste of both parties’ attention.

## THE STRENGTH OF WEAK OPINIONS

Grant revises his earlier advice to have strong opinions weakly held. If an opinion is held weakly but expressed with high certainty, the delivery can still signal arrogance; moderate confidence better communicates confident humility and invites nuance. A job candidate, Michele Hansen, used this approach by opening her cover letter with the real qualifications she lacked and then naming a few strengths that could not be taught. Her curiosity about times the team had been wrong helped them see learning ability as part of the role, and she got the job. Acknowledging weaknesses preempts objections that an informed audience will notice anyway, while limiting the case to a few core strengths avoids argument dilution. The point is not to hide conviction, but to hold it provisionally and communicate it in a way that leaves room for evidence.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Debate as dance**: Treat disagreement as coordinated movement toward a better understanding, not a contest to drag someone across a line.
  - When to use: In negotiation, debate, feedback, and persuasive conversations.
  - How: Find common ground, adapt to the partner, ask questions, and make room for reciprocal movement.
- **Steel man**: Present and engage the strongest version of the other side’s case rather than its easiest target.
  - When to use: When an opposing position has a valid premise mixed with a disputed conclusion.
  - How: State what the other side gets right, then focus on the specific mechanism or trade-off you question.
- **Argument dilution rule**: A weak supporting reason can give a skeptical audience a reason to reject stronger ones.
  - When to use: When the audience is invested, skeptical, and resistant to your conclusion.
  - How: Choose a few cohesive, well-explained reasons; add breadth only when the audience is receptive or uninvested.
- **Persuasive listening**: Use genuine questions so the other person supplies and owns the reasons for reconsideration.
  - When to use: When direct correction is likely to trigger resistance.
  - How: Ask what evidence would change their mind, how a proposal would work, and what merit they see in it.
- **Conversation about the conversation**: Shift from the disputed content to the process and feelings of the exchange.
  - When to use: When the discussion becomes hostile or unproductive.
  - How: Name your experience, test your understanding, and re-establish a respectful common goal.

## Key Concepts

- **Common ground**: Points of agreement that show the discussion is not an all-or-nothing fight.
- **Steel man**: The strongest credible form of an opponent’s argument.
- **Argument dilution**: Loss of persuasive force when weak or unrelated reasons are added to a strong case.
- **Self-persuasion**: Change generated by reasons the listener identifies and owns.
- **Freedom of choice**: Respect for the other person’s autonomy over beliefs and behavior.
- **Defend-attack spiral**: Reciprocal dismissal and doubling down that closes both minds.
- **Moderate confidence**: A level of certainty that is credible but leaves room for revision.
- **Logic bully**: Grant’s label for overwhelming someone with rational arguments rather than helping them think.

## Mental Models

- **Dance steps**: Each concession, question, or clarification should make the next reciprocal move easier.
- **Weakest-link case**: Assume a resistant listener will attack the least compelling reason; remove it before they do.
- **Ownership test**: Ask whether the other person can state their own reason for changing.

## Anti-patterns

- **Winning the argument by exhaustion**: More talking and more data can strengthen the other person’s defenses.
- **Straw-manning**: Refuting a weak caricature proves little and signals political or prosecutorial motives.
- **Answering hostility with hostility**: It turns an opportunity to reset the process into a contest of identities.
- **“Agree to disagree” as a full stop**: It can instead begin a learning conversation about what each side missed.

## Worked Example

### Harish Natarajan and Project Debater

In a public debate on government-subsidized preschool, IBM’s Project Debater had millions of articles and supplied more facts than Harish. Harish nevertheless shifted the audience by agreeing that poverty was a serious problem and that some preschool research was valid, then asking whether subsidies reached the poorest children and whether public resources had better uses. He used two connected objections rather than a long list and posed several questions that made listeners examine their own assumptions. Support for subsidies fell from 79 percent to 62 percent, while opposition rose from 13 percent to 30 percent. The example demonstrates common ground, steel-manning, argument restraint, and self-persuasion working together.

## Key Takeaways

1. Start a debate by naming what you and the other person share.
2. Steel-man the strongest opposing case before presenting your objection.
3. Use the fewest strong, connected reasons that fit the audience and situation.
4. Ask questions that let people discover and own their reasons for change.
5. If the process becomes hostile, discuss the process; if nothing could change either mind, stop.
6. Express convictions with enough uncertainty to make revision possible.
