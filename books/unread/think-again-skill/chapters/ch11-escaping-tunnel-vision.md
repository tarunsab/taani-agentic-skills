# Chapter 11: Escaping Tunnel Vision

## Core Idea

Life plans can guide action, but they can also become a tunnel that hides alternative careers, relationships, identities, and sources of meaning. Identity foreclosure occurs when a person settles on a self-definition before doing enough exploration; escalation of commitment then makes changing course feel like wasting the past. Grit is valuable for sustained goals but can become stubbornness when persistence continues after evidence says the path is failing. Grant recommends regular life or career checkups, possible selves, hypotheses about fit, and small experiments such as interviews, shadowing, and sample projects. He also argues that changing daily actions toward learning, connection, and contribution is often more durable than changing surroundings in pursuit of happiness. Because careers, relationships, and communities are open systems with multiple possible routes and destinations, a near-term plan plus willingness to revise is wiser than a ten-year identity contract.

## GOING INTO FORECLOSURE

Grant’s cousin Ryan followed a family dream of medicine, then repeatedly dismissed doubts about economics, business, a joint M.D.–M.B.A., health-care entrepreneurship, and the life costs of neurosurgery with the refrain “stay on track.” He completed years of residency and fellowship, eventually becoming a neurosurgeon who enjoyed patients but regretted the early closure of alternatives. Escalation of commitment leads people to invest more in failing strategies, contracts, wars, or careers because they rationalize earlier choices and protect their identities. Grit can amplify the trap: passion and perseverance are useful until they turn into staying at an impossible task or pushing toward a summit that should be abandoned. Children and adults are better served by learning careers as things they do and explore, not as a single thing they are.

## Possible selves and career experiments

Identity foreclosure is the opposite of accepting uncertainty: a person uses compensatory conviction to avoid the discomfort of not knowing who they will become. Research cited by Grant suggests that children retain more interest in science when they learn about doing science rather than being scientists, because experimenting is accessible while a fixed identity feels exclusive. Herminia Ibarra’s framework begins with possible selves: notice people whose work is interesting and observe what they actually do. Then form hypotheses about fit with your own interests, skills, and values, and test them through informational interviews, job shadowing, or sample projects. The objective is to expand the repertoire of plausible selves, not to confirm one early fantasy.

## TIME FOR A CHECKUP

A career checkup twice a year asks when the current aspiration was formed, how the person has changed, whether learning or lifestyle has plateaued, and whether a pivot or experiment is warranted. Marissa Shandell used these conversations with her husband after rapid promotions at a consulting firm; when both growth and lifestyle plateaued, she pursued a doctorate. The same method applies to relationships: Grant’s former student realized that his teenage image of an ambitious partner reflected who he wanted to be, not necessarily what would make a relationship fulfilling. Honest discussion allowed both partners to release an idealized image and make room for the actual person and her future development. The checkup is not an instruction to abandon commitments; it is a scheduled opportunity to distinguish current values from inherited scripts.

## WHEN CHASING HAPPINESS CHASES IT AWAY

Research cited by Grant finds that people who place very high importance on happiness can become less happy, partly because they evaluate their lives instead of experiencing them, chase peak intensity instead of frequent positive emotion, or privilege pleasure over purpose. Changing a dorm, schedule, or climate often produces a temporary shift back toward a baseline, while changing actions—joining a group, altering study habits, or starting a project—can create more lasting gains. A Wharton student who considered transferring for a friendlier culture instead built a microcommunity through coffee chats and weekly tea, gaining connection without changing schools. The lesson is not to remain in a harmful environment or deny structural constraints; it is to ask which part of the problem is a surrounding and which part can be changed through action.

## LIFE, LIBERTY, AND THE PURSUIT OF MEANING

Passion is often developed through effort, mastery, and problem solving rather than discovered fully formed before action. Grant’s test of meaningful work is to ask how much worse off people would be if the job did not exist, shifting attention from status and pleasure to contribution. He describes a progression from “I’m not important” to “I’m important” to “I want to contribute to something important,” with happiness becoming a by-product of mastery and meaning. Open systems have equifinality—many routes to one end—and multifinality—one starting point can lead to many ends—so there is no single route to success. Job crafting makes this principle practical: Candice Walker, officially a hospital custodian, expanded her daily role through emotional care, humor, and urgent help for cancer patients because those actions fit who she was and what mattered.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Identity foreclosure**: Prematurely settling on a self-definition without sufficient exploration of alternatives.
  - When to use: When a career or life plan feels sacred, inherited, or impossible to question.
  - How: Separate identity from action, broaden possible selves, and test alternatives.
- **Escalation of commitment**: Investing more in a failing path to justify earlier decisions and protect the ego.
  - When to use: When sunk time, money, status, or identity is the main reason to continue.
  - How: Run a checkup, ask what you would choose now, and compare the cost of leaving with the cost of staying.
- **Career/life checkup**: A scheduled review of learning, values, aspirations, and next experiments.
  - When to use: Twice a year or at a plateau, transition, or persistent doubt.
  - How: Ask when the plan formed, how you changed, what is plateauing, and what evidence would justify a pivot.
- **Possible selves**: Alternative future identities explored as hypotheses rather than commitments.
  - When to use: When the next path is unclear or the current path feels too narrow.
  - How: Observe role models, form fit hypotheses, and run informational, shadowing, or sample-work experiments.
- **Job crafting**: Changing daily tasks and relationships to better fit values, interests, and skills.
  - When to use: When a field is acceptable but the current role feels empty or misaligned.
  - How: Rethink what you do, how you interact, and whom you serve within real constraints.

## Key Concepts

- **Tunnel vision**: A plan’s narrow focus that hides alternatives and changing conditions.
- **Identity foreclosure**: Premature closure on who one will be.
- **Escalation of commitment**: Persistence driven by prior investment rather than current fit.
- **Grit**: Passion and perseverance, with a possible dark side when it blocks a necessary turn.
- **Possible self**: A plausible future identity worth examining.
- **Career checkup**: A recurring review of aspirations, learning, and fit.
- **Happiness baseline**: The tendency for situational gains to return toward a prior level.
- **Meaning**: A sense of purpose grounded in learning and contributing beyond the self.
- **Equifinality**: Multiple routes can reach the same end.
- **Multifinality**: The same starting point can lead to different ends.
- **Job crafting**: Actively reshaping daily work to fit personal values and strengths.

## Mental Models

- **Headlights, not a ten-year GPS**: Plan the next visible stretch while leaving room for new information.
- **Past cost versus future cost**: Compare the two years lost by changing with the twenty years lost by staying misaligned.
- **Actions over surroundings**: Before moving, test whether different habits, relationships, or contributions solve the real problem.

## Anti-patterns

- **“What do you want to be?” as a fixed identity test**: It narrows a child’s possible future too early.
- **Grit as a moral command**: Persistence is not automatically virtuous when the path is failing or impossible.
- **Status substitution**: Choosing a prestigious role that conflicts with values and then calling the resulting misery a failure of grit.
- **Happiness relocation**: Expecting a new place or role to fix patterns that travel with the self.
- **One-route success**: Treating a chosen destination or path as the only legitimate definition of a good life.

## Worked Example

### Ryan’s career checkup

Ryan’s childhood identity as a neurosurgeon kept overriding doubts about the work’s hours, bureaucracy, emotional cost, and his interest in business. After years of training, he eventually explored entrepreneurship, cofounded Nomad Health, advised medical-device startups, and used his medical knowledge in new ways. The later pivot did not erase the value of his medical training, but it showed that the earlier identity had been too narrow. A checkup earlier could have tested possible selves before the cost of leaving became so high. The example illustrates why future fit, learning, and contribution matter more than protecting a past narrative.

## Key Takeaways

1. Treat career and life plans as hypotheses, not permanent identities.
2. Schedule checkups before a crisis and ask what you would choose with today’s information.
3. Use possible selves and small experiments to explore alternatives safely.
4. Distinguish persistence that serves a goal from persistence that protects sunk costs.
5. Change actions and contributions before assuming a new environment will create lasting happiness.
6. Keep identity open so relationships, roles, and purposes can evolve.
