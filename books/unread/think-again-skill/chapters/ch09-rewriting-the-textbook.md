# Chapter 9: Rewriting the Textbook

## Core Idea

Grant argues that education should teach students how to question and revise knowledge, not only how to retain a teacher’s answers. Erin McCarthy uses old textbooks and student-led rewriting to show that historical accounts are selective and change as evidence and perspectives change. Active learning feels less enjoyable than a polished lecture in the moment, yet it produces deeper understanding and is better at disrupting misconceptions. Ron Berger’s classrooms make rethinking visible through “grapples,” multiple drafts, specific critique, and evolving standards of excellence. Students learn to treat confusion as a signal to investigate and feedback as a tool for improving the work rather than judging the author. The chapter extends these practices to parents, mentors, and colleagues: great educators introduce ways of thinking that remain useful after the facts of one lesson have changed.

## LEARNING, INTERRUPTED

Erin intentionally assigned an excerpt from a 1940 history textbook so students could detect errors, omissions, and whose stories were absent. She then wrote a mock history section using female pronouns and women as all the important characters, allowing a boy to feel the absence of an entire group from the narrative. Her culminating project asks students to choose an underrepresented theme, research it, and rewrite a textbook chapter; projects added the original March on Washington context and the contributions of Hispanic and Japanese American soldiers in World War II. Inquiry does not mean accepting every interpretation: Erin teaches students to examine evidence and reject Holocaust denial. Her fact-checking principles are to interrogate information, reject rank and popularity as proxies for reliability, and distinguish the sender of information from its source.

## THE DUMBSTRUCK EFFECT

In a physics experiment, students preferred lecture-based instruction and rated the lecturer as more effective, even though they learned more from active-learning sessions in which they solved problems in groups and discussed the reasoning. A meta-analysis of 225 STEM studies involving more than 46,000 undergraduates found that traditional lecture students averaged about half a letter grade lower and were 1.55 times more likely to fail. Lectures can entertain and inform, but they often make students passive receivers and are especially weak at correcting misconceptions. Inspiring delivery can also reduce scrutiny while creating the impression of superior memory. Grant does not abolish lectures; he argues that they should not dominate education when the goal is durable understanding and the ability to rethink.

## THE UNBEARABLE LIGHTNESS OF REPEATING

Philosopher Robert Nozick taught a new course almost every year because he wanted students to learn with him while he did original thinking, rather than receive a finished system. Grant adopted the spirit by replacing part of his class each year and assigning students mini-podcasts or talks that challenged conventional wisdom. Straight-A students struggled because school had rewarded being right and polished, while creative careers require risk and revision; making the assignment worth 20 percent changed the incentive. He later left one class session blank and let students pitch how to use it, leading to letters to freshmen and then “passion talks” in which students taught one another. The practice shows that changing the structure of a course can reveal expertise and curiosity that a fixed syllabus hides.

## JACK OF ROUGH DRAFTS, MASTER OF CRAFTS

Ron Berger begins with problems rather than established taxonomies, letting students invent categories, test hypotheses, and discover that frameworks are useful but not inevitable. He gives students time for confusion, then uses think-pair-share, experiments, and hands-on projects to make them leaders of their own learning. Four or more drafts are normal, not remedial; critique sessions include gallery reviews and focused discussions where students state goals, identify strengths, and ask specific, kind questions about the work. Students critique the artifact rather than the author and say “I think” instead of presenting subjective judgments as facts. Because standards of excellence also evolve, outside architects and scientists contribute new criteria and vocabularies that the class incorporates into later work.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Inquiry-based learning**: Start with questions and investigation rather than only transmitting established answers.
  - When to use: When learners need judgment, evidence evaluation, and transfer rather than memorization alone.
  - How: Let students inspect, investigate, interrogate, interpret, and defend a revised account.
- **Active learning**: Have learners solve, discuss, simulate, or teach instead of only receiving explanations.
  - When to use: For mechanisms, misconceptions, and skills that require understanding.
  - How: Present a problem, let small groups work, ask questions, then debrief the reasoning.
- **Multiple drafts**: Treat revision and feedback as part of quality, not evidence of initial failure.
  - When to use: In writing, design, art, science models, and any craft with an inspectable product.
  - How: Make versions visible, set a minimum number of drafts, and celebrate improvement.
- **Critique protocol**: Discuss goals, strengths, and development areas with specific, kind, question-based feedback.
  - When to use: When a group needs challenge without humiliation.
  - How: Critique the work, use “I think” and “Have you considered?”, and update shared standards.
- **Do science, not be a scientist**: Frame science as an activity available to everyone, not a fixed identity.
  - When to use: To preserve curiosity and reduce identity-based barriers.
  - How: Ask learners to develop hypotheses and run experiments.

## Key Concepts

- **Inquiry-based learning**: Learning through self-directed questions and evidence gathering.
- **Active learning**: Learning through doing, explaining, discussing, and solving.
- **Dumbstruck effect**: Mistaking a compelling lecture experience for deep learning.
- **Grapple**: A problem students work through in phases before receiving a settled framework.
- **Multiple drafts**: Deliberate iterations used to improve work and thinking.
- **Gallery critique**: Reviewing many works on display to identify what makes quality visible.
- **Challenge network**: Here, classmates and experts who question work constructively.
- **Fact-checker mindset**: Interrogate information rather than defer to status or popularity.

## Mental Models

- **Confusion as a compass**: When a learner is stuck, ask what new territory the confusion may signal.
- **Drafts as experiments**: Each version tests a design or explanation against feedback.
- **Teacher as co-learner**: Change some content or structure so the instructor also practices rethinking.

## Anti-patterns

- **Lecture satisfaction as learning evidence**: Enjoyment and instructor ratings can rise while mastery falls.
- **Single polished draft**: Perfection on the first try hides the value of revision and makes error threatening.
- **Deference to textbooks or experts**: Authority is not a substitute for checking evidence, source, or omission.
- **Critiquing the author**: Personal judgment creates defensiveness and suppresses the work’s improvement.

## Worked Example

### Ron Berger’s multiple-draft butterfly

First graders in Ron Berger’s network created a scientifically accurate butterfly drawing. Their classmates offered two rounds of specific suggestions about wing shape and symmetry, then later asked for a more complete pattern. The student produced successive drafts, was not discouraged by correction, and ended with a drawing far beyond the first version. The class learned that quality is a process of rethinking, reworking, and polishing, and that critique can be both demanding and kind. Grant’s own classroom adopted the same principle by rewarding students for challenging conventional wisdom and allowing them to redesign part of the course.

## Key Takeaways

1. Teach learners to interrogate sources, not only memorize their claims.
2. Use active learning when understanding mechanisms or correcting misconceptions matters.
3. Normalize confusion, multiple drafts, and feedback as the route to excellence.
4. Critique work specifically and kindly; treat standards as revisable too.
5. Let students teach, design, and question the learning environment itself.
