# Prologue: Rethinking Under Pressure

## Core Idea

The prologue uses the 1949 Mann Gulch wildfire to show that mental fitness includes the ability to rethink under pressure, not merely intelligence or speed. Foreman Wagner Dodge survived by overriding the firefighter’s trained impulse to extinguish fire and improvising an escape fire, while most of his crew remained attached to familiar tools and assumptions. The same difficulty appears in ordinary decisions: people cling to opinions, roles, and facts long after the environment changes because rethinking feels unpredictable and identity-threatening. Grant pairs this story with evidence that students usually fear changing test answers even though most revisions move from wrong to right, and with the corrected frog story to show how readily people accept familiar explanations without checking them. The book’s central invitation is to anchor identity in flexibility and learning rather than consistency. Rethinking can generate new solutions, recover old solutions for new conditions, improve conversations, and reduce regret.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Mental fitness**: Treat the ability to rethink and unlearn as a cognitive skill alongside thinking and learning.
  - When to use: When the situation, evidence, or goal has changed but an old response still feels automatic.
  - How: Notice the assumption, question whether the trained response still fits, and test an alternative before the window closes.
- **First-instinct fallacy**: The common belief that changing an answer is usually harmful; the reviewed evidence instead found that most changes were from wrong to right.
  - When to use: When a first answer feels authoritative only because it arrived first.
  - How: Spend time evaluating whether revision is warranted; the value is in reconsidering, not in changing mechanically.
- **Seizing and freezing**: Seizing a view and freezing it because conviction is more comfortable than doubt.
  - When to use: When an old belief has become part of a self-image or is being protected from new evidence.
  - How: Treat knowledge and opinions as revisable tools rather than permanent identity markers.

## Key Concepts

- **Rethinking**: Updating knowledge, assumptions, goals, or methods when evidence or circumstances warrant it.
- **Unlearning**: Releasing a once-useful belief or habit that now obstructs adaptation.
- **Escape fire**: Dodge’s improvised cleared patch of ground that turned fire from threat into shelter.
- **Mental fossil**: An outdated fact preserved by habit rather than current evidence.
- **Seizing and freezing**: Settling on a conclusion and stopping the search for revision.
- **Identity attachment**: The way tools, roles, and beliefs can become part of who someone thinks they are.

## Mental Models

- **Use the smokejumper test** when a familiar tool or goal may now be dangerous: ask what must be dropped or reversed to survive the new conditions.
- **Use a second opinion on your own judgments** when you would demand one for someone else’s diagnosis.
- **Treat assumptions as equipment**: useful in the right environment, burdensome when the environment shifts.

## Anti-patterns

- **Autopilot under change**: Well-learned responses are adaptive only in the environment that made them necessary.
- **Protecting tools as identity**: Firefighters may cling to equipment because abandoning it feels like abandoning their role, even when it slows escape.
- **Accepting a familiar story without checking it**: The frog anecdote survives because people rarely question an explanation once it feels known.

## Worked Example

### Mann Gulch and the escape fire

Dodge’s crew was sent to contain a remote lightning fire, but the blaze changed direction and accelerated toward them. He first ordered the men to drop heavy tools, then lit grass ahead of himself and sheltered in the burned patch while the wildfire passed. The crew interpreted the action through their existing role—firefighters put out fires and run away from them—so most did not follow. Later investigations suggested that earlier abandonment of packs and tools could have made the difference for many of the people who died. The deeper failure was systemic: a profession committed to extinguishing every fire had not sufficiently questioned whether some fires should be allowed to run their course.

### The Facebook opportunity missed

Grant recalls helping create an early Harvard online e-group that later became part of Facebook’s origin story. The founders treated it as a way for students to make friends, then assumed it was unnecessary once they lived on the same campus; they did not test whether it could serve other schools or become a broader social network. They also saw themselves as students rather than entrepreneurs and lacked the coding tools to pursue a larger vision. The example shows how habits, local context, and identity can close off a possibility before anyone deliberately rejects it.

## Key Takeaways

1. Reconsider your assumptions before a changing environment makes them costly.
2. A first instinct is not sacred; inspect whether revision would improve the answer.
3. Notice when a tool, role, or opinion has become part of your identity.
4. Build flexibility into ordinary decisions, not only emergencies.
5. Treat rethinking as mental fitness: humility about what you know and willingness to change course.
