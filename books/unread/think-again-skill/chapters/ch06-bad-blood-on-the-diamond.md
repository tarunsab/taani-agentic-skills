# Chapter 6: Bad Blood on the Diamond

## Core Idea

Grant examines how stereotypes and prejudice become emotionally charged through group identity, rivalry, conformity, and the need for belonging and status. Red Sox and Yankees fans can form strong animosity around an arbitrary team affiliation, then define their own identity partly by disidentifying with the rival. Attempts to bridge the divide through a shared identity or a sympathetic individual can help in limited circumstances but often leave the group stereotype intact. A more effective intervention asks people to notice the arbitrariness of their own position and imagine how their beliefs might differ in another family, place, time, or social identity. Counterfactual reflection destabilizes the certainty that a stereotype is a direct reading of reality, while meaningful intergroup contact gives people a chance to revise the group picture. The chapter also makes a moral and practical boundary clear: conversations can help, but marginalized people should not be expected to risk themselves educating people who hold power or prejudice.

## HATE ME OUT AT THE BALLGAME

Daryl Davis began asking how someone could hate him without knowing him after white spectators attacked him during a childhood parade. Years later, a white supremacist told him he was a Klansman after hearing him play piano, yet the conversation became a friendship and eventually led the man to leave the KKK. Grant uses the Boston baseball rivalry to show a milder version of the same mechanism: Red Sox fans can feel pleasure at Yankees failures, spend more money to avoid rooting for the Yankees than to root for their own team, and even treat fans of the rival as a disliked category. Rivalries matter because they combine resource competition, identity threat, and group belonging; they encourage people to preach their side and prosecute the other.

## FITTING IN AND STANDING OUT

People can develop animosity even around trivial group boundaries because groups provide belonging and status. Fans bask in reflected glory after their team wins, then distance themselves from the team after a loss, and the effect can influence behavior outside sports. Group polarization makes members’ attitudes more extreme after they discuss them with like-minded people, while conformity rewards the prototypical member who expresses the strongest view. Reminding fans that a rivalry is “just a game” can backfire because it feels like a devaluation of their identity. Once a stereotype has been socially rehearsed, evidence against it threatens both a mental model and a community position.

## WHY RED SOX FANS HATE YANKEES FANS / WHY YANKEES FANS HATE RED SOX FANS

When passionate fans were asked for negative descriptions of the opposing group, they produced reciprocal stereotypes—about accents, beards, smell, and character—rather than observations that uniquely distinguished one side. George Kelly’s reality-goggle metaphor explains why: when evidence cracks a belief, people can contort their interpretation to keep the current view intact. Socially, repeated contact with people who share the stereotype intensifies it through polarization and conformity. The two groups can therefore arrive at similar descriptions while each experiences its own account as obvious truth. The first step toward revision is seeing that a stereotype is a constructed lens, not a transparent record of a whole group.

## HYPOTHESIS 1: NOT IN A LEAGUE OF THEIR OWN

The overview effect describes astronauts returning from space with a stronger sense of global common identity and less attachment to narrow group divisions. A soccer experiment found that rival-team fans were more likely to help an injured supporter when they had first written about what they shared with other soccer fans rather than only about their own team. Yet Grant and Tim Kundro could not reliably improve Yankees–Red Sox attitudes merely by asking fans to reflect on a common identity as baseball lovers. Shared identity can motivate help in an emergency without changing a settled group stereotype in ordinary conditions. The lesson is to treat superordinate identity as a situational bridge, not a universal cure for rivalry.

## HYPOTHESIS 2: FEELING FOR OUR FOES

Herb Kelman’s Israeli–Palestinian problem-solving workshops built trust and friendships among individuals across a conflict, but participants could still retain negative stereotypes of the opposing group. Similarly, UNC students spent more time helping a financially needy Duke student and liked him more when they felt empathy, yet continued to see Duke students in general as rivals. Meeting an individual who violates a stereotype often produces an exception rather than a revised category: “this person is okay, unlike the typical member.” The person-specific change therefore does not necessarily transfer to the group-level judgment. Humanization is valuable because it recognizes individual dignity, but it does not automatically change the general belief or the structures that sustain it.

## HYPOTHESIS 3: BEASTS OF HABIT

Grant and Kundro tested whether fans would rethink the arbitrariness of their animosity. After listing both positive and negative traits of rival fans, some participants reflected on how they would likely have supported the other team if they had been born into the other family or city. Those participants chose less harmful hot sauce for the rival’s stadium, assigned easier math problems to a rival fan, and became more willing to wear the rival jersey, support its players, or acknowledge its success. The key ingredient was not finding more virtues in the rival but recognizing that the basis of hatred was accidental and socially inherited. That recognition reduced the moral confidence behind the stereotype.

## WHAT RED SOX FANS LIKE ABOUT YANKEES FANS / WHAT YANKEES FANS LIKE ABOUT RED SOX FANS

The exercise of naming positives mattered because it forced fans to see that their descriptions were not one-dimensional, but the strongest change came from questioning why the rivalry existed at all. Counterfactual thinking asks how one’s view would differ under a different birthplace, family, culture, historical period, or social identity. It creates humility about the origin of the belief, then curiosity about people who were previously compressed into a label. The technique does not require pretending that all groups or behaviors are equally good; it asks whether the available evidence warrants judging every member by a group stereotype.

## ENTERING A PARALLEL UNIVERSE

Grant’s astrology conversation with a startup founder illustrates how counterfactual questions can expose arbitrary categorization. When she considered that the same birth-date labels produce different stereotypes in China and the West, she saw the pattern as a cultural construction rather than a natural fact. Research cited in the chapter suggests that groups have more internal variation and more cross-group similarity than stereotypes imply. A meta-analysis of more than 500 studies involving over 250,000 people found that intergroup contact reduced prejudice in 94 percent of cases, although contact is not a panacea. The most reliable route to revision is often perspective-seeking through actual conversation, combined with reflection on how easily one’s own category could have been different.

## HOW A BLACK MUSICIAN CONFRONTS WHITE SUPREMACISTS

Daryl Davis challenged a KKK leader’s claim about a genetic predisposition to Black criminality by applying the same logic to white serial killers and asking whether the leader had a latent serial-killer gene. The symmetry exposed the claim’s arbitrariness and planted doubt without requiring Daryl to deliver a sermon. Over time, shared interests in music and sustained conversation helped former supremacists see him as an individual, recognize how little they knew about other groups, and reconsider inherited group narratives. Davis emphasizes that he did not convert people; he created conditions for them to examine their own direction. The case is extraordinary and dangerous to imitate without attention to power and safety: the responsibility for dismantling oppression belongs especially to those with power, not to the people targeted by it.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Group polarization**: Discussion among like-minded members pushes attitudes toward more extreme positions.
  - When to use: Diagnose escalating certainty in partisan, organizational, or identity-based communities.
  - How: Add real diversity, question prototypical extremes, and interrupt conformity incentives.
- **Common-identity bridge / overview effect**: Activate a broader identity to reduce rivalry or increase help.
  - When to use: In moments where a shared human or activity-based identity is salient.
  - How: Name what is shared, while recognizing that a common label may not change general stereotypes.
- **Arbitrariness reflection**: Ask how one’s belief would differ under another birthplace, family, culture, or era.
  - When to use: When prejudice rests on inherited or weakly examined categories.
  - How: Make the counterfactual concrete, then compare the certainty of the belief with the evidence behind it.
- **Intergroup contact**: Use sustained, meaningful interaction with members of another group to create opportunities for revision.
  - When to use: When stereotypes are maintained by distance and lack of personal knowledge.
  - How: Seek reciprocal, safe contact; do not treat a single “exception” as the entire intervention.

## Key Concepts

- **Stereotype**: A generalized belief about a group that compresses individual variation.
- **Prejudice**: Negative evaluation or feeling directed at a group or its members.
- **Rivalry**: An identity-laden relationship marked by competition and special animosity.
- **Disidentification**: Defining oneself partly by what one’s group is not.
- **Group polarization**: Movement toward more extreme group attitudes after like-minded discussion.
- **Conformity**: Adjusting views or behavior to gain belonging or status in a group.
- **Overview effect**: Astronauts’ reported shift toward global identity after seeing Earth from space.
- **Counterfactual thinking**: Imagining how different circumstances would produce different beliefs or outcomes.
- **Intergroup contact**: Interaction across group boundaries that can reduce prejudice.

## Mental Models

- **Reality goggles**: A stereotype is a lens; ask what it hides and what would crack it.
- **Exception versus category**: Notice whether a sympathetic individual changed the group belief or was simply exempted.
- **Parallel-universe test**: Would the belief survive if your birth, place, or identity changed?

## Anti-patterns

- **“It’s just a game” minimization**: Dismissing identity can make people defend the rivalry more aggressively.
- **Token individualism**: Treating one admirable member as an exception leaves the stereotype untouched.
- **Assuming empathy solves systems**: A warm interaction can coexist with group prejudice and structural inequality.
- **Outsourcing anti-prejudice work to targets**: Asking marginalized people to educate hostile groups can reproduce the burden and risk.

## Worked Example

### Rethinking the Yankees–Red Sox rivalry

Fans first listed negative traits of the rival and then, in one condition, reflected on how arbitrary their animosity was. The reflection changed concrete choices: less punitive hot sauce, easier test questions for a rival fan, and greater willingness to support the rival team. It also changed general attitudes, including whether the rival’s failure counted as one’s own success. The results show why asking people to like the other side may be weaker than helping them question why they hate it. Counterfactual reflection loosens the identity grip so later contact and empathy have more room to work.

## Key Takeaways

1. Ask whether a group judgment is evidence-based or an accident of identity and socialization.
2. Use counterfactual questions to make the origin of a stereotype visible.
3. Distinguish humanizing one person from revising the group category.
4. Use meaningful intergroup contact when it is safe and reciprocal.
5. Treat shared identity as a bridge with limits, not proof that prejudice has disappeared.
