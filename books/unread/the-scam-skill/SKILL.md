# The Scam — Study Skill

## How to Use

Use this skill to explain, compare, or apply the book's account of the 1992 securities scam, the 2001 Ketan Parekh episode, and the institutions around them. Start with the chapter that contains the relevant actor or mechanism, then follow the linked connections and the supporting files. Treat claims about fraud, bribery, collusion, and regulatory intent as the authors' reported allegations or interpretations unless the chapter explicitly distinguishes an admission, finding, conviction, or other outcome. The book is historical and investigative, not current legal, investment, or compliance advice.

## Core Frameworks

1. **Substitutes and opacity.** Bank Receipts, Securities General Ledgers, settlement entries, and off-market carry-forward trades allowed paperwork or book entries to stand in for cash and securities. The key question is always what asset, delivery, or control actually existed behind the document.
2. **Regulatory arbitrage.** Forced statutory allocations, artificial bond pricing, permissive Portfolio Management Services, and fragmented exchange rules created spaces where institutions could obtain returns while shifting risk outside ordinary balance-sheet controls.
3. **Network codependency.** Banks needed brokers for speed and portfolio management; brokers needed bank or corporate money; mutual funds and public-sector companies supplied scale; promoters supplied stories and stock. A scam becomes durable when every participant can describe another participant as the real owner of the risk.
4. **Principal escalation.** Harshad Mehta and Ketan Parekh are shown moving from intermediary or broker roles into concentrated principal positions. Leverage, publicity, promoter alliances, and the refusal to exit convert a trading edge into a systemic vulnerability.
5. **Fiduciary conflict.** The book repeatedly tests whether banks, mutual funds, exchanges, boards, and regulators acted for depositors, unit-holders, investors, or the public—or to protect reputations and connected insiders.
6. **Enforcement as a second failure.** Fragmented agencies, weak evidence management, closed inquiries, personnel turnover, and slow courts can punish visible juniors while leaving money, institutions, and senior decision-makers untouched.
7. **Narrative power.** Media access, “Big Bull” mythology, research branding, political proximity, and personal attacks shape who is believed, who receives funding, and which trail is investigated.
