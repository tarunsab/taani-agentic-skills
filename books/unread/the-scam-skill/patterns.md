# Patterns

Recurring mechanisms extracted from the book. Each pattern is a diagnostic model, not a claim that every similar transaction is unlawful.

## 1. Substitute paper for an asset

**When it appears:** a bank receives a BR, allotment letter, or account entry instead of a security or cash. The mechanism works while the recipient trusts the issuer and the custodian is not independently reconciled. It appears in SBI's Mehta exposure, Stanchart's Karad/Metro paper, Fairgrowth certificates, and DSQ's shares. **Test:** confirm the underlying asset with the independent issuing or holding institution, then check whether the document is still outstanding.

## 2. Route money through a network of legal entities

**When it appears:** a broker, bank, promoter, or fund needs to move funds without showing a direct exposure. Accounts at GTB, Parekh companies, affiliates, and shell entities made same-day transfers appear unrelated. The 2001 chain through Classic Credit, Panther firms, Chitrakoot, Goldfish, and Nakshatra is a clear example. **Test:** consolidate common control, same-day transfers, common directors, and shared brokers before assessing exposure.

## 3. Manufacture liquidity before selling inventory

**When it appears:** an illiquid stock must look attractive to institutional buyers. Parekh generated inter-exchange and circular volume, timed announcements, and then placed stock with mutual funds or FIIs. Harshad used publicity and market pressure for a similar effect. **Trade-off:** manufactured liquidity can support a price temporarily but leaves the operator holding inventory when outside demand stops.

## 4. Use a connected institution as a shock absorber

**When it appears:** a fund, bank, or public-sector company buys or finances a failing position to avoid an immediate default. UTI's DSQ purchase and Shriram Mutual Fund's Videocon bailout show how the rescuer can move the loss to unit-holders. GTB and Madhavpura served as credit shock absorbers for Parekh. **Test:** identify the fiduciary duty, price paid, independent demand, and post-bailout liquidation value.

## 5. Hide effective financing inside a trading label

**When it appears:** a ready forward, differential brokerage, “arbitrage,” or cash-management service performs the economic function of a loan. CSFB's early payment and differential brokerage, Citi's PMS, and Nedungadi's “arbitrage” illustrate the pattern. The label may be technically plausible while the cash-flow direction shows financing. **Test:** calculate the implied interest, timing, collateral, and who bears the settlement risk.

## 6. Let reputation substitute for controls

**When it appears:** a famous broker, foreign bank, star analyst, or respected banker receives latitude that a less connected participant would not. Mehta's taxpayer and Big Bull image, First Global's research brand, and GTB's regulatory reassurance all turned reputation into operating capital. **Trade-off:** reputation can reduce transaction friction but makes independent verification more important, not less.

## 7. Fragment the investigation

**When it appears:** CBI, RBI, tax, SEBI, the JPC, and a court each hold a partial record. The book argues that agencies pursued separate mandates, inflated turnover, withheld information, and punished easy targets while the network remained intact. **Test:** build one transaction matrix with cash, securities, beneficial owner, legal issue, decision-maker, and outcome columns; no agency's label should replace the shared map.

## 8. Convert delay into a defense

**When it appears:** litigation, appeals, staff transfers, deaths, asset freezes, and missing records outlast the original market event. Foreign banks could write off losses, while public banks and smaller defendants remained in court; Ketan Parekh's promised repayments bought time without restoring money. **Trade-off:** delay may reduce immediate contagion but destroys evidence and weakens recovery. **Test:** maintain a live liability ledger and time-bound recovery plan.

## 9. Turn media access into market power

**When it appears:** an operator or company controls columns, research, publicity, or a media stake while influencing the stock. Harshad's post-1992 columns and website, Parekh's entertainment network, and First Global's research and Tehelka links show different versions. **Test:** disclose ownership, financing, proprietary positions, placement fees, and timing of recommendations; separate reported facts from promotional claims.
