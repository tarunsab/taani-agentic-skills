# Chapter 21: Play It Again Sam

## Core Idea

Scam 2001 is presented as a replay of the 1992 pattern with a different operator, funding mix, and market infrastructure. Bank of India's Rs 130 crore hole led to Madhavpura and Ketan Parekh; UTI held K-10 stocks; Nirmal Bang provided multiple trading cards; GTB and Fairgrowth-like entrepreneurial banking supplied credit; and brokers, corporates, FIIs, and exchanges multiplied the position. The chapter then follows Credit Suisse First Boston (CSFB), First Global, and Nirmal Bang to show how foreign accounts, proprietary trading, routed orders, and synchronized trades could move money and stock without transparent ownership. Regulators again had information but did not connect it. The useful comparison is not “1992 repeated exactly,” but “the same incentive architecture survived modernization.”

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Sequel mapping:** compare actors, funding sources, market plumbing, regulators, and failure triggers across scandals.
- **Cross-bourse bubble:** rolling positions between exchanges can create large apparent liquidity from finite capital.
- **Synchronized trade:** matched orders can move funds, hide beneficial ownership, or manufacture volume.
- **Research-brand conflict:** a firm can claim fundamental research while using its market access to place or trade promoted stock.

## Key Concepts

### The replay matrix

The chapter explicitly pairs Harshad with Ketan, SBI with Bank of India, UTI with the mutual-fund nexus, Hiten Dalal with Nirmal Bang, Fairgrowth with GTB, and the 1992 JPC with its successor. It also notes that 2001 involved more private corporate money and fewer banks, while the market now had BSE/NSE daily volumes around Rs 12,000 crore and many exchanges. Poor settlement design and the absence of uniform dates allowed positions to roll across bourses. The regulator's warning signs were familiar, but the infrastructure made the bubble appear more sophisticated.

### Credit Suisse First Boston

SEBI identified five Mauritius OCBs operating for Parekh and a reported Rs 2,900 crore repatriated abroad from March 1999 to March 2001. CSFB paid for some sales before receiving the official stock delivery and charged differential brokerage that looked like 7–14 day financing. Synchronized buy and sell orders in Adani Exports, Shonkh Technologies, GTB, and Zee were valued at about Rs 1,813 crore in the first quarter of 2001. The “1,2,3 system” used identical orders entered into computers and executed on a telephone count, allegedly allowing Parekh to sell through an FII account and buy through an Indian broker.

### Sachin Tendulkar of the market

First Global, run by Shankar Sharma and Devina Mehra, built a research-driven, contrarian brand and attracted FII business. It also financed Tehelka, placed HFCL at Rs 1,050, and promoted Global Tele, HFCL, and Vikas WSP even as the stocks later collapsed. SEBI found routed proprietary trades through Vruddhi, Bang firms, Palombe, and CSL; it alleged naked short sales, synchronized circular trades, and possible front-running. First Global denied the charges aggressively, and the chapter preserves both the firm's defense and the regulator's interpretation rather than treating rhetoric as evidence.

### The quiet operator: Nirmal Bang

Bang used five entities and multiple sub-brokers to provide exposure limits and a nationwide channel for day traders and large operators. SEBI identified heavy short sales timed to price collapses, including several lakh Global Tele shares sold in seven minutes on March 2. Palombe and CSL linked Bang to both Parekh and First Global, while unsecured corporate loans and fund transfers suggested a broker–corporate nexus. BSE president Anand Rathi was also forced out after allegations that he passed market information to short sellers, echoing the 1998 BSE cover-up.

## Mental Models

Build a **sequel matrix** with five columns: operator, funding source, market channel, regulator warning, failure trigger. Then test whether the new technology changed the economic substance. Matched orders, early payment, differential brokerage, and multiple cards should be analyzed as a single transaction when they perform one financing or ownership function.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming automated trading makes a transaction independent or transparent.
- Treating research branding as separate from placement and proprietary trading incentives.
- Comparing scandals only by the first default amount.

## Worked Example

The CSFB funding trade is the worked case. An overseas account sold K-10 shares, CSFB released money early with a differential brokerage charge, and Parekh-linked Indian brokers bought matching shares. The exchange settlement mechanism then protected the financier while obscuring the economic round trip. The practical response is to reconcile order time, beneficial owner, delivery date, brokerage, and funding cost in one record.

## Key Takeaways

1. Scam 2001 changed the plumbing but retained the network logic.
2. Volume and automation can make collusion harder to see, not harder to perform.
3. Research, brokerage, proprietary positions, and corporate finance must be separated by controls.
