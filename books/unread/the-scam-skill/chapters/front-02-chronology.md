# Chronology

## Core Idea

The chronology places the two scams inside a long sequence of warnings, interventions, arrests, inquiries, bailouts, and delayed outcomes. Its most important pattern is not one spectacular day but the repeated interval between a warning and effective action. The 1992 sequence begins with the April 23 report that SBI wanted Harshad Mehta to square roughly Rs 500 crore, followed by parliamentary attention, RBI and CBI action, the Janakiraman committee, arrests, the Special Court ordinance, and the JPC. The 2001 sequence begins with the February 28 Budget and March 2 market fall, then moves through the MMCB/Bank of India pay-order crisis, SEBI action, the JPC, and the eventual GTB moratorium and forced merger in July 2004. The dates make visible how discovery, investigation, recovery, and punishment moved at very different speeds.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Warning-to-action lag:** an early report or inspection may exist long before an agency acts.
- **Crisis cascade:** a market fall reveals payment, banking, and governance failures in sequence.
- **Institutional memory failure:** the same actors, mechanisms, and warnings recur across 1992 and 2001.

## Key Concepts

### Scam 1992

The chronology records the rapid spring 1992 escalation: SBI's reconciliation problem, Parliament's response, the Janakiraman inquiry, the Indian Express report on NHB money, CBI raids and arrests, the discovery of Stanchart and smaller-bank exposures, and the creation of the Special Court and JPC. It also marks the deaths and resignations that changed the investigation's continuity. Dates such as May 21, when MJ Pherwani died, and June 4, when Harshad and SBI's DMD were arrested, carry both factual and narrative weight because they altered who could explain the money trail.

### Scam 2001 and aftermath

The second sequence starts with the Sensex fall after the Budget and the collapse of K-10 stocks, then tracks CSE turmoil, the arrests of Ketan Parekh and other operators, UTI and SEBI actions, the JPC report in December 2002, the continuing GTB questions, and the July 2004 moratorium and amalgamation. The chronology ends in an unresolved register: investigations, bans, and institutional changes occur, but the book repeatedly asks whether they recovered money or prevented recurrence.

## Mental Models

Use the chronology as a “control-room” layer before reading an individual chapter. Mark four dates for any episode: first warning, first visible failure, first official intervention, and first final disposition. When those dates are separated by years, the gap is itself evidence about institutional capacity and incentives.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating the arrest date as the discovery date.
- Treating a committee appointment as a completed investigation.
- Treating a penalty, merger, or ban as proof that losses were recovered or the mechanism was closed.

## Worked Example

The 1992 chain is diagnostic: April 23's newspaper report precedes the May 6 exposure of NHB funding; the May 30 first Janakiraman report precedes the later public reconstruction of Stanchart and Karad exposures; and the July JPC formation precedes years of litigation. A reader who follows only the dramatic June arrests misses the earlier accounting and inspection signals and the much later recovery problem.

## Key Takeaways

1. The book's central chronology is a history of delayed response.
2. The two scams share mechanisms and actors despite different market settings.
3. A crisis timeline should separate exposure, intervention, evidence, recovery, and adjudication.
