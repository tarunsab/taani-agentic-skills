# Chapter 17: Who Won, Who Lost, Who Got Away

## Core Idea

The chapter distributes the consequences of the 1992 scandal and argues that the visible losses were not the same as the economic or legal winners. Harshad Mehta, some officials, and many junior bank employees lost money, careers, or reputation, while foreign banks, politically connected industrialists, ministers, regulators, and public-sector institutions often escaped meaningful scrutiny. Citibank's PMS and forward transactions, Stanchart's weak claims, ANZ Grindlays' NHB dispute, coupon-hike trades, and corporate price support are used to show how gains and losses moved across institutions. The authors repeatedly distinguish a named connection from a provable offence, but argue that incomplete investigation itself protected the strongest participants. The chapter is a map of distributed responsibility and asymmetric accountability.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Incidence of loss:** identify who ultimately bears the cash loss, not merely who appears in the transaction.
- **Escape through complexity:** cross-bank, cross-market, and cross-jurisdiction structures reduce the chance of a complete case.
- **Punishment asymmetry:** visible or low-power participants can be punished while senior beneficiaries negotiate, transfer, or write off.

## Key Concepts

### The supply side of the scam

The authors identify PMS and public-sector money as a core supply source, with Citibank at the center of many channels. Janakiraman material cited in the chapter describes Citibank's forward commitments of about Rs 14,758 crore outside its main audited system, along with dummy customers and mismatched books. PFC, GIC, UTI, and corporate bond transactions allegedly provided clients or brokers with returns and liquidity while hiding the downside. The absence of a criminal prosecution against the foreign bank is presented as a major accountability gap.

### Specific trails and unresolved responsibility

The chapter discusses PFC's 14.25% arrangements, questionable Birla bond purchases, a GIC Mutual account, and a circular involving PFC, GIC, Grasim, UTI, and Mehta. It also describes Stanchart's later inability to prove much of its claimed loss, the ANZ/NHB dispute, and suspicious SBI purchases before coupon-rate increases. Industrialist links to Harshad and admissions by T B Ruia are contrasted with dropped charges or absent follow-up. These are leads and reported findings in the authors' reconstruction, not a blanket legal conclusion about every named party.

### People who paid

Middle and junior bank officers were often prosecuted for technical violations or negligence, even when they lacked control over the network. Meanwhile, senior bankers changed jobs, foreign executives left India, and some institutions wrote off losses. The chapter lists deaths that damaged continuity and notes that shares, properties, and benami holdings stayed tied up with the Custodian. The result was a social distribution of cost that did not track decision power.

## Mental Models

Use a **winner–loser–escaper ledger**. For each transaction, list the immediate beneficiary, ultimate loss-bearer, person with decision authority, and person who avoided investigation or recovery. This keeps “lost money,” “lost a job,” “was convicted,” and “left with the gain” analytically separate.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming a write-off is a neutral business decision when public or client money bears it.
- Treating a transfer or resignation as accountability.
- Reading a list of names as proof without checking the underlying transaction and outcome.

## Worked Example

Citibank's PMS is the chapter's example. A bank could earn fees or trading gains, brokers could receive funding, and clients could absorb the loss, while books separated or omitted the second leg. To identify the winner, a reviewer must follow the cash and securities past the bank's customer label and into the final settlement.

## Key Takeaways

1. The largest economic winners may be the least visible in the legal record.
2. Complexity can distribute losses while concentrating gains.
3. Accountability must be measured by recovery and decision power, not only by arrests.
