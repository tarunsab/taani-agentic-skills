# Chapter 20: Ketan's Kamaal

## Core Idea

Ketan Parekh is presented as a quieter successor to Harshad Mehta whose operation was built around institutional exits, concentrated “K-10” stocks, corporate funding, and an enormous web of shell companies and accounts. He bought promoter stakes at discounts, generated liquidity and news, and aimed to place the shares with fund managers rather than rely on a promoter buyback or retail worship. Technology, media, and telecom stories made stocks such as HFCL, Zee, Global Tele, DSQ, and Aftek appear liquid and institutionally desirable. As prices fell, Parekh violated his own discipline by holding beloved core stocks, supporting them with GTB, Madhavpura, and promoter money, and refusing to exit. The crisis became visible when Bank of India's discounted Madhavpura pay orders were dishonoured, exposing a funding chain and roughly Rs 130 crore immediate liability.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Liquidity manufacture:** artificial volume makes an illiquid stock pass an institutional selection test.
- **Institutional exit:** a manipulator's exit depends on fund managers and mutual funds accepting the inventory.
- **Promoter funding loop:** companies whose prices are being supported lend money back to the operator.
- **Attachment to core positions:** personal relationships turn a trader into an insider who cannot sell.

## Key Concepts

### Modus operandi

Parekh selected discounted promoter stakes, put the stock “in play,” and created volume through inter-exchange arbitrage and circular trading. Announcements about contracts, acquisitions, exports, and profits were timed to the price movement. The K-10 list included Silverline, Global Telesystems, Satyam Computers, Zee, HFCL, SSI, Aftek, DSQ, Ranbaxy, and Pentamedia. Parekh's key difference from Harshad was his focus on persuading fund managers; his family firm NH Securities already had institutional access.

### The bubble portfolio

HFCL moved from about Rs 40 to Rs 2,500, Zee reached roughly Rs 1,500 at a P/E around 250, Global Tele rose from about Rs 80 to Rs 3,550, and DSQ moved from about Rs 250 to Rs 2,500. The chapter treats these numbers as evidence of scale, not as valuation benchmarks. Parekh also accumulated GTB and used relationships with Vinay Maloo, Manoj Tirodkar, Subhash Chandra, and entertainment companies such as ABCL, Tips, Balaji, UTV, and others to broaden the network.

### Funding the rescue

When TMT stocks fell, Parekh remained exposed—at one point his HFCL position was nearly 60% of the BSE carry-forward position. GTB exposure to known Parekh entities was reported above Rs 250 crore, while the bank's chairman publicly cited a Rs 100 crore individual-broker limit. Madhavpura issued pay orders to Parekh companies, which Bank of India discounted; SEBI later found Madhavpura exposure through eleven Parekh firms of about Rs 798 crore, far above the admitted Rs 200 crore and the regulatory Rs 15 crore limit. Zee and HFCL routed about Rs 669 crore to Parekh-linked firms, with additional support from Kopran and other promoters.

## Mental Models

For an illiquid stock, separate **price**, **volume**, **ownership**, and **funding**. A high volume number is not genuine liquidity if the same group trades back and forth. Then ask whether the issuers, banks, and fund managers all benefit from a higher price; common incentives can explain why an apparently diversified market is one position.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating inter-exchange volume as independent demand.
- Assuming fund-manager ownership proves the stock was fundamentally vetted.
- Letting a trader's friendship with a promoter override exit discipline.

## Worked Example

The Madhavpura–BOI pay-order chain is the chapter's worked example. Parekh companies presented Rs 137 crore of pay orders; BOI credited or discounted them before RBI declared Madhavpura a defaulter, and the bank then discovered transfers to Chitrakoot, Goldfish, and Nakshatra. That chain turns a price story into a cash-flow map and explains why an immediate Rs 130 crore shortfall could trigger a much larger market crisis.

## Key Takeaways

1. Parekh's innovation was to manufacture liquidity for institutional distribution.
2. Promoter and bank financing made the stock price a shared solvency problem.
3. The final failure came from refusing to exit and relying on short-term refinancing.
