# Chapter 14: The Buccaneer Bankers

## Core Idea

This chapter argues that foreign banks were not detached victims of an Indian scam; some operated aggressively inside the same weak market and used their reputations to shield risky practices. Citibank, Standard Chartered, and Bank of America used brokers, PMS, ready forwards, and public-sector funds to generate trading profits, while records, reserve requirements, and exposure limits were treated as obstacles. The authors contrast Citibank's sophisticated version of the machine with Harshad Mehta's cruder, more visible version. Janakiraman's findings—BRs where SGLs were possible, unsigned paper, missing delivery records, and settled bounced SGLs—show that the problem was institutional. The chapter's broader claim is that management transfers and “clerical error” explanations let global banks exit reputationally while local officers bore the consequences.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Reputation arbitrage:** a prestigious institution receives more trust than its controls deserve.
- **PMS risk transfer:** a bank books fees and trading gains while clients absorb losses.
- **Denial-and-transfer cycle:** deny, label the error clerical, settle or write off, and move senior staff.

## Key Concepts

### Citibank's operating model

Citibank used front brokers, client and proprietary accounts, and ready-forward transactions to move money and securities. The chapter highlights transactions with Canfina in which second legs were not accepted, the use of unsigned or unsecured BRs, and missing contract notes and delivery documents. In the GIC Rise example, C Mackertich and Stewart made roughly Rs 89 crore through a structure that charged PMS clients. The authors present these not as isolated mistakes but as a repeatable way to turn a bank's access and reputation into trading leverage.

### Public-sector and PMS money

Banks used labels such as assured, benchmark, or indicative returns even though the rules required market risk. A Telco guarantee of roughly Rs 85 crore and PFC money of about Rs 150 crore are cited as examples of clients or public-sector institutions being pulled into transactions that benefited brokers. The bank could describe the transaction as client service while transferring price or liquidity risk to the client. This is the same economic mechanism seen in Canfina and SBI, but backed by an international brand.

### BankAm and common excuses

Bank of America is described as selling IRFC bonds to Andhra Bank for about Rs 102.48 crore and buying them back for Rs 90 crore, and as making clean loans to Mehta and Polyolefins. When challenged, senior management invoked clerical errors or local staff failures. The chapter argues that the recurring absence of criminal accountability at the top was a regulatory outcome, not proof that the behavior was harmless.

## Mental Models

Use a **reputation discount** in diligence: the more a counterparty relies on brand, foreign ownership, or “professional” status, the more independently verify the asset, delivery, and client allocation. Compare the economic result for the bank, broker, and PMS client; asymmetric outcomes reveal hidden risk transfer.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating foreign ownership as a control environment.
- Accepting “clerical error” without checking whether the same error benefits the institution repeatedly.
- Imposing penalties without tracing client losses and senior decision rights.

## Worked Example

The GIC Rise transactions are a compact test. C Mackertich and Stewart earned about Rs 89 crore, but the structure placed the cost on PMS clients. The relevant question is not whether a document was signed; it is who earned the upside, who carried the downside, and whether the client had knowingly accepted that risk.

## Key Takeaways

1. Brand and regulation can coexist with aggressive market conduct.
2. PMS and public-sector money made private trading losses socially transferable.
3. Accountability requires following the economic beneficiary, not stopping at the local employee.
