# Chapter 4: A Greenhorn

## Core Idea

Harshad Mehta's later scale is presented as an accumulation of learned behaviors, not as an inexplicable genius. The chapter follows him from a family shop and a short period in an insurance investment department into the BSE ring, where he learned jobbing, market-making, rumor, settlement pressure, and the emotional cost of being trapped in a position. Early losses taught him that knowing how to enter was not the same as knowing how to exit. His 1980s battles over individual shares, especially the SPIC episode, also taught him that external funding and institutional relationships mattered more than personal conviction. By 1987 he was ready to move from broker to principal and to use the money market as a larger source of leverage.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Operator formation:** repeated small trades, losses, and social learning create a later operating style.
- **Exit constraint:** a position is incomplete until the operator can sell or deliver without collapsing the price.
- **Principal transition:** brokerage becomes proprietary risk-taking when the intermediary wants to capture the whole upside.

## Key Concepts

### Learning the ring

After arriving in Bombay in 1973, Mehta tried several businesses before joining New India Assurance's investment department in 1977. He entered the BSE ring as a jobber through P Ambalal and initially lost about Rs 2,000. The chapter depicts the ring as an apprenticeship in liquidity: a jobber has to quote, absorb inventory, read other operators, and survive settlement cycles. Information from a Premier Auto plant contact later gave him a crude form of equity research, although the authors note that insider trading was not expressly prohibited in India until November 1992.

### The cost of being cornered

In 1982 Mehta accumulated or supported positions in Blow Plast and Bharat Gears, then learned that it was easier to buy a position than to escape one. On “Black Thursday” in March he lost roughly Rs 10 lakh, pawned jewellery, and faced personal and family pressure. His small Growmore office then shifted toward managing client portfolios. The SPIC episode in 1986 was another stress test: a rumor of default and a bear attack forced him to pay early, survive the squeeze, and clean up weak client positions.

### The strategic turn

The margin structure created a lose-lose problem for a broker: if clients lost, the broker was exposed; if the broker carried the position, he needed far more capital. Mehta therefore sought a different arena where bank and institutional money could support larger trades. In 1987 he turned toward the money market, while retaining the lessons of his earlier equity battles: control the flow of funds, cultivate the source of liquidity, and build enough market presence that other participants must respond.

## Mental Models

Use the “entry–carry–exit” test for any celebrated operator. What created the position? Who financed the carrying period? Who was expected to buy or accept delivery at the exit? A track record made during rising prices says little about the third question.

## Anti-patterns

- Mistaking persistence after an early loss for proof of superior skill.
- Treating privileged information as research quality.
- Scaling a position before identifying a credible exit and loss-bearing capital.

## Worked Example

The SPIC episode is a compact operating lesson. A rumor threatened the share, the bear side expected a failure, and Mehta chose to make payment early rather than allow the rumor to define him. He survived, but the episode also convinced him that the stock market alone could not supply reliable funding. In the later scam, that search for dependable outside liquidity became a much larger vulnerability.

## Key Takeaways

1. Harshad's style grew from market-making, repeated risk, and an obsession with control.
2. Early positions exposed the difference between information, conviction, and exit liquidity.
3. The move into money markets was a financing decision before it was an investment thesis.
