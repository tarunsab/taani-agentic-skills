# Chapter 12: The Fairgrowth Story

## Core Idea

Fairgrowth was conceived as a financial supermarket linking banking, securities, housing finance, and other businesses, but it was built around relationships and borrowed money rather than durable governance. Its leaders filled boards with loyalists and gave mismatched roles to trusted associates; while Ratnakar lived, his authority held the pieces together. The company and its affiliate ABFSL borrowed heavily from public-sector institutions and routed funds through brokers, including Hiten Dalal, Stanchart, VBD, and NHB. Reported profits and trades looked impressive, but the balance sheet contained fictitious certificates, forged allotments, and paper assets that could not support the liabilities. The chapter shows how a high-growth “supermarket” becomes a shell when the control system cannot distinguish client assets, borrowed cash, and the operator's own positions.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Conglomerate illusion:** many related entities create an appearance of diversification while concentrating one risk.
- **Borrow-and-route model:** a financial company raises funds from institutions and passes them through brokers or affiliates.
- **Paper asset collapse:** certificates and allotment letters can inflate assets until one counterparty asks for delivery.

## Key Concepts

### Ambition without structure

Fairgrowth planned alliances with large industrial houses and presented itself as a broad financial platform. But the boards were loyalty-heavy and operational roles did not match competence. Before Ratnakar's death, Fairgrowth reported about Rs 81 crore of pre-tax profit on Rs 89 crore of income and trades exceeding Rs 7,000 crore, while borrowings were around Rs 500 crore. The board did not understand the exposure; when Ratnakar died, a power struggle exposed how little institutional structure existed beneath the growth.

### ABFSL as a conduit

Syndicate Bank collected about Rs 337 crore in PMS and passed around Rs 90 crore onward; other routes moved roughly Rs 200 crore. ABFSL, created in 1991 and linked to former Ratnakar associate K R Nayak, borrowed from public-sector companies and passed funds to Fairgrowth, brokers, and institutions. The chapter says Fairgrowth's picks reached roughly Rs 1,769 crore while ABFSL's assets and receivables were largely paper. The relationship between a bank affiliate and the financial company blurred whether a transaction was investment, financing, or diversion.

### Fictitious securities

Investigators found fake securities receipts, altered UTI certificates, and false HUDCO and NTPC allotments. Fairgrowth allegedly owed ABFSL about Rs 240 crore, but its assets were not real enough to cover the claim; the shortfall was estimated at about Rs 206.42 crore, leaving a net figure near Rs 105 crore after adjustments. Employees and senior figures were arrested, and the chapter also records allegations of investigative abuse. Institutional debts were later paid or settled in part, but the story remained a warning about paper wealth.

## Mental Models

For a financial group, consolidate every affiliate and ask whether the same rupee is being counted as a deposit, receivable, security, and investment. Then verify certificates with the issuing institution rather than with the affiliate that presents them. A “supermarket” is diversified only if the risks, records, and decision rights are independent.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating group companies as independent counterparties.
- Inferring solvency from reported trading profit without validating assets.
- Letting a founder's relationship network replace board-level risk visibility.

## Worked Example

The ABFSL–Fairgrowth path is the worked case: public-sector funds enter ABFSL, pass to Fairgrowth or named brokers, and return as paper claims or certificates. Once investigators compare the alleged allotments with UTI, HUDCO, and NTPC records, the asset side collapses. The practical test is independent confirmation of both the asset and the beneficiary.

## Key Takeaways

1. A financial conglomerate can hide concentration behind many legal entities.
2. Reported profit is weak evidence when securities and receivables are unverified.
3. Governance must survive the death or departure of the central operator.
