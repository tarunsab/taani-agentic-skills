# Chapter 10: Stanchart's Money Trail

## Core Idea

Stanchart's attempt to recover money shows how difficult a financial scandal becomes when the institution has accepted weak paper, allowed a large exposure, and then has to negotiate with the people who may explain it. At meetings in The Chambers on May 21 and 23, 1992, the bank heard shifting estimates involving Karad and Metro, Hiten's position, and the liabilities of Narottam, Ruia, and Gandhi. Hiten promised securities, cash, or indemnity, then changed his story; books disappeared or were burned, cheques bounced, and a later letter alleging coercion complicated the record. The bank sought CBI assistance and filed numerous claims against banks and mutual funds, but much of the trail remained a “shot in the dark.” The chapter's practical insight is that recovery requires contemporaneous documents and cross-party reconciliation, not just a confession or a large claim.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Recovery under information asymmetry:** the claimant must reconstruct the loss while the counterparty controls records and explanations.
- **Negotiated evidence:** settlement offers can reveal capacity and liability, but can also contaminate later testimony.
- **Claim decomposition:** separate direct loss, bank-to-bank settlement, borrowed funds, and suspected onward transfers.

## Key Concepts

### The Chambers meetings

Stanchart's early estimates included roughly Rs 350 crore in Karad BRs, Rs 400 crore paid to Karad through Metro-linked dealings, and about Rs 550 crore connected to Metro; the numbers were still moving. Hiten's account was around Rs 720 crore, while the other named parties were associated with about Rs 580 crore. He said the money had been used to plug other banks' holes or had been lost, while the bank wanted securities and books. The mismatch between the bank's ledger, the participants' stories, and the underlying assets made immediate recovery urgent and reliable attribution difficult.

### Letters, cheques, and burned books

Hiten's May 11 letter offered indemnity and security, but his May 27 letter retracted the commitment and alleged pressure. Cheques presented for clearing bounced. In a June interview he said Gandhi was the brain, Bhupen and Ruia benefited, roughly Rs 600 crore was used, and much of it funded other banks, but later he became evasive and said he had been deceived. Narottam's office was locked and records were reportedly burned. These contradictions are not automatically proof of one version; they show why a recovery team must preserve documents before negotiating.

### Agencies and the litigation strategy

Stanchart approached the Attorney General and CBI, but jurisdiction, staffing, and competing investigations slowed action. RBI official Parameshwaran suggested that some money was used for rollovers, yet the trail was not pursued far enough. Stanchart eventually filed 24 claims against ten banks and mutual funds, totaling about Rs 990 crore, partly because the bank could not tell which institution ultimately held the loss.

## Mental Models

Construct a **loss waterfall**: original cash outflow → document received → asset due → intermediate bank → broker or promoter → final beneficiary → recovered amount. Label each step as proven, admitted, inferred, or disputed. This avoids treating the gross exposure as identical to the amount any one defendant owes.

## Anti-patterns

- Negotiating away records before preserving them.
- Counting every connected account as a proven beneficiary.
- Filing a gross claim without decomposing the underlying transaction paths.

## Worked Example

Hiten's shifting commitment is a useful evidence exercise. The May 11 indemnity letter, the later retraction, the bounced cheques, and the June interviews should be placed on one timeline alongside Stanchart's ledger and the Karad/Metro documents. The value is not in choosing the most dramatic statement; it is in identifying what each statement can and cannot establish.

## Key Takeaways

1. A bank's recovery effort can become a second investigation with its own evidence risks.
2. Gross exposures must be reconciled to asset, cash, and beneficiary paths.
3. Delay destroys records, witnesses, and bargaining power at the same time.
