# Chapter 3: Creed of Greed

## Core Idea

The authors argue that the 1992 scandal was systemic because regulation, infrastructure, incentives, and professional culture all pushed institutions toward the same evasions. Banks were required to hold large proportions of deposits in statutory liquidity and cash reserves, yet the bond market offered artificial prices and poor liquidity. Portfolio Management Services, ready-forward trades, BRs, and broker routing let institutions chase returns while keeping losses out of view. Public-sector companies and mutual funds supplied money, banks supplied balance sheets, and brokers supplied speed and concealment. The “creed” is a shared belief that the transaction can be justified after the fact if it produces a yield, meets a target, or protects the institution's reputation.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Regulatory arbitrage:** a rule creates an economic burden, and participants shift the activity into a less supervised form.
- **Paper substitution:** BRs, allotment letters, and book entries stand in for delivery.
- **Yield camouflage:** a transaction's apparent return hides its effective price, financing cost, or loss transfer.
- **Accommodation ecosystem:** each participant's exception makes the next participant's exception seem normal.

## Key Concepts

### Forced portfolios and artificial prices

Until the 1992–93 Budget, banks had to place 38.5% of deposits in SLR assets and 25% in CRR, leaving only 36.5% freely deployable. The government-securities market was large but not an open, liquid market; the RBI was not a dependable buyer, and banks could value securities at cost rather than current value. A bank could therefore sell, switch, or route securities to avoid recognizing a loss. The authors call this a form of regulatory arbitrage: institutions were formally compliant while economically transferring risk.

### PMS, brokers, and the paper chain

PMS rules required a one-year term, market-linked rates, no guaranteed return, and client risk. Banks nevertheless used “assured,” “benchmark,” or “indicative” returns and sometimes re-described the service as cash management. About Rs 5,000 crore was said to have been churned in PMS during 1990. Brokers were used in call-money transactions even when prohibited, and BRs were issued where SGLs should have been delivered; a BR against another BR could make the paper chain longer while the underlying asset became less visible.

### Yield step-ups and hidden transfers

The “rape deal” example shows why nominal coupon and effective yield must be separated. An old lower-yield bond could be sold at a price that looked acceptable but produced a very high yield to the buyer or a compensating loss to the seller. SBI used such transactions and retaliatory call-money pressure against public-sector banks. The PFC example describes 17% bonds whose money was effectively returned to Citi or Canfina at 14.25%, while Canfina's money moved through Hiten Dalal and a bond deal left Canfina with less value than the face amount. The GIC Rise transactions similarly produced about Rs 89 crore for C Mackertich and Stewart at PMS clients' expense.

### Liberalization shock

Interest-rate deregulation in June–July 1991 devalued older bonds and created a notional loss of about Rs 6,000 crore. Coupon increases from 11.5% to 12% and then 12.5% made the old paper harder to hide. Institutions responded by using PMS, equity-market gains, and compensatory arrangements to mask losses rather than restructuring the underlying risk. The market boom became a second camouflage: a rising share price could make a weak treasury position appear healthy.

## Mental Models

For any “safe” institutional return, calculate the effective yield and ask where the counterparty's money came from. Then trace whether the client, bank, broker, or public issuer bears the downside. A transaction is not made safe by being called PMS, cash management, arbitrage, or a ready forward; the economic substance is the test.

## Anti-patterns

- Confusing a statutory label with controlled risk.
- Valuing a security at cost after its market economics have changed.
- Treating missing delivery as an administrative delay when the document is financing a third party.

## Worked Example

In the chapter's accommodation chain, Grindlays or SBI could issue a cheque to Mehta, UCO could discount accommodation bills worth about Rs 50.40 crore, and NHB could make call loans of roughly Rs 40 crore to UCO and Rs 17 crore to SBI that were credited to Mehta. Each transaction could be narrated as a bank operation; together they formed a funding pipe to a broker. The practical lesson is to consolidate the “temporary” transactions before judging risk.

## Key Takeaways

1. Formal compliance can coexist with economic fraud or concealed loss.
2. The core controls are delivery, valuation, exposure limits, and independent reconciliation.
3. Rising markets hide broken systems until liquidity reverses.
