# Chapter 23: Epilogue

## Core Idea

The epilogue updates the story after Scam 2001 and concludes that the two scandals remained unresolved despite arrests, reports, bans, and institutional changes. Harshad Mehta died in custody, several investigators and witnesses died or moved on, and the continuity needed for complex financial cases disappeared. Global Trust Bank's own inspection warnings were diluted, its links to Ketan Parekh and large corporate borrowers were tolerated, and it was eventually placed under moratorium and merged with Oriental Bank of Commerce. The second JPC again failed to establish the broker–bank–corporate nexus; DSQ's promoter manipulated capital and assets while regulators acted late; and media relationships shaped both promotion and attack. The book ends with a three-part diagnosis: regulators did not learn, recovery was not organized, and public narratives often protected the connected.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Institutional recurrence:** an old failure returns when warnings, incentives, and personnel are not changed.
- **Regulatory capture by reassurance:** a regulator's public confidence can prolong a weak institution's life.
- **Recovery vacuum:** a scandal without a custodian, frozen accounts, or a money map leaves beneficiaries untouched.
- **Media–finance nexus:** ownership, funding, research, and editorial influence can reinforce a market story.

## Key Concepts

### For whom death tolls

Harshad died of a heart attack in Thane Central Jail on December 31, 2001, while facing a case involving 27 lakh shares. The chapter lists the deaths of Pherwani, Goiporia, Khemani, Kamat, Dharmapal, Gandhi, and others, as well as Nirmal Bang's fatal car accident and suicides among brokers. Personnel turnover in the CBI removed institutional memory. Death is not treated as proof of wrongdoing; it is evidence that time can destroy the witness and continuity needed to resolve a complex case.

### Another bank collapse

RBI's Department of Supervision had flagged GTB's irregularities in 1999–2000, including suspected money laundering, write-offs such as the Rs 82 crore PEPCO case, large Balaji and Bharat Shah exposures, and a preferential allotment involving Nishkalp, Nakshatra, and Chitrakut. The report was diluted and released late. GTB accounts formed a hub for Parekh's network, and the bank's lending to Zee and HFCL was routed to him. Ramesh Gelli was asked to step down in 2001 but allegedly continued to guide the bank through relatives; RBI supported restructuring and a foreign investor before the July 2004 moratorium and forced merger.

### Unaccountable regulators

RBI said price manipulation was outside its purview even when the manipulated GTB price affected the proposed UTI Bank merger. It issued a reassuring 2003 statement about GTB's “cleaned-up” balance sheet despite losses and provisioning, while independent directors resigned over loan-loss provisions. It also tolerated Rajendra Banthia's influence at Nedungadi until an inspection found Rs 1,352 crore of “arbitrage” trades and a Rs 95 crore loss. The Department of Company Affairs responded to abnormal price movement with hesitation, lacked staff, and rarely used its prosecution powers.

### The New Old JPC

The 30-member JPC was created through political bargaining after the Tehelka defence sting and was chaired by a retired lieutenant general with little market expertise. It took extensions, blamed SEBI for failures, focused on Ketan Parekh, and did not seriously investigate GTB, UTI, or the corporate entities funding the positions. It could not even identify which regulator was responsible for OCBs. One member, Nilotpal Basu, acknowledged that the committee failed to establish the broker–bank–corporate nexus.

### The DSQ Saga

Dinesh Dalmia's DSQ issued 14 million shares to Mauritius companies without proper disclosure, used shares in the CSE crisis, demerged and sold foreign assets for about Rs 145 crore while leaving a shell listed, and repeated similar maneuvers at DSQ Biotech. In the latter case, 8.03 million preferential shares were issued to four OCBs at Rs 275 while the market price was above Rs 800; only about 30% of the money was received, and two OCBs were linked to Dalmia. Funds were circulated through 13 Kolkata bank accounts to create the appearance of payment. SEBI eventually barred Dalmia and DSQ for ten years and penalized independent directors, but other alleged corporate-law, forex, asset-sale, and manipulation issues remained unresolved.

### Where is the Money?

Scam 2001 lacked the organized freezing and recovery process used in 1992. T B Ruia admitted liabilities to the Custodian but retained assets; Parekh promised staggered repayments of Rs 882 crore to MMCB and did not make them, while Bank of India's Rs 130 crore exposure was provided for and its negotiating chairman retired. Mukesh Babu admitted or was reported as liable for Rs 225.63 crore to MMCB yet continued institutional business because references to him were diluted or dropped. The episode shows that recovery fails when regulators do not preserve a consistent liability ledger.

### Media manipulation

Media companies, editors, and journalists were financially or socially connected to Parekh, HFCL, and First Global. First Global's 1999–2000 GTB inspection trail included Rs 354 crore released through oral sanctions to investment companies and a Rs 44.5 crore overdraft for HFCL equity; the firm also bought shares at Rs 1,050 and placed them with foreign institutions at a premium. Despite those facts, commentators framed action against First Global as persecution for financing Tehelka. A 2003 First Global report still called collapsed HFCL a strong buy. The chapter treats this as evidence that research branding and media independence require financial disclosure.

## Mental Models

Use a **post-scandal closure checklist**: warnings corrected, risky persons excluded, assets frozen, liability ledger reconciled, recovery distributed, senior decisions reviewed, and public report published. If one item is missing, the scandal may be administratively over but economically open. Also compare regulator statements with balance-sheet evidence before accepting reassurance.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a moratorium or merger as resolution.
- Assuming a JPC report's named culprit explains the whole network.
- Allowing reputational sympathy or media access to substitute for transaction evidence.

## Worked Example

GTB is the epilogue's synthesis case. RBI had inspection information about bad loans, related-party exposures, Parekh accounts, and manipulated share prices, yet softened the report, endorsed the bank, and supported restructuring. When GTB failed, the merger transferred the institution's unresolved questions into a new entity. The sequence joins warning, regulatory reassurance, delayed failure, and incomplete recovery in one timeline.

## Key Takeaways

1. Scandals recur when knowledge is not converted into institutional change.
2. Recovery needs a live, public, transaction-level ledger.
3. Media, regulators, and financial institutions can reinforce the same story unless conflicts are disclosed.
