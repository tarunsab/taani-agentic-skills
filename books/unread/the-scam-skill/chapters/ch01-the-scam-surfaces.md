# Chapter 1: The Scam Surfaces

## Core Idea

The scandal first became legible through reconciliation, not through a dramatic confession. SBI compared its internal books with the Public Debt Office ledger and found that its holdings of 11.5% Central Government Loan 2010 did not match: the PDO showed about Rs 1,170.95 crore while SBI's books showed about Rs 1,744.95 crore, a gap that later widened as more transactions were examined. Junior officer R Sitaraman had been supplying Harshad Mehta with Bank Receipts (BRs) in place of the Securities General Ledger (SGL) entries SBI expected, allowing money to leave the bank while the securities remained unverified. SBI initially tried to recover cash commercially before escalating, but the subsequent investigative process shifted attention toward SBI and away from the wider network. The chapter's governing lesson is that a balance-sheet mismatch becomes a systemic scandal when institutions prioritize settlement, reputation, or jurisdiction over preserving the complete trail.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Reconciliation as detection:** compare the custodian's record, the bank's books, the document presented, and the actual counterparty.
- **Commercial recovery versus criminal attribution:** an institution may try to get money back before naming wrongdoing, creating later evidentiary complications.
- **Substitute-document risk:** a BR can conceal the absence of an SGL or underlying security when controls are weak.

## Key Concepts

### The SBI gap

SBI's officers—DMD C Khemani, general manager Subba Rao, and DGM R L Kamat—followed entries through the securities department and found that money from ready-forward transactions had gone to Mehta rather than to the banks that were supposed to receive it. Mehta's position was effectively financed by SBI's cheques while the bank held promises and BRs. The authors describe the immediate exposure as roughly Rs 499 crore plus another Rs 170 crore, with the overall hole later discussed as about Rs 575–660 crore. The precise amount changed as reconciliation continued; that changing number is itself a reminder to distinguish a first discrepancy from the final loss estimate.

### From repayment to public exposure

Mehta paid SBI through ANZ Grindlays cheques, including amounts of Rs 243.18 crore, Rs 142 crore, and Rs 160 crore, leaving only a small balance in one account. A *Times of India* report on April 23, 1992 made the repayment public and raised the question of where the money had come from. The *Indian Express* then connected the funds to the National Housing Bank (NHB), whose chairman M J Pherwani was drawn into the story. A separate reconciliation exposed Standard Chartered's large Bank of Karad and Metropolitan Cooperative Bank BR positions, while Canfina held additional Karad documents.

## Mental Models

Use a four-column ledger when studying a financial scandal: **claimed asset**, **document**, **custodian record**, and **cash beneficiary**. Any row in which the document exists but the custodian record or underlying asset does not is a control failure, not merely an accounting oddity. Then add a fifth column—**who chose the next action**—because the choice to recover, conceal, refer, or accuse shapes the later investigation.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating the first number reported in a crisis as the final loss.
- Assuming repayment proves a transaction was legitimate or identifies every beneficiary.
- Blaming the institution that discovered the gap while ignoring the document and funding network.

## Worked Example

The Palani episode is the chapter's practical model. Kamat tracks Sitaraman down during a family ceremony, returns him to the branch, and asks why SBI's ledger and the PDO do not agree. Sitaraman says Mehta has BRs rather than SGLs; the bank then reconstructs which counterparties should have received securities and realizes that Mehta received the liquidity. The sequence shows how a mundane control—matching settlement records—can uncover a large funding channel.

## Key Takeaways

1. Start with independent records, not the dominant narrative.
2. Follow both the missing security and the cash that replaced it.
3. Preserve the distinction between a discovered mismatch, an estimated loss, and a legally established offence.
