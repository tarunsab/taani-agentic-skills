# Chapter 7: Harshad in the Net

## Core Idea

Harshad Mehta was not caught by one heroic regulator; he was enclosed slowly by partial actions from the Income Tax department, RBI, SBI, and the press. RBI had warnings about BR misuse and small-bank irregularities as early as 1986 and 1991, but inspections were delayed, qualified, or treated as information rather than triggers. Mehta's Delhi outreach, including a presentation to V Krishnamurthy and an unsecured payment, shows how he sought political and institutional legitimacy while his funding structure became fragile. The February 1992 tax raid and RBI monitoring of his SBI account preceded the decisive SBI reconciliation, but the response still focused on managing a crisis rather than preserving a clean cross-institution trail. The chapter's lesson is that early signals matter only when an institution is willing to connect them and act.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Distributed detection:** separate agencies each see one part of the exposure.
- **Warning without escalation:** an inspection can identify a violation yet fail to change behavior.
- **Legitimacy-seeking:** a powerful operator courts officials and institutions to turn influence into credibility.

## Key Concepts

### RBI's missed warnings

RBI had a tip in March 1991 about BR misuse and ordered inspections at five banks, but the instructions oddly softened the treatment of foreign banks. Earlier reports had already named Bank of Karad, Vijaya, Syndicate, Andhra, and Citibank. Inspectors found artificial rates and BR violations; banks later self-certified compliance, and Standard Chartered's certification was false according to the authors. A 1991 circular addressed BRs, but the chapter argues that a circular without verification was not supervision.

### Delhi, tax, and political proximity

Mehta sought an introduction to V Krishnamurthy and presented his capital-market and disinvestment ideas in February 1992. The authors report that he gave Krishnamurthy Rs 32.74 lakh, or possibly $100,000, without collateral. RBI's governor later investigated Andhra, Karad, and Madura but accepted advice against extreme action. The tax raid on February 28 and the subsequent monitoring of Mehta's SBI account found transfers and shrinking balances; by March 14 the account was nearly empty. These episodes show how access and liquidity were moving together.

### The final net

When SBI discovered its gap, Mehta needed to raise cash while preserving his market position. He paid about Rs 575 crore via Grindlays, and the money led back to NHB. RBI ordered repayment and NHB tried to roll over its claims; Mehta also explored HUDCO and other sources. Meanwhile, Citibank offered a haircut and Standard Chartered's exposure grew. The crisis was therefore a race between reconciliation and the next refinancing event.

## Mental Models

Build a **warning ledger** for every red flag: date, agency, subject, action available, action taken, and what later event it could have prevented. This separates “the regulator knew” from the more precise question “what did it know, when, and what did it do with the knowledge?”

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a warning circular as proof of effective control.
- Letting institutional prestige replace collateral and documentation.
- Investigating accounts one agency at a time when the risk is a network.

## Worked Example

The sequence from RBI's March 1991 tip to the March 1992 SBI discovery is the chapter's worked case. The same kinds of BR and small-bank concerns appeared in inspections, but they were not consolidated with Mehta's growing market position. Once SBI matched its records to the PDO and saw the cash beneficiary, the earlier warnings became explanatory evidence rather than preventive control.

## Key Takeaways

1. The net existed before the public scandal, but its strands were not joined.
2. Institutional access can delay scrutiny while increasing a broker's capacity.
3. The most valuable regulatory action is often early, boring, and cross-institutional.
