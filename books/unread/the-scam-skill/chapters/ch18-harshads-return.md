# Chapter 18: Harshad's Return

## Core Idea

Harshad Mehta's return shows how a public scandal can become a new source of media power when legal status, regulatory memory, and investor appetite are treated as separate questions. In June 1993 he accused Prime Minister P V Narasimha Rao of accepting a Rs 1 crore payment, while presenting a detailed account of suitcases, political intermediaries, and alleged leniency at the BSE. The accusation generated publicity but no decisive resolution; the chapter presents the Mehtas' account as contested and notes contradictions and denials. After lying low, Harshad attempted comebacks through Jindal shares, a Rediff chat, newspaper columns, a website, and price operations in BPL, Videocon, Sterlite, and related stocks. Without the bank funding of 1992, the 1998 operation collapsed after the nuclear-test shock and a BSE payment crisis, while SEBI acted slowly.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Narrative as operating capital:** public attention can attract speculative demand even after a collapse.
- **Regulatory memory test:** a conviction or ban matters only if market access and front networks are actually closed.
- **Leveraged comeback:** the old strategy fails faster when the funding source is weaker.

## Key Concepts

### The political allegation

Harshad and Ashwin said they carried Rs 67 lakh to the Prime Minister's residence in November 1991 and sent another Rs 33 lakh the next day, describing it as a donation for an election. The PMO denied the charge, and the authors note disputes over the visit, suitcase capacity, tape evidence, and the timing. Harshad said he spoke because he feared COFEPOSA detention and political persecution; the authors also describe alleged feelers for a settlement and pressure around narcotics accusations. The chapter does not resolve the allegation; its study value lies in how a legal and political conflict became public leverage.

### Same actor, same role

In December 1996 and March 1997, Harshad tested a comeback through Jindal shares, public predictions that the Sensex would reach 7,000, newspaper columns, and a website. He positioned himself as a champion of small investors, but the operational technique remained hype around selected stocks. BPL rose from about Rs 40 to Rs 400, and SEBI later attributed the manipulation to seven front companies and eighteen brokers; those entities held about 70% of BPL's traded stock and 50% of deliverable trade in Videocon and Sterlite. The concentration indicates that the “small investor” constituency was more audience than financing base.

### No longer just an accused

The 1998 failure came when the nuclear-test shock prompted foreign selling and the bank-funded support that had sustained 1992 no longer existed. Brokers failed to pay, BSE concealed or managed the crisis, and SEBI's investigation took until April 2001 despite evidence of manipulation. The September 1999 conviction in the Rs 38 crore Maruti Udyog case removed the media's “merely accused” excuse, but the larger pattern of access and repeated operators remained. The chapter ends with Harshad still projecting optimism after conviction, illustrating how identity can outlive capital.

## Mental Models

Use a **comeback test**: what is the new source of cash, what legal access remains, who are the front entities, and what changed in the exit mechanism? If only the media channel changed, it is probably a reputational relaunch rather than a new investment process.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a public prediction as research or a press column as a fiduciary service.
- Assuming “not convicted” means fit to influence investors.
- Ignoring front companies and payment failures because the operator appears personally short of cash.

## Worked Example

The BPL operation is the worked case. Media hype and promoter coordination lifted the stock, but the majority of inventory stayed within Harshad's broker cartel. When foreign selling removed the outside demand, the cartel lacked the funds to settle. The difference between 1992 and 1998 was not the operator's story; it was the depth of the funding and exit network.

## Key Takeaways

1. Public attention can relaunch a manipulation strategy without repairing its economics.
2. A conviction changes the legitimacy question, but enforcement must also close the operating network.
3. Payment failure is often the first visible proof that the “mass investor” story was hollow.
