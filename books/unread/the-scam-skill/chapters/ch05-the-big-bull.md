# Chapter 5: The Big Bull

## Core Idea

Harshad Mehta's “Big Bull” identity combined a real financing innovation with a public mythology that made his positions easier to carry. He entered the money market in 1987 because equity positions needed larger and more reliable funding, then built alliances with Bank of America, ANZ Grindlays, and selected brokers to challenge the Citibank-centered cartel. He redirected money-market liquidity into equities, used promoter relationships and institutional prestige to make concentrated purchases look like a national investment story, and promoted theories such as replacement cost and price-driven demand. His public visibility created confidence, but it also increased the scale of his obligations and made retreat psychologically and politically harder. The chapter's warning is that a market leader can become trapped by the identity that helped him lead it.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Liquidity capture:** secure the funding channel first, then use it to shape the asset market.
- **Narrative leverage:** publicity and theory recruit other buyers and normalize extreme prices.
- **Promoter–operator alliance:** buy or finance a block, lift the market, and expect the promoter or institutions to support the next stage.
- **Respectability as collateral:** prestigious relationships substitute for transparent balance-sheet strength.

## Key Concepts

### Breaking the cartel

Mehta's entry challenged a network associated with Citibank, Ajay Kayan, Bhupen Dalal, Jitu Shroff, Hemendra Kothari, and Canfina. BankAm's Neeraj Batra and Grindlays' Aggarwala became important allies because they were willing to accept small losses to demonstrate that Mehta could transact at scale. Hotlines and fast execution made the alliance operational. The point was not simply to find a better stock; it was to change who controlled access to bank money and market liquidity.

### The equity myth

Money-market funds flowed toward equities, and the public saw the visible results: ACC's price rose from about Rs 300 to Rs 10,000, while Mehta's lifestyle and Mazda became symbols of the boom. His theories argued that historical P/E ratios were less important than replacement cost, future opportunity, and the belief that demand could rise with price. These ideas could be useful questions about valuation, but in the chapter they also function as rationalizations for buying into a self-reinforcing price movement.

### Promoters, institutions, and publicity

Mehta bought blocks in companies such as Apollo and Gujarat Ambuja and worked with promoters who could supply stock or public credibility. He sought institutional and political recognition through people such as V Krishnamurthy, N D Prabhu, and Varshneya, and cultivated newspaper coverage, the “highest taxpayer” image, and a public dispute with Merrill Lynch. The respectability campaign helped him appear as a market-builder rather than a leveraged principal. It also made the gap between image and funding more dangerous.

### The exit problem

The bull run required a buyer large enough to absorb positions: foreign funds, new retail distribution, or public issues were supposed to provide that exit. Mehta continued borrowing and fighting for supremacy instead of reducing exposure when the funding loop became fragile. The chapter presents this as an addiction to intuition and hype: the same confidence that created the market constituency prevented a quiet retreat.

## Mental Models

Separate a **price thesis** from a **funding thesis**. Ask what makes an asset worth more, then independently ask who supplies the cash during the wait and who takes the inventory at the end. A persuasive story about replacement cost cannot repair a funding channel that depends on undocumented bank credit.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating public recognition as independent validation.
- Confusing an operator's ability to move a market with the ability to create durable value.
- Assuming a promised institutional exit exists because institutions appear in the story.

## Worked Example

The ACC ascent shows the complete loop: bank-linked liquidity enabled concentrated buying; a replacement-cost story justified a much higher price; public attention and institutional interest supplied more demand; and the rising price became evidence for the story. If the original funding is withdrawn, the loop reverses. That is why the chapter's ACC anecdote is less an investment tip than a model of reflexive leverage.

## Key Takeaways

1. The Big Bull was built from liquidity, alliances, narrative, and scale.
2. A promoter deal can produce a tradable story without producing durable cash flow.
3. The unexamined exit is the hidden liability of a successful operator.
