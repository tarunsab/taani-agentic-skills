# Chapter 16: The Witch-hunt

## Core Idea

The investigation became a multi-agency spectacle without a shared plan for reconstructing transactions, recovering money, or distinguishing senior responsibility from technical negligence. CBI pursued criminal charges, Income Tax pursued concealment, RBI documented bank violations, and the JPC sought a political explanation; they often withheld information from one another. The authors argue that CBI relied on drama, inflated aggregate turnover, raids, custody, and planted stories instead of following the money through each broker and institution. Junior officers were harassed and ruined while obvious trails involving politicians, industrialists, foreign banks, and public-sector institutions were dropped or softened. The chapter's distinction is between visible investigative activity and useful evidence management.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Agency fragmentation:** multiple mandates can create gaps rather than coverage.
- **Aggregate-volume fallacy:** large turnover, especially double ready-forwards, is not the same as loss or beneficiary exposure.
- **Theater versus inquiry:** raids, arrests, and press briefings can substitute for a tested transaction map.

## Key Concepts

### CBI's numbers and targets

The CBI investigation used figures such as Rs 17,573 crore of overall activity, while a per-broker comparison was about Rs 1,579 crore. The authors say double ready-forward transactions inflated volume and obscured where money was actually lost. Rather than begin with the largest economic beneficiaries, investigators concentrated on easily accessible officers such as Khemani, Subba Rao, and Kamat. The resulting case looked large while its evidentiary structure remained thin.

### Custody as a method

The chapter records raids, locked jewellery, allegations of torture, and repeated FIRs that kept Harshad in custody. CBI circulated an inaccurate claim that it had frozen Swiss bank accounts and suggested narcotics links; the authors say the Indian Express checked and found the claims false. K Madhavan's suggestion to investigate foreign accounts was ignored, and his explanation for resigning was not circulated to the JPC. Custody and publicity became tools of pressure rather than a disciplined way to preserve testimony.

### JPC's blind spots

The JPC had data errors, fragmented broker names, omitted PMS, and little direct engagement with market participants. Members were unfamiliar with basic market terms and debated political questioning instead of asking where the money went. The investigation did not systematically connect Citibank, brokers, public-sector funding, or corporate beneficiaries. The authors argue that its procedure produced a report that could malign selected individuals without explaining the system.

## Mental Models

Create a **case matrix** with one row per transaction and separate columns for criminal act, tax issue, regulatory breach, civil loss, and recovery target. Do not let one agency's conclusion stand in for another's mandate. The matrix makes it harder to confuse a witness, a document custodian, a beneficiary, and a legally responsible party.

## Anti-patterns

- Measuring an investigation by number of raids or charge-sheets.
- Conflating turnover with loss and association with guilt.
- Keeping agencies, committees, and the public on different versions of the evidence.

## Worked Example

The CBI's focus on aggregate turnover is the worked case. A ready-forward can be recorded on both legs, so summing every leg makes the market appear larger without showing who received cash or failed to deliver securities. Reconstructing one broker's money and delivery path would have been less dramatic but far more useful for recovery and prosecution.

## Key Takeaways

1. Investigative activity is not the same as explanatory evidence.
2. Political sensitivity and institutional reputation can determine which trails survive.
3. Financial cases require shared transaction data and role-specific accountability.
