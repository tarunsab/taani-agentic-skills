# Chapter 8: Wielding the Crowbar

## Core Idea

The chapter portrays RBI's authority as a crowbar used to force SBI and ANZ Grindlays to settle National Housing Bank's claims, even though NHB's own records and governance were deeply compromised. NHB's letters changed the amounts it claimed from Grindlays and SBI, often without clear supporting documents, while RBI repeatedly sided with the subsidiary. NHB had conducted a huge volume of transactions with inadequate controls: its dealer could sign cheques, fictitious purchases and sales appeared in its books, and call loans were disguised as securities activity. The central governance problem is selective enforcement: the regulator treated the discovery bank as responsible for an overdraft while failing to pursue the institution whose liquidity had enabled it. The chapter also flags conflicts of interest in the overlapping roles of RBI, SBI, and inquiry officials.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Regulatory leverage:** formal power can compel a settlement even when the regulator's own institution is implicated.
- **Documentation asymmetry:** a changing claim may still prevail if one party controls the supervisory channel.
- **Conflict-of-interest map:** board memberships and investigative roles can blur who is reviewing whom.

## Key Concepts

### NHB's shifting claims

Between May 5 and June 3, 1992, NHB wrote to Grindlays with claims that moved through roughly Rs 500 crore and later figures; its claim against SBI similarly changed from about Rs 604.61 crore to Rs 734.73 crore and then to Rs 707.76 crore. The chapter does not treat every change as proof of fraud, but asks why the documents and calculations were not settled before coercive action. RBI pressured SBI and Grindlays to return funds while NHB's evidence remained opaque.

### The institution behind the claim

Under part-time chairman M J Pherwani, NHB had no effective board for much of the relevant period and reportedly executed 1,332 deals worth around Rs 26,400 crore. Ravi Kumar was both dealer and cheque signatory; Parthasarathy, another key official, disappeared. NHB lent money through banks in ways that looked like call loans to Mehta, and the authors say its accounting of purchase and sale transactions was fictitious. Pherwani's previous UTI role and political appointment amplify the chapter's question about whether institutional reputation protected reckless decisions.

### SBI under pressure

SBI's board was forced to deal with NHB's claim while its own officers were exposed to criminal scrutiny. At a June 11 meeting, government directors who also sat on the first NHB board left to consult the Finance Minister and RBI governor, then returned with a threat of direction. SBI eventually debited Mehta with an unsecured Rs 707 crore overdraft, while records omitted important parts of the confrontation. The chapter uses this as evidence that “commercial recovery” became a liability transfer rather than a transparent finding of responsibility.

## Mental Models

When a regulator orders a settlement, separate three questions: **is the claim calculated?**, **is the claimant controlled?**, and **who benefits from immediate compliance?** If the answer to the second question is unclear, the order may solve a liquidity problem while damaging evidence and fairness.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming the supervised institution's claim is reliable because RBI supports it.
- Treating a board position as ceremonial when it affects access to information.
- Booking an overdraft against a broker instead of identifying the actual source and use of funds.

## Worked Example

The NHB–SBI dispute is the practical case. SBI found a hole and initially pursued Mehta; NHB then asserted its own changing claim, and RBI used supervisory pressure to make SBI absorb it. A complete reconstruction would require NHB's books, the bank statements, the BR/SGL trail, and the decision records of RBI and SBI. The chapter shows what is lost when institutional power decides the accounting before the evidence is reconciled.

## Key Takeaways

1. Regulatory force is not the same as factual clarity.
2. A weak claimant can still dominate a dispute if it has supervisory backing.
3. Overlapping governance and investigation roles must be disclosed and managed.
