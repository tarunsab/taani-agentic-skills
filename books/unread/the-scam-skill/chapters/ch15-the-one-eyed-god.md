# Chapter 15: The One-eyed God

## Core Idea

RBI is the book's “one-eyed god”: powerful enough to license and supervise banks, yet institutionally separated from the market intelligence needed to see a networked scam. RBI inspectors had identified false BRs, name-lending, SGL problems, balance-sheet dressing, and PMS practices as early as the 1986 Kurias report. Senior officials debated the implications, issued vague or weak circulars, and repeatedly chose not to enforce their own findings. The chapter treats the failure as cultural and structural: an ivory-tower supervisor, limited access to brokers, fragmented records, and a preference for policy promotion over uncomfortable intervention. Its practical lesson is that supervision is a continuous information system, not a periodic paperwork exercise.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Supervision gap:** formal inspection knowledge does not become control unless it triggers action.
- **Market-intelligence deficit:** regulators who do not speak to traders or join records see only institutional fragments.
- **Conflict between promotion and supervision:** a regulator can encourage market growth while avoiding the restrictions needed to keep it safe.

## Key Concepts

### The Kurias warning

The 1986 RBI report described Allahabad and Karad using false BRs, Andhra and VBD lending their names, SGLs and BRs issued without balances, and Syndicate and Andhra dressing their balance sheets. It also warned that public-sector money managed through PMS could amount to deposit-taking. The report's underlying point was explicit: good intentions do not legalize violations. Yet the warnings did not become a sustained enforcement program.

### Rules without verification

In the 1987 PMS debate, Augustine Kurias's concerns competed with a more pragmatic view from KK Mukherjee and others. RBI issued rules—no guaranteed returns, a one-year term, market rates, and documentation—but banks invented “cash management” language and continued the same economics. RBI treated brokers as a necessary evil and did not enroll or monitor them effectively. A regulator cannot supervise a transaction chain if it refuses to supervise the intermediary who joins the chain.

### The 1991–92 delay

RBI received further reports and tips, including warnings about Karad, Andhra, and foreign-bank practices. S Venkitaramanan, governor from 1990, asked for action but accepted advice against a drastic response and delayed. Inspectors could not inspect brokers' books, and bank audits did not connect the securities and cash sides of the market. The chapter argues that the eventual response looked active only because the market had already broken.

## Mental Models

Measure supervision by a **signal-to-action chain**: signal captured → independent corroboration → responsible owner → deadline → intervention → post-intervention verification. A long, vague circular is not a control if the chain stops before verification. Also ask what information the supervisor is structurally unable or unwilling to see.

## Anti-patterns

- Issuing rules without testing them in live transactions.
- Assuming banks' self-certification proves compliance.
- Treating brokers and public-sector affiliates as outside the supervisory perimeter.

## Worked Example

The Kurias report is the worked case. It described the same false-document and PMS mechanisms later found in the scam, yet the system continued until SBI's reconciliation forced attention. Comparing the report's warnings with the later events identifies not an absence of information but a failure to convert information into an accountable intervention.

## Key Takeaways

1. RBI's failure was one of continuity, perimeter, and action—not merely lack of rules.
2. Supervision must combine books, market intelligence, and counterparty behavior.
3. A regulator's silence protects the system only until liquidity reverses.
