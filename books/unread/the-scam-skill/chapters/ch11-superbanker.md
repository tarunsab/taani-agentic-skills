# Chapter 11: Superbanker

## Core Idea

The “Superbanker” chapter profiles B Ratnakar, the ambitious former Canara Bank chairman whose leadership style made growth, relationships, and rule-bending appear compatible with public banking. He rose from poor beginnings through Canara Bank, expanded branches and deposits, and cultivated business and political contacts while celebrating contribution over hierarchy. His institutions—Canfina, CanMutual, and related ventures—became powerful conduits for money-market and equity activity. Ratnakar is shown as energetic and often helpful, rescuing payrolls or accommodating companies, but his appetite for speed and personal trust weakened the controls his protégés later inherited. His death in February 1992 removed the central personality holding the network together and left institutions with the same ambition but less judgment and accountability.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Founder-style banking:** a charismatic leader substitutes personal judgment for institutional process.
- **Relationship capital:** the ability to help clients creates access, loyalty, and future obligations.
- **Clone risk:** protégés inherit risk tolerance without inheriting the founder's tacit knowledge—or his limits.

## Key Concepts

### Growth as proof of virtue

Ratnakar's rise from a small-town and branch-banking background to one of the youngest Canara Bank chairmen is presented as evidence of drive, not necessarily of control. He pushed branch and deposit growth and said targets should not block creativity. His management language emphasized challenge, recognition, loyalty, and equality of contribution. The same culture encouraged staff to solve problems quickly, sometimes by bypassing rules.

### Accommodation and connections

The chapter gives examples of Ratnakar helping BHEL with payroll, Maruti, and friends in industry, and of his relationship with V Krishnamurthy. Canara Bank's ecosystem moved PMS funds into Canfina's CIAS, eventually reaching around Rs 4,000 crore. A Reliance G-series problem was treated as something the institution could absorb. The authors do not present every accommodation as criminal; they show how repeated favors made a safety net for connected borrowers and normalized exceptions.

### The institutional legacy

Canfina, CanMutual, and the financial-services ventures grew around trusted lieutenants and a willingness to exploit market opportunities. A CBI house case against Ratnakar did not result in a charge-sheet, adding to the impression that senior relationship-builders could survive scrutiny. After his death, Fairgrowth later collapsed and the chapter argues that many protégés were “cowboy bankers” who copied the style without the stabilizing authority of the founder.

## Mental Models

Separate **relationship capacity** from **control capacity**. A leader may be able to mobilize money and solve a client's immediate problem while the institution is incapable of tracking the accumulated contingent risk. A useful board question is: “Which exceptions depend on one person being right?”

## Anti-patterns

- Treating rapid growth and social influence as evidence of sound risk management.
- Calling repeated accommodations customer service without measuring concentration.
- Relying on a founder's reputation to govern successor institutions.

## Worked Example

Canara Bank's PMS-to-Canfina pipeline is the chapter's example. A service meant to manage client money was shifted into a specialized vehicle that could operate at much larger scale, while the bank's relationship network reassured investors. When the same network supported aggressive brokers and risky companies, the institutional boundary no longer protected the client.

## Key Takeaways

1. Personal leadership can accelerate both institution-building and risk accumulation.
2. Financial conglomerates inherit the incentives of their parent bank unless controls are explicit.
3. Succession is a risk event when the system depends on a single broker-like banker.
