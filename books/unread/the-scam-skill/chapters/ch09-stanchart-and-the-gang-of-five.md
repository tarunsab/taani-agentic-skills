# Chapter 9: Stanchart and the Gang of Five

## Core Idea

Standard Chartered's crisis was a parallel stream of the 1992 scandal, not merely another account in Harshad Mehta's story. Its executives discovered that Hiten Dalal's exposure had expanded from roughly Rs 300 crore to Rs 900 crore, supported by false or unacceptable Bank Receipts from Metropolitan Cooperative Bank and Bank of Karad. The bank had grown aggressively, rewarded treasury targets, and tolerated weak documentation, concentration, and exposure controls. Around the bank stood a “Gang of Five”—Hiten Dalal, Abhay Narottam, J P Gandhi, Teju Ruia, and Bhupen Dalal—linked through family, agency, bank directorships, and business relationships. The authors distinguish direct proof of a transaction from the broader influence network that made the transactions possible.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Fast-growth control debt:** expansion outpaces risk systems and makes exceptions routine.
- **Networked responsibility:** an institution can be exposed through a set of linked actors rather than a single borrower.
- **Influence versus evidence:** strategic relationships may explain access without proving every alleged benefit.

## Key Concepts

### The discovery at Stanchart

On April 30, 1992, executives found an apparent Rs 300 crore gap in Hiten Dalal's account; within days the exposure was understood as much larger. Metro Bank BRs of about Rs 525 crore and an SGL of Rs 50 crore were questionable, and Karad paper added another layer. The bank had accepted photocopied bonds, ignored the fact that Metro's capital was only about Rs 14 lakh, and allowed cash-neutral forwards to disguise the asset position. Internal audit appeared clean because the activity was designed to settle on paper.

### The bank's culture

After 1988 Stanchart pursued a fast-track strategy, with Pesi Nat and Kannan pushing targets and an attempt to emulate Citibank. The Merchant Banking Division borrowed public-sector money and lent to brokers as apparently clean credit, evading reserve and exposure expectations. Yardi's warnings were not integrated into control; the chapter says he was pushed out. A target-driven culture made a large unexplained position look like a performance achievement until the counterparty failed.

### The Gang of Five

Hiten's family relationship with Narottam, Gandhi's employment and kinship ties, Ruia's business association and Metro directorship, and Bhupen's strategic connections to Karad and Stanchart formed a web. BCD handled a huge volume of securities and a substantial proportion of Karad business, while Bhupen was a non-executive director and influential “godfather.” The CBI treated him as a kingpin, but the chapter notes the absence of direct proof for some of the strongest allegations. That distinction matters: a network map is a guide to inquiry, not a substitute for evidence.

## Mental Models

For a bank exposure, map both the **legal counterparty** and the **relationship counterparty**. Ask who signs the document, who controls the bank, who receives the cash, and who can arrange a replacement document. A low-capital bank or a related director should trigger concentration review even if each trade is formally settled.

## Anti-patterns

- Using clean internal audit reports to override economically implausible exposures.
- Treating a non-executive title as proof of non-involvement.
- Turning “connected to” into “proved to have benefited.”

## Worked Example

Stanchart's acceptance of Metro Bank BRs is the worked example. A bank with only about Rs 14 lakh of capital supplied documents for hundreds of crores, yet fast-track growth and target pressure let the position pass. Once the bank compared the document to the counterparty's capacity and the missing underlying securities, the exposure became visibly implausible. The lesson is to test documentary validity against institutional scale.

## Key Takeaways

1. Stanchart's own growth strategy was part of the risk mechanism.
2. Family, directorship, and business ties help explain access but require transaction-level proof.
3. Audit that checks form without economics can certify a false comfort.
