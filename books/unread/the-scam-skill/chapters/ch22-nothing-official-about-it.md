# Chapter 22: Nothing Official About It

## Core Idea

The chapter argues that Scam 2001 cannot be understood through official exchange records alone because Kolkata's grey market was as economically important as the formal market. Unofficial badla sessions, cash financing, cross-exchange positions, and fake client notes connected CSE to BSE, NSE, brokers, UTI, and corporate operators. CSE accepted volatile shares as collateral, post-dated cheques, and margin arrangements that concealed defaults; BSE brokers diverted client badla money to Kolkata and left investors unsecured. UTI and SEBI then helped bail out CSE and operators by buying DSQ, HFCL, Zee, and Global Tele-related inventory, shifting losses onto unit-holders. The DSQ investigation reveals the corporate side: a suspicious acquisition, duplicate share numbers, and a promoter who could use capital issuance as a rescue instrument.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Official/unofficial continuum:** a grey market is not economically separate when money and positions flow into the exchange.
- **Bailout moral hazard:** a regulator or fund that rescues a failing position may preserve the next manipulation.
- **Corporate-capital manipulation:** preferential allotments and duplicate certificates can manufacture both capital and collateral.

## Key Concepts

### Kolkata's grey market

The authors estimate losses in unofficial K-10 trading at around Rs 2,000 crore, with the top six operators holding positions above Rs 150 crore each. Badla rates of 24–30% in Kolkata drew money that clients believed was invested through official BSE channels at 10–12%. BSE brokers issued fake contract notes or confirmation orders while using the money to meet their own pay-ins. The official and unofficial systems were intertwined because the same operators, cash, shares, and settlement obligations crossed the boundary.

### CSE failure and bailout

CSE initially described a Rs 96 crore payment problem as resolved, then admitted Rs 40 crore remained; later gaps were also disputed. It accepted post-dated cheques and K-10 shares as collateral, failed to deactivate troubled members, and blamed a margin-calculation “bug” that sometimes displayed zero. UTI bought 13.3 lakh DSQ shares at Rs 189 and other K-10 stocks to support settlement 148, even though DSQ later fell sharply and the deal cost unit-holders. The authors compare this to SEBI's earlier punishment of Shriram Mutual Fund for bailing out Harshad-linked brokers and ask why UTI's fiduciary duty should be different.

### DSQ's corporate mystery

Dinesh Dalmia said DSQ had acquired Fortuna Technologies and increased capital from about Rs 30.5 crore to Rs 47 crore. The company allotted 1.4 crore shares at Rs 675 to three Mauritius companies, but the alleged Fortuna owner said those companies neither owned Fortuna nor were owned by it. The distinctive-number band later appeared on physical shares delivered to defaulting Kolkata brokers, suggesting duplicate issuance. The chapter therefore links the corporate action, CSE bailout, promoter speculation, and overseas entities into one question about capital and disclosure.

## Mental Models

Use an **officiality test**: if an “unofficial” position can affect exchange settlement, bank liquidity, or mutual-fund NAV, include it in the system map. For a bailout, compare the price paid, the likely liquidation value, the beneficiary, and the fiduciary duty of the rescuer.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a cash or grey-market trade as harmless because it is outside exchange records.
- Accepting post-dated cheques or volatile collateral as if they were cash.
- Calling a discounted purchase a bailout success before testing the post-bailout price.

## Worked Example

UTI's DSQ purchase is the chapter's worked case. UTI already held large, expensive DSQ positions, then bought 13.3 lakh shares from CSE at Rs 189 to help settle a defaulting broker's position. DSQ fell to roughly a third of that level by July, transferring the cost to US-64 unit-holders. The same act can be described as market stabilization or a fiduciary failure; the deciding test is whose interests and losses it served.

## Key Takeaways

1. “Unofficial” does not mean immaterial.
2. Market bailouts can hide default while extending the manipulation.
3. Corporate disclosure and share numbering are part of the financial-control system.
