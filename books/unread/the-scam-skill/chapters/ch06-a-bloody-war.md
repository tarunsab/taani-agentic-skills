# Chapter 6: A Bloody War

## Core Idea

The 1991–92 market rise is portrayed as a war between an established bear-oriented cartel and Harshad Mehta's public, principal-bull operation. The conflict was fought with short sales, settlement prices, margins, institutional portfolios, bank money, promoter alliances, and reputation. The Sensex moved from roughly 700–800 levels to 4,467 in April 1992, and favored shares rose many times over; the scale of the move made every participant both beneficiary and hostage. The old cartel could absorb losses through banks and mutual funds, while Mehta used outside funding to attack shorts and establish a new center of power. The war became dangerous because neither side was merely expressing a view: both were trying to force the market and the other side to finance it.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Market microstructure as weapon:** margins, settlement prices, and carry-forward rules can be used strategically.
- **Cartel versus insurgent:** an incumbent network may defend access while a newcomer changes the funding route.
- **Reflexive escalation:** rising prices validate the bull, while short pressure and forced buying validate the bear.

## Key Concepts

### Two camps

At a March 14, 1992 house-warming hosted by Ajay Kayan, the chapter contrasts Kayan's discreet, institution-linked bear posture with Mehta's publicity-heavy principal role. The old network included Kothari and UTI, Citibank with C Mackertich and Stewart, and Canfina; Mehta relied more on BankAm and Grindlays. Their differences were cultural as well as financial. One side treated control and discretion as strength, while the other treated visibility and mass belief as force multipliers.

### The price ladder

The market moved from the 1990 index level of about 1,602, through the 1991 fall to around 956, to the extraordinary 1992 climb. Mehta accumulated favorites such as ACC and Apollo, then attacked the bears' short positions by buying aggressively and using institutional and bank-linked liquidity. The bears used settlement mechanics, margins, and mutual-fund support to withstand the pressure. The chapter's repeated comparisons to later 1999–2000 movements show the same mechanism in a new technology-stock costume.

### Institutional shock absorbers

The established network could shift losses into PMS clients or mutual-fund portfolios. One cited Citi arrangement transferred about Rs 89 crore to house brokers at PMS clients' expense, allowing the cartel to absorb pressure without exposing the whole position. Mehta's camp, by contrast, required a continuing supply of bank and corporate money. Both strategies weakened when the positions became too large for any normal buyer or when a regulator asked where the financing came from.

## Mental Models

When a price rise looks unstoppable, chart both sides' **inventory** and **funding**. A bull needs cash and future buyers; a bear needs borrowable stock, time, and margin. If either side is using client or institutional assets as a shock absorber, the market price is carrying hidden balance-sheet risk.

## Anti-patterns

- Calling a forced short-covering rally organic demand.
- Treating the bear cartel as a neutral source of price discovery.
- Assuming more volume means more liquidity when the same inventory circulates within a group.

## Worked Example

Mehta's January 1992 purchases broke resistance because they attacked the bear side when the latter depended on institutional support. As the price rose, shorts were forced to buy, strengthening the bull narrative. The same feedback loop later appears in Ketan Parekh's K-10 stocks, except the funding travelled through corporate accounts, cooperative banks, and inter-exchange positions.

## Key Takeaways

1. Market structure can turn a disagreement into a financing war.
2. The decisive variable is often who can carry the position longest.
3. Institutional portfolios may conceal the true distribution of gains and losses.
