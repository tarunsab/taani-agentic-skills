# Chapter 19: Justice Delayed

## Core Idea

The post-scam legal process became a second institutional failure: hundreds of civil cases, dozens of charge-sheets, attached assets, and travel applications moved slowly while evidence, witnesses, and money degraded. The Special Court was created to concentrate the cases, but it lacked specialist help for complex securities transactions and spent much of its time on procedural permissions. The authors contrast a public, time-bounded inquiry into the earlier Mundhra scandal with the closed-door JPC process and argue that secrecy reduced participation and accountability. A few convictions occurred—Hiten Dalal, the State Bank of Saurashtra case, and Harshad's Maruti case—but most cases remained unresolved. The chapter's thesis is that delay is not neutral: it rewards institutions that can write off losses and destroys weaker defendants whose assets and careers remain frozen.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Justice-delay spiral:** procedural complexity, staff turnover, and evidence loss reinforce one another.
- **Recovery versus punishment:** attaching assets or imprisoning a broker does not itself return money to victims.
- **Public inquiry as evidence infrastructure:** openness can recruit witnesses and expose the reasoning behind a decision.

## Key Concepts

### The slow court

The book reports about 600 civil cases, 72 CBI charge-sheets with only four advancing meaningfully, and a likely trial horizon of decades. By October 1999, the Special Court had processed 14,927 applications just for travel by notified persons; the Harshad group had also made many applications for legal fees. Shares held by the Custodian included registered, unregistered, and benami holdings across roughly 300 companies, but litigation blocked disposal. These procedures consumed court capacity without resolving the underlying transaction disputes.

### The farce of JPC

The JPC struggled with documents and political pressure and did not ask the central recovery question: where had the money gone and how? Harshad's allegation against Prime Minister Rao, the Goldstar rights-issue funding, and Romesh Bhandari's alleged role were relegated to “other issues” or attached notes. Opposition members sought further inquiry, while government members stressed contradictions; the consensus rested on minimum agreed points rather than a complete evidentiary record. The authors see the report as a political document that failed to explain the market.

### CBI, ED and other agencies

CBI allegedly planted false stories about Swiss accounts and narcotics, ignored K Madhavan's foreign-account lead, and used prolonged custody. ED had information about foreign money but delayed complaints for years. RBI knew that NHB's books were fictitious and still did not follow through; the 1994 penalties on 35 banks were significant in number but, in the authors' view, weak in deterrent effect. One conviction among 27 cases against Harshad is used as a measure of the gap between investigation and resolution.

## Mental Models

Track four clocks separately: **investigation**, **trial**, **asset recovery**, and **institutional reform**. A case can progress on one clock while the others stand still. For a complex financial case, appoint transaction experts early, preserve records, and publish a clear map of claim, evidence, and disposition.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating custody, charge-sheets, or penalties as completed justice.
- Using a closed inquiry that prevents affected investors from supplying evidence.
- Freezing assets indefinitely without a practical sale and distribution plan.

## Worked Example

The travel-permission backlog is a precise example of misallocated capacity. Tens of thousands of court minutes were spent deciding whether notified persons could leave Bombay, while the Custodian's shares and 600-odd civil cases remained unresolved. A procedural safeguard became the dominant output of the system and displaced recovery work.

## Key Takeaways

1. Financial justice requires specialist reconstruction, not only ordinary hearings.
2. Delay redistributes harm toward people without money or institutional protection.
3. Public accountability and recovery need to be designed into the inquiry from the start.
