# Chapter 12: Skins

**Part**: Part Three — The Long Fetch.

## Core Idea

“Skins” asks what lies beneath the visible surface of a holiday coast. Newquay presents itself as a town that depends on seasonal visitors while making rough sleepers, workers, and local housing problems easy to overlook. An ex-soldier leads the walkers toward a soup kitchen and St Petroc's support, where they learn that official counts can miss people who sleep rough outside the permitted time or place and that shelters cannot meet every need. Further along, the coast's beautiful dunes and mines carry histories of dangerous labor, chemical weapons, compensation, and delayed institutional responsibility. Raynor's own skin becomes a record of heat, salt, dehydration, age, and movement, while the people they meet keep misreading them as tramps, poets, or harmless tourists. The chapter connects bodily exposure to social exposure: both show what a comfortable public surface can conceal.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Look beneath the tourist surface**: When a place is marketed as leisure, ask where workers live, where rough sleepers go, and who carries the cost of the visitor economy.
  - When to use: When public prosperity coexists with invisible deprivation.
  - How: Follow the services, housing, labor, and people excluded from the postcard view.
- **Count the missing people**: An official figure is shaped by its counting rule; a narrow count can make a structural problem appear small.
  - When to use: When an institution reports a precise number about a fluid, hidden, or stigmatized population.
  - How: Ask who qualifies, where the count occurs, who confirms it, and who is absent.
- **Read the body as a record, not a verdict**: Weathered skin shows what the route has done, but does not tell the whole story of capacity, age, or worth.

## Key Concepts

- **Newquay**: A surf and party town whose winter economy and local exclusion sit in tension.
- **Soup kitchen**: Immediate food support encountered through an ex-soldier.
- **St Petroc's**: Support for single homeless people.
- **Rough-sleeper count**: A limited official method that can undercount people outside the chosen criteria.
- **Leisure huts**: Coastal structures that might shelter people but are more likely to be treated as holiday property.
- **St Agnes mine**: A site carrying industrial and environmental history.
- **Sarin B**: The chemical-weapons context and associated deaths, illness, payouts, and cleanup.
- **Kissing gate**: A pack-handling technique for passing narrow gates.
- **Skin**: Both physical exposure and the social surface that conceals hardship.

## Mental Models

- **Use a definition audit**: Before accepting a number, inspect the rule that generated it. Precision in a statistic does not guarantee completeness.
- **Treat visible wear as evidence of a process**: Cracked skin, worn clothes, and damaged gear tell the story of heat, salt, labor, and time; they are not proof of moral failure.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assume a holiday town has no homelessness**: Tourism can make rough sleeping less visible while intensifying housing pressure.
- **Use the official count as the whole reality**: Counting rules can exclude people precisely because they are mobile or hidden.
- **Turn the coast's history into scenery**: Mines, chemical sites, and military fences carry human costs that the landscape does not advertise.

## Worked Example

In Newquay, the couple meet an ex-soldier who directs them toward a soup kitchen and learn about St Petroc's. They hear that rough-sleeper figures are made by counting people at a particular time and place, which leaves many people outside the number. In Perranporth and St Agnes, the holiday huts, mines, military site, and chemical-weapons history make the coast's social and industrial layers visible. At a kissing gate, a group films the walkers and once again treats Moth as Simon Armitage, turning their appearance into a public story. The day ends with seals, Godrevy light, and a bus to St Ives, but beauty has not erased the exposed lives beneath it.

## Key Takeaways

1. Inspect the labor and housing beneath a place's tourist image.
2. Audit the definitions behind official numbers about hidden populations.
3. Treat bodily wear as evidence of conditions, not a character judgment.
4. Keep environmental, military, industrial, and social histories in the same landscape.
