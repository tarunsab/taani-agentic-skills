# Chapter 5: Walk

**Part**: Part Two — The South West Coast Path.

## Core Idea

“Walk” moves the couple from the shock of starting into the repetitive physical work that makes the route possible. The cliffs bring exhilaration and danger, while towns expose the contrast between paid comfort, tourism, housing, and the walkers' narrowing purse. Moth's pain and balance make every steep section a caregiving decision, and Raynor begins to fear that suggesting the journey may be accelerating his decline. The chapter's clearest medical crisis comes when they realize that Moth's Pregabalin is still in the van after he has stopped taking it; withdrawal, heat, dehydration, and the underlying illness make the night on the beach frightening. The pair continue not because the route has become safe, but because the next action—cooling him, making tea, finding food, or reaching Clovelly—keeps open a future that stopping would close.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Pace for continuity, not pride**: The walkers repeatedly choose a slower rhythm, a stream, a ferry, or a place to stop over an impressive mileage target.
  - When to use: When performance pressure threatens the shared plan.
  - How: Use the body's response and the next resource point to set the pace.
- **Track medication as part of route planning**: A prescription left in a vehicle can become a route, transport, and emergency problem, not merely a packing oversight.
  - When to use: Whenever a journey separates a person from medication or clinical support.
  - How: Check supplies before departure, identify access points, and do not infer safety from a temporary improvement.
- **Hold responsibility and uncertainty together**: Raynor asks whether the walk is harming Moth, but cannot answer from fear alone. She observes, helps, and keeps moving while the question remains open.

## Key Concepts

- **Great Hangman**: A demanding height where heat and steepness expose the cost of the route.
- **Braunton Burrows**: A military-closed stretch that forces a truck rescue and a bus detour.
- **Bideford Black**: Local geological material encountered near the hot cliffs.
- **Peppercombe**: The stream and shade where Moth collapses in the heat.
- **Pregabalin**: Moth's nerve-pain medication, forgotten in the van.
- **Withdrawal**: The dangerous cluster of symptoms Raynor fears after the medication stops.
- **Clovelly**: The estate village they reach while hungry and under-resourced.

## Mental Models

- **Use resource points as anchors**: Streams, shops, ferries, and shaded places matter as much as named destinations. A route is a chain of recovery points, not a line on a map.
- **Prefer observable claims**: “He seems clearer” is a report of experience; “the walk cured him” is a conclusion the memoir does not support.

## Anti-patterns

- **Turn endurance into a moral contest**: The couple's decision to continue is shaped by illness, money, weather, and fear; it is not a test of who can suffer most.
- **Stop a prescribed medication casually**: The narrative presents the forgotten Pregabalin and possible withdrawal as a serious danger, not an experiment to imitate.
- **Spend the day's strength on the wrong detour**: Braunton Burrows and town routes show that a technically correct path can still be operationally impossible.

## Worked Example

After a hot stretch, Moth falls at Peppercombe and cannot continue in the sun. Raynor reads the map, identifies the stream, and gets him into shade rather than treating the collapse as a character test. At the beach they discover that his Pregabalin is still in the van; returning would cost time and transport, while stopping suddenly may cause withdrawal. Moth develops headache, nausea, sweating, shakes, and sickness, and Raynor manages water, food, shelter, washing, and observation through the night. He wakes later clearer and stronger, but the episode remains a warning: the improvement is real as an experience, while its cause and safety are unresolved.

## Key Takeaways

1. Set pace by the capacity required to continue tomorrow.
2. Put medication, access, and recovery points on the route plan.
3. Keep observed bodily changes separate from medical claims.
4. A detour can be the safest way to preserve the larger journey.
5. Hunger and heat narrow judgment; use shade, water, food, and help before interpretation.
