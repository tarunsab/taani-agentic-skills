## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Walk the next step when the whole future is unmanageable
When life has collapsed into legal loss, illness, and homelessness, Winn and Moth reduce the horizon to the next practical movement: pack, find water, reach the next landmark, or put one foot in front of the other. This is not denial that the larger problems exist; it is a way of making action possible while the larger problems remain unresolved. The Coast Path gives them a repeatable rhythm and a form of certainty that ordinary life no longer supplies. The boundary matters: the path does not cure CBD, restore the farm, or guarantee housing, so the model is a coping discipline rather than a promise of redemption.

### 2. Let the path become home without romanticizing homelessness
The walkers discover that wild space feels safer and more truthful to them than towns, institutions, and the stigmatizing assumptions attached to rough sleeping. Their tent, guidebook, weather routines, food, and shared movement form a temporary home made of practice rather than property. At the same time, the memoir records hunger, cold, medical risk, fear, inaccessible services, and the exhaustion of having nowhere to return to. Use the distinction carefully: choosing a path as a survivable environment is not the same as saying homelessness is easy or voluntary for everyone.

### 3. Treat constraints as decision data
Money, ferry fares, medication, weather, tides, access to water, campsite prices, credit history, and Moth's changing body repeatedly alter what is possible. The pair make decisions with the resources actually available: they buy lighter equipment, budget food and crossings, accept help, skip built-up miles when safety and cash require it, and later choose paid work and a student loan as routes toward a roof. Their choices are often imperfect and sometimes risky, but the memoir makes the trade-offs visible rather than presenting grit as sufficient. Before acting, name the constraint, the cost of each option, and the next deadline.

### 4. Tell the truth when a relationship can hold it
Early in the walk they tell strangers that they sold their house because that story is socially acceptable, while “we lost it and are homeless” attracts discomfort or rejection. The lie briefly protects them from pity and lets them inhabit a more flattering story, but it also keeps grief at a distance. Later Moth begins telling the truth, and Anna responds with coffee, attention, and an apartment rather than the recoil they expect. The lesson is not that disclosure is always safe; it is that truthful disclosure can create real connection when the listener has room for it.

### 5. Accept mortality so that present life is not consumed by avoidance
Moth names the likely end of CBD and asks that their ashes eventually travel together along the coast. Raynor can finally acknowledge that he will lose the fight without treating that acknowledgment as surrender. In the memoir, letting death into the tent also lets life return: touch, memory, humor, appetite, and a present moment no longer organized entirely around fear. This is a relational practice of honest attention, not a medical intervention or a claim that acceptance removes pain. It applies when avoidance has become more consuming than the fact being avoided.

### 6. Receive and extend practical solidarity
The path is sustained by small acts: a shower, hot water, pasties, food, a lift, a place to sleep, a coffee, a sandwich, a job, and finally Anna's offer of a rental. The walkers also pass help onward, sharing food with people sleeping rough even when their own supplies are low. These encounters expose the difference between abstract judgments about homelessness and the person in front of you. Prefer concrete, dignified help that meets the immediate need, while recognizing that kindness does not replace secure housing, healthcare, or structural change.

### 7. Keep identity larger than diagnosis, age, property, or social role
Moth is not reduced to CBD: he remains a partner, storyteller, student, joker, reader, worker, and decision-maker. Raynor is not only a former farmer, a homeless woman, or a carer; walking lets her notice capacities and desires outside the identities that loss has stripped away. Street encounters expose how quickly strangers assign labels such as “tramp,” “old,” “poet,” or “hiker,” and how those labels can be both wounding and unexpectedly generous. Use this model to ask what a person can still choose and contribute, without pretending that illness or material conditions are irrelevant.
