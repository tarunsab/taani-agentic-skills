# Chapter 18: Qualified Success

## Thesis

Stuxnet's result cannot be reduced to either “it destroyed the centrifuges” or “it failed.” David Albright's distinction is the chapter's organizing test: the operation would have failed if its goal was to destroy all centrifuges, but may have succeeded if its goal was to delay a limited part of the program. Estimates ranged from no durable setback to roughly eighteen–twenty-four months or several years, because sanctions, diplomacy, ordinary technical problems, other covert actions, and malware all affected the timeline. The most defensible conclusion is qualified: Stuxnet damaged or disrupted a real process and may have bought time, while Iran recovered, expanded enrichment, and moved toward more efficient centrifuges.

## Mechanism and source evidence

Iran's low-enriched-uranium production dipped around Stuxnet's active periods but later rose to roughly 120–150 kilograms per month, even as more centrifuges were needed to produce it. Olli Heinonen estimated that more than 1,000 and perhaps closer to 2,000 centrifuges were damaged, partly because IAEA reports were periodic snapshots and broken seals might have permitted unseen replacements; other researchers found the evidence circumstantial. Iran wasted uranium gas, lost scarce equipment, and faced tighter sanctions, but it also installed IR-2m and IR-4 centrifuges that were faster and more resilient. By 2013, negotiations produced a temporary freeze and limits, leaving open the possibility that Stuxnet helped create diplomatic time without being the decisive cause.

## Distinctions and limitations

Tactical effect, operational delay, strategic deterrence, and political settlement are separate outcomes. A weapon can achieve the first and contribute to the second while failing at the third or worsening long-term security. Public IAEA data cannot isolate malware from equipment design, hurried installation, sanctions, or human response, and claims based on anonymous sources may conflict with forensic logs. The operation was effectively one-shot: once exposed, the target could inspect, reconfigure, and distrust the same assumptions, even if the first attack had worked.

## Practical use

Evaluate cyber operations against a predeclared outcome tree: what was supposed to change, by when, for how long, and at what cost? Measure recovery and adaptation, not just initial damage. Include opportunity costs such as lost secrecy, burned vulnerabilities, hardened targets, copycat capability, legal exposure, and diplomatic backlash. For defenders, rehearse reconfiguration after a targeted attack; the ability to change the target's assumptions can be a powerful recovery asset.
