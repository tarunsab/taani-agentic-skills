# Chapter 12: A New Fighting Domain

## Thesis

Cyberwar did not appear suddenly with Stuxnet; it emerged from decades of military thinking about network dependence, electronic warfare, and computer attack. The US military repeatedly discovered that its systems were both powerful and exposed, from early hacker incidents to the 1997 Eligible Receiver exercise that showed off-the-shelf tools could create severe disruption. Institutions such as JTF-CND and later US Cyber Command grew amid an unresolved tension: the same agencies were expected to defend networks and develop offensive capabilities. The chapter argues that doctrine, authority, and vulnerability disclosure lagged behind technical capability.

## Mechanism and source evidence

The source traces ideas from a 1970 Defense Science Board discussion and the 1993 RAND paper “Cyberwar Is Coming!” through the Air Force's information-warfare units, Solar Sunrise, Eligible Receiver, and post-9/11 offensive planning. It describes the US shift toward a fifth domain, the 2003 Information Operations Roadmap, the separation and later recombination of offensive and defensive functions, and the role of Gen. Keith Alexander. The vulnerability-equities problem appears as a policy choice: disclose a flaw to protect systems or retain it to enable intelligence and attack. The source cites an average zero-day life of roughly ten months, with some remaining useful for years, making every retained vulnerability a race against discovery.

## Distinctions and limitations

Cyber espionage, network mapping, disruption, and physical sabotage do not carry identical legal or strategic thresholds, even if they use overlapping tools. Attribution can be obscured by proxies and false flags, so the ability to penetrate a system does not establish who will be blamed. The chapter describes institutions and reported policy choices, not a complete classified history; public readers cannot verify every operation or rule of engagement. It also avoids assuming that an offensive capability automatically provides defensive superiority.

## Practical use

Governance should separate operational need from the security interest of system owners, and should require explicit review of reuse, domestic exposure, patchability, and collateral effects. A mature equities process should document who was consulted, how long retention is justified, what mitigations protect allies, and what triggers disclosure. Defenders should assume that a vulnerability retained elsewhere may eventually be discovered and use compensating controls rather than waiting for a vendor patch. Policy exercises should test attribution uncertainty and escalation, not just whether an intrusion can be technically executed.
