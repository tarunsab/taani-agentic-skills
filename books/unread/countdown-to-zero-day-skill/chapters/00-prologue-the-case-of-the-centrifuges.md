# Prologue: The Case of the Centrifuges

## Thesis

The book opens with a physical mystery that investigators initially cannot explain: Iran's Natanz enrichment plant is replacing centrifuges at a rate far above normal wear. In January 2010, IAEA observers notice that machines are being removed and replaced even though the agency's mandate is primarily to track nuclear material, not diagnose equipment failures. The ordinary baseline matters: an IR-1 centrifuge was expected to last about a decade, and annual replacement of roughly 10 percent could be normal, but reports suggested that hundreds or perhaps around a thousand machines had been damaged. The mystery is therefore a bridge between nuclear-inspection evidence and a later digital explanation.

## Mechanism and source evidence

Natanz contained thousands of centrifuges arranged into cascades, so a failure pattern could be visible in installation and replacement counts before anyone understood its cause. IAEA cameras watched the facility from fixed positions while inspectors compiled reports from periodic visits; that created useful evidence but not continuous process telemetry. The source places the initial digital intrusion in June 2009, when a destructive program had entered Iranian systems and was designed to reach the control environment without announcing itself. Its significance was not simply that computers were compromised, but that software was aimed at preventing or delaying a nuclear-enrichment process.

## Distinctions and limitations

The Prologue distinguishes equipment failure from sabotage, material accounting from machinery diagnosis, and public evidence from classified intelligence. The replacement count is an observation; the estimate of how many centrifuges were damaged is a reconstruction that depends on incomplete inspection windows. The source does not present this opening as a fully proven chain of causation at the time it occurred. It instead establishes the investigative question that later chapters answer by combining malware analysis, industrial expertise, and IAEA reporting.

## Practical use

When reviewing an industrial incident, begin with the unexplained physical symptom and establish a normal failure baseline before naming malware. Ask what the monitoring authority can actually observe, how often it observes it, and which important failure modes fall outside its mandate. Track replacement, downtime, production, and maintenance records alongside endpoint evidence. A good investigation preserves the possibility of mundane explanations while testing whether several weak clues align into a stronger causal account.

## Connections

This opening anticipates the later distinction between a digital weapon's technical effect and its strategic result. It connects directly to Chapter 3's history of Natanz, Chapter 8's explanation of process manipulation, and Chapter 18's debate over how many centrifuges Stuxnet affected. It also foreshadows the book's evidence problem: an operation designed to look like equipment trouble leaves the public with fragments rather than a single decisive record.
