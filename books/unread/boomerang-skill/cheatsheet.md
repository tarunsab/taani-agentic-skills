# Cheatsheet

## Decision Rules

| If you see… | Ask / do this | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A “private” bank is too important to fail | Add its likely rescue cost to public debt and compare with tax revenue | Legal ownership does not define the real loss perimeter |
| A boom justified by “this time is different” | Re-anchor prices to rent, income, cash flow, population, and prior bubbles | Stories become testable when compared with outside anchors |
| A triple-A rating or “fundamentally sound” claim | Inspect underlying assets, funding maturity, concentration, and monitoring | Form can be clean while substance is dangerous |
| A blanket guarantee is proposed | Separate depositors, bondholders, shareholders, and taxpayers | “Contagion” does not eliminate distributional choices |
| A deficit number changes suddenly | Start a reconciliation / discovery process; seek independent records | The first official number may be a political artifact |
| Future receipts are sold for cash today | Identify what future flexibility has already been spent | Monetization can disguise present insolvency |
| A warning is attacked as jealousy or self-interest | Verify the evidence independently before judging the messenger | A self-interested warning can still be true |
| A system has a formal collective structure but no trust | Inspect enforcement, reciprocity, and who can shift costs to others | Civic spirit determines whether institutions work |
| Resources are cut but demand remains | Measure failure modes and redesign the service around the new margin | “Do more with less” is not an operating plan |

## Country / System Contrast

| Case | What cheap credit enabled | Where the loss landed |
|---|---|---|
| Iceland | Foreign acquisitions, carry trades, oversized banks, and male financial overconfidence | A tiny state and households facing bank and currency collapse |
| Greece | Public payrolls, pensions, bribes, tax evasion, and future-income financing | Citizens, banks, and external rescuers; civic trust deteriorated |
| Ireland | Property development financed by foreign bank funding | Taxpayers after the state guaranteed bank creditors |
| Germany | Domestic restraint alongside foreign purchases of risky structured debt | German banks and the European rescue system |
| United States / California | Household, state, city, pension, and public-safety promises | Lower levels of government, future workers, and degraded services |

## Tells & Smells

- Prices rise far faster than rents or income.
- A bank grows faster than the economy that must rescue it.
- Everyone repeats the same reassuring phrase while independent data is scarce.
- Analysts debate tiny contractual distinctions while no one can describe the underlying loans.
- A guarantee is called “free” because losses are not yet visible.
- A higher level of government reports improvement after pushing obligations to cities or households.
- A warning becomes a loyalty test: “you’re either for us or against us.”
- A service’s staffing falls while response time, workload, or error cost is left unmeasured.

## Minimal Investigation Sequence

1. Map who borrowed, who lent, and who is expected to rescue whom.
2. Replace the headline metric with revenue, cash flow, rents, or actual demand.
3. Inspect the underlying asset and the incentives of the intermediary.
4. Trace the obligation to the person, level of government, or future period that absorbs it.
5. Test whether the institution can still coordinate around a common purpose.
6. If the environment has changed, redesign the work and its safety margin.
