# Chapter IV: The Secret Lives of Germans

## Core Idea

Germany stands on the creditor side of the crisis: it avoided a domestic consumer and real-estate credit boom, yet German banks financed foreign excess and became Europe’s decisive source of rescue money. Jörg Asmussen describes a country in which borrowing for consumption was culturally unacceptable and fiscal responsibility had broad electoral support, while the euro forced Germans to choose between fiscal integration and demanding painful “structural reform” from countries with different institutions and habits. German banking nevertheless became dirty abroad: banks put large sums into Iceland, Ireland, Greece, and U.S. subprime securities, often through offshore vehicles that hid the exposures from ordinary oversight. IKB’s Rhineland Funding borrowed short-term commercial paper, bought long-term structured credit, relied on a triple-A rating, and continued expanding even after the price of risk collapsed; its analysts were precise about small contractual distinctions but did not know what loans actually sat inside the bonds. Lewis calls this “clean on the outside, dirty on the inside,” and distinguishes German failure from Wall Street’s: German traders were comparatively poorly paid and may have been incompetent rather than deliberately predatory, but they still treated ratings and rules as substitutes for judgment. The euro’s technical rules and the European Central Bank’s collateral problem made a Greek default a threat to banks and sovereigns across the continent, so economically necessary aid became politically unacceptable. The chapter uses German folklore, historical memory, and Commerzbank’s hostility to trader pay to explore a culture that trusts order and official forms, while also warning—through Charlotte’s objections—that national-character explanations are rhetorical lenses, not complete causal accounts.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Clean outside, dirty inside**: A system can look orderly, compliant, and well rated while its underlying exposures, incentives, and counterparties are dangerous.
- **Riskless-asset fallacy**: A rating or formal rule cannot remove the risk that justifies a return; a triple-A label is not a guarantee of safety.
- **Form over substance**: Precise adherence to categories, models, or checkboxes can conceal failure to understand the economic object being evaluated.
- **Rules as a substitute for judgment**: Rules coordinate behavior only when participants share the same game; they can mislead when one side follows them strategically or dishonestly.
- **Fiscal-integration dilemma**: A currency union with unequal fiscal cultures must either share resources and losses or demand reforms that may be politically and economically destructive.
- **Moral-arbiter position**: The largest creditor can become the de facto judge of other countries’ behavior, even when its own institutions helped create the exposures.

## Key Concepts

- **Triple-A rating**: The highest formal credit rating, treated by the investors in this chapter as if it meant no meaningful risk.
- **Riskless asset**: A misleading category for an asset whose return necessarily compensates for some risk.
- **Conduit**: An off-balance-sheet or offshore vehicle used to borrow short and invest in longer-term assets.
- **Rhineland Funding**: IKB’s Delaware-incorporated, Dublin-listed conduit that bought U.S. consumer-loan-backed securities.
- **Collateralized debt obligation (CDO)**: A structured bond backed by pools of loans or other debt claims.
- **Landesbank**: A German state-linked bank; in the chapter, part of the class of boring institutions courted by Wall Street salespeople.
- **Structural reform**: The demand that debtor countries change labor, tax, spending, and productivity institutions to fit the currency union.
- **Fiscal integration**: Sharing tax resources and obligations across countries in a common fiscal system.
- **ECB collateral rule**: The European Central Bank’s restriction on accepting bonds rated in default, which magnified the consequences of a Greek default.

## Mental Models

- **Inspect the substrate**: For a rated or modeled instrument, identify the cash flows, originators, leverage, funding maturity, and monitoring system underneath the label.
- **Separate error from incentive**: Ask whether a bad risk was taken for private gain, from ignorance, or because an institution rewarded volume and target achievement.
- **Test the union’s missing contract**: When different regions share money, specify who bears losses, who controls policy, and whether the rules are credible in a crisis.

## Anti-patterns

- **Ticking every box**: A checklist about originators or ratings cannot replace portfolio-level exposure analysis.
- **Assuming supervision is substance**: The German view of New York supervision survived until e-mail evidence and losses showed that official oversight had not constrained behavior.
- **Confusing shared currency with shared culture**: A common monetary instrument does not automatically create common tax compliance, productivity, or political legitimacy.
- **Blaming only the visible outsider**: Germany’s losses were enabled by both aggressive foreign sellers and German institutions willing to accept their story.

## Worked Example

IKB’s Rhineland Funding shows how formal sophistication can coexist with conceptual blindness. The conduit borrowed short and bought longer-term CDOs backed by American consumer loans, while IKB guaranteed the structure and Moody’s gave its commercial paper the highest rating. After the price of risk fell in 2005, management sought to preserve its profit target by buying more; after senior staff left, five young officers bought at increasing scale and the portfolio grew from roughly $10 billion to $20 billion, on its way toward $30 billion. They rejected some mortgage originators but accepted others, even though the bonds they compared could collapse from par to only 2 or 3 percent; meanwhile IKB declined a $6.5 million system that would have shown what loans sat behind its $20 billion portfolio. The bank ultimately lost more than $15 billion against about $4 billion of capital and had to be rescued. The mechanism is not simply “German bankers were reckless”: it is that rules, ratings, and local precision prevented the institution from seeing the exposure that mattered.

## Limits & Distinctions

The chapter’s scatological folklore is a literary device used to connect public cleanliness with hidden disorder; it is not evidence that a national psyche caused a banking loss. Charlotte explicitly challenges the idea that eighty million people share one character, and the book does not resolve that objection empirically. The fiscal-integration dilemma is described from the crisis-era perspective, with dated ECB balances, political promises, and reported losses. Distinguish the book’s cultural interpretation from current German or European policy facts.

## Key Takeaways

1. Treat ratings, rules, and model precision as inputs to judgment, not substitutes for it.
2. Map the underlying assets and the monitoring system before trusting a structured product.
3. Examine compensation and institutional incentives before assigning intent.
4. In a currency union, make loss-sharing and enforcement assumptions explicit.
5. A creditor can be both disciplined at home and dangerously permissive abroad.
6. Keep national-character claims provisional and test them against institutional evidence.
