# Chapter III: Ireland’s Original Sin

## Core Idea

Ireland’s catastrophe came from a different use of the same cheap credit: Irish banks borrowed abroad and used the money to buy Ireland from Irish property developers, homeowners, and one another. The Celtic Tiger had already produced a real but poorly understood transformation from poverty to exceptional wealth, supported by factors such as trade access, free higher education, a low corporate tax rate, and a favorable working-age population; because no one could explain the success fully, the country entered the boom without a stable sense of what normal growth looked like. Economist Morgan Kelly punctured the “soft landing” story by comparing house prices with income, rents, and prior bubbles, then tracing the property boom into bank funding, falling lending standards, and a €100 billion exposure to developers. The decisive policy error came on September 29, 2008, when the government guaranteed the debts of the major banks after advisers and regulators called them “fundamentally sound”; the guarantee converted private losses into a national obligation and rescued bondholders who could not have withdrawn their money like depositors. Anglo Irish was not inherently systemic—it had few branches and was essentially a property lender—but it became systemic once the state made its losses everyone’s. Ireland then created Anglo’s national successor, the National Asset Management Agency, while the European Central Bank and foreign creditors gained practical control over the country’s finances. The chapter’s human counterpoint is Irish silence: shame, tight lips, and social loyalty suppressed protest even as ghost estates, receivership, unemployment, and a shrinking economy made the cost visible; Gary Keogh’s rotten-egg protest and Joe McNamara’s cement mixer show that silence is not the same as consent.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The iron law of house prices**: The more house prices rise relative to incomes and rents, the more they subsequently fall; test the narrative against anchors outside the price itself.
- **The family lie**: A belief can remain “true” inside a closed group while everyone benefits from not asking whether it is sustainable. Comparison to other places or periods breaks the spell.
- **Bank-funding mismatch**: When domestic banks shift from local deposits to easily withdrawn foreign funding while concentrating loans in property, a local price reversal can become a cross-border liquidity crisis.
- **The guarantee trap**: A blanket promise can calm markets immediately while making the state responsible for losses it could not afford and removing expected discipline from private creditors.
- **Debt power reversal**: Small debts leave a borrower dependent on the bank; very large debts can make the bank and state dependent on the borrower’s survival.
- **Ghost-estate evidence**: Empty, unfinished developments are physical evidence that expected demand, financing, and the story of future growth were all overstated.

## Key Concepts

- **Celtic Tiger**: Ireland’s rapid late-twentieth-century rise from poverty to high measured prosperity.
- **Soft landing**: The claim that an inflated real-estate market can slow without a sharp price and credit contraction.
- **Iron law of house prices**: Kelly’s price-to-income-and-rents heuristic for identifying an unsustainable housing boom.
- **Bank guarantee**: The Irish state’s promise to cover major bank liabilities, including private bondholders.
- **Senior bondholder**: A creditor with a claim on a bank, distinct from a depositor who can withdraw funds.
- **National Asset Management Agency (NAMA)**: Ireland’s vehicle for taking roughly €80 billion of troubled commercial-property loans from banks.
- **Ghost estate**: An unfinished or largely empty housing development built on the assumption of demand that never arrived.
- **Receivership**: A severe Irish insolvency process that publicly constrained the debtor’s future borrowing, assets, earnings, and travel.

## Mental Models

- **Re-anchor the story**: Compare prices with rents, incomes, population, and prior bubbles before accepting claims of national uniqueness.
- **Follow funding, not only lending**: A bank’s loan book can look local while its ability to survive depends on foreign money that can disappear quickly.
- **Ask who was rescued**: Distinguish deposit protection from making sophisticated bond investors whole, then trace the cost to taxpayers and future public services.

## Anti-patterns

- **Calling a property bubble a liquidity problem**: If prices depend on expectations and credit, a pause in belief can expose insolvency rather than merely temporary illiquidity.
- **Letting advisers’ conflicts define the diagnosis**: Merrill Lynch earned from Irish banks and then retracted a report that questioned their lending; its later advice supported the guarantee.
- **Using legalism after the fact**: “The law required it” can be narrowly accurate while hiding available political choices, such as imposing losses on senior creditors.
- **Protecting the family narrative**: Treating criticism as disloyalty delays recognition and makes the eventual correction more painful.

## Worked Example

Kelly’s investigation is a compact bubble test. He found that more than one-fifth of the workforce was building houses, construction approached a quarter of GDP, Dublin prices had risen more than 500 percent since 1994, rents in some places were under 1 percent of purchase prices, and lending to construction and real estate rose from about 8 percent of bank lending to 28 percent. Irish banks had moved from domestic deposits in 1997 to heavy foreign funding by 2005, while roughly €100 billion—about the country’s total bank deposits—went to commercial property developers. Kelly first estimated a 40–50 percent fall relative to income and a possible 66 percent fall at the peak, then connected the property decline to bank losses, reduced lending to useful businesses, and a construction stop. The value of the example is not the exact forecast; it is the chain from external anchor, to asset price, to bank balance sheet, to public economy.

## Limits & Distinctions

The chapter does not deny that Ireland’s earlier growth had real foundations; it stresses that the country could not explain how much of the later prosperity was durable. The account of the September 2008 guarantee relies on retrospective interviews and disputed legal and policy judgments, so the book’s explanation should not be mistaken for a complete institutional record. “Irish silence” is a narrative contrast, not evidence that all citizens accepted the bailout or shared one psychological type. All figures and forecasts belong to the book’s historical moment.

## Key Takeaways

1. Test real-estate prices against rents, incomes, population, and history.
2. Map bank lending and bank funding separately.
3. Separate deposits that can run from bonds that can absorb losses.
4. Treat a guarantee as a distributional choice, not a free confidence-restoring gesture.
5. Use physical leftovers—empty sites, idle buildings, failed projects—as evidence against an abstract growth story.
6. Do not infer consent from the absence of protest.
