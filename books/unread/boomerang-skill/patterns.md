# Patterns

## Balance-Sheet Perimeter
**When to use**: A private institution is widely believed to be too important to fail.
**How**: Add likely rescue obligations to official debt; compare the total with recurring revenue, liquidity, and credible policy capacity.
**Trade-offs**: It exposes tail risk early, but the rescue boundary and political willingness to pay may remain uncertain.

The book’s central accounting move is to look past the legal owner of a liability. Iceland, Ireland, and the U.S. financial crisis all show private losses becoming public when officials fear contagion. The relevant question is not only “what does the government owe?” but also “what will it be forced to honor?” The method is a risk perimeter, not a prediction that every contingent liability will be realized (Ch 0, Ch 1, Ch 3).

## Bubble Re-Anchoring
**When to use**: People defend a rising asset price with a story of exceptional growth or national uniqueness.
**How**: Compare the price with rents, incomes, population, cash flow, and prior bubbles; then map the lenders funding the purchase.
**Trade-offs**: Historical comparisons can miss genuine structural change, but refusing comparison leaves the story self-validating.

Morgan Kelly breaks Ireland’s “soft landing” narrative by comparing house prices with rents and income rather than with other houses. Once the price is re-anchored, construction concentration and bank exposure reveal how a property story becomes a banking story. The same technique applies to any asset whose value is justified mainly by the expectation that someone else will pay more (Ch 3).
Kelly’s method allows genuine growth but refuses to let the current price provide its own justification.

## Underlying-Asset Inspection
**When to use**: A product is highly rated, modeled, or checklist-compliant.
**How**: Trace cash flows to the underlying borrowers; inspect maturity mismatch, concentration, monitoring, incentives, and the meaning of the rating.
**Trade-offs**: It costs time and may reduce apparent diversification, but it catches risks that formal categories hide.

IKB could distinguish mortgage originators to the last basis point while failing to know whether its complex bonds contained prime or subprime loans. The problem was not a lack of arithmetic; it was a missing view of the object being measured. Use the pattern whenever precision about a proxy is replacing understanding of the real exposure (Ch 4).
The failure came from not distinguishing the risk in the thing itself from the neatness of the analysis around it.

## Future-Income Monetization
**When to use**: A borrower wants immediate cash against taxes, tolls, lottery receipts, property, or another future stream.
**How**: Identify the future income, discount it, ask what remains after the cash is spent, and test whether the asset can support the promise under slower growth.
**Trade-offs**: Up-front money can fund useful investment, but it removes future flexibility and can hide current insolvency.

Greek officials sold or pledged future receipts to meet present needs, while Irish and Icelandic borrowers used future appreciation as if it were income. The common mechanism is bringing tomorrow’s capacity into today’s spending. It is sustainable only when the future stream is real, measurable, and not already promised elsewhere (Ch 2, Ch 3).
The danger grows when the future stream is treated as both evidence of wealth and collateral for more borrowing.

## Messenger-Discount Check
**When to use**: A warning comes from someone who could profit from a downturn or who lacks conventional standing.
**How**: Separate the messenger’s incentive from the claim; verify the data independently; compare the warning with observable balance-sheet evidence.
**Trade-offs**: Dismissing self-interested warnings can protect against manipulation, but dismissing them solely because of motive can preserve a dangerous consensus.

Danske Bank, Bob Aliber, Morgan Kelly, and Meredith Whitney were each discounted or misrepresented in different ways. Their incentives and credentials mattered, but the stronger test was whether the underlying evidence held up. A warning can be both self-interested and true; credibility requires inspecting the mechanism rather than choosing between trust and cynicism (Ch 1, Ch 3, Ch 5).

## Guarantee-Distribution Test
**When to use**: A crisis response proposes a blanket guarantee to restore confidence.
**How**: List depositors, bondholders, shareholders, banks, taxpayers, and future governments; model who can flee, who can absorb losses, and what incentives the guarantee creates.
**Trade-offs**: A guarantee can stop a run immediately, but it may make the state insolvent and reward the private decision-makers who created the risk.

Ireland’s guarantee calmed markets and made private bank debts national obligations. The book distinguishes depositors, who can withdraw, from bondholders, who cannot run in the same way and might have absorbed losses. “Contagion” is a real concern, but it does not make every distributional choice free or inevitable (Ch 3).
The immediate calming effect can therefore conceal a transfer of wealth and risk rather than eliminate the crisis.

## Culture-as-Infrastructure Diagnosis
**When to use**: Financial symptoms persist despite new rules, funding, or personnel.
**How**: Ask which behaviors are rewarded, whether enforcement is credible, how people trust one another, and whether actors can sacrifice the common good without consequence.
**Trade-offs**: Cultural diagnosis reveals durable incentives, but national-character explanations can overgeneralize and obscure specific institutional causes.

Greece’s weak records and non-enforcement made tax evasion self-reinforcing; Vallejo’s financial hole was accompanied by a collapse of trust between city, unions, and citizens. The diagnosis treats finance as a symptom and behavior as part of the causal system. It should be applied to observable norms and incentives, not to stereotypes about an entire population (Ch 2, Ch 5).
Changing the rule book without changing these expectations leaves the equilibrium intact.

## Scarcity Redesign
**When to use**: A service must meet similar demand with fewer staff, money, stations, or margin for error.
**How**: Measure the new demand and failure modes; locate the reduced margin; tailor training, staffing, response, and priorities to the changed environment.
**Trade-offs**: Redesign can preserve the mission, but it cannot make the lost capacity or risk disappear.

Paige Meyer’s Vallejo fire department did not solve scarcity by pretending conditions were unchanged. He measured injuries, response-time loss, and larger fires, then reconsidered fitness and tactics for the remaining team. This is the book’s practical counterpoint to debt denial: once the environment changes, the operating model must change too (Ch 5).
Meyer’s plan accepts that a longer response time changes the size of the fire and the room for error, so the old pattern cannot simply be run faster.
