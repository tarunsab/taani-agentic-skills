# Patterns and Techniques

## Challenge the Status Quo
**When to use**: A consensus forecast treats recent growth, inflation, and returns as the default future.
**How**: List the assumptions underneath it; compare them with long-run history; identify competing megatrends; assign probabilities to alternatives.
**Trade-offs**: Reduces blind anchoring but does not justify confident contrarian timing.

The book’s status-quo test asks whether several tectonic forces are really expected to cancel each other. Historical decades such as the 1920s, 1950s, 1990s, and 1970s show that regimes can shift rather than balance. The output is a range of scenarios, not a single replacement forecast. Use the result to stress-test a portfolio or strategy against the more probable non-consensus paths (Ch 1, Ch 7).

## Technology Radar and J-Curve
**When to use**: A new technology is popular but its economy-wide impact is unclear.
**How**: Look for company investment, hiring, diffusion, complementary infrastructure, and the growth of ideas; distinguish innovative from transformative technology; allow for a lag before GDP responds.
**Trade-offs**: Early signals can be noisy and cannot identify the exact winning application.

Electricity and the personal computer generated financial and labor-market signals before productivity visibly surged. The J-curve explains why early investment can coexist with weak measured growth, and why later applications can produce a sudden payoff. Adoption alone is not enough: economic transformation requires new opportunities or higher-value work. The radar is therefore a shape-and-scale detector, not a crystal ball (Ch 1, Ch 6).

## Tasks-Not-Jobs Matrix
**When to use**: Assessing how automation may affect an occupation.
**How**: Decompose the job into critical tasks; score each for automation, augmentation, physicality, judgment, and relationship demands; infer job outlook from the mix, not from the occupation label.
**Trade-offs**: Occupation-level averages hide individual employers, workers, and implementation constraints.

Phone operators had a short list dominated by automatable routing tasks, while fund accountants lost data entry but gained analysis and control work. Nursing and financial advice are modeled as mostly augmentative; programming as more automatable; electrical field work as minimally affected without advanced robotics. This matrix distinguishes disruption in task composition from mass unemployment and helps identify where training should move (Ch 2).

## Idea Multiplier Tracking
**When to use**: Estimating whether globalization is still a source of future productivity.
**How**: Trace patents, research papers, citations, R&D, and cross-border collaboration; count how many later useful ideas build on an originating idea; locate clusters and bottlenecks.
**Trade-offs**: Citation and patent networks are proxies, and geopolitical closure can break the exchange.

The multiplier treats ideas as reusable building blocks rather than depleted goods. The book reports a statistically meaningful relationship between a changing multiplier and later productivity, including a cited estimate that doubling it can add 1.2 percentage points to productivity growth over five years. The Wright brothers’ use of imported ornithological research and the iPhone’s distributed patent lineage show the mechanism. A rising multiplier is a leading signal, not proof that commercialization will occur (Ch 3).

## Structural-Deficit Lens
**When to use**: A debt headline is being used as a complete fiscal diagnosis.
**How**: Separate debt stock from recurring deficit flow; remove temporary war/recession effects; inspect age-related spending, interest costs, tax capacity, growth, and technology.
**Trade-offs**: Structural estimates are model-dependent and fiscal reform can change the path.

The book contrasts postwar high debt with near-zero structural deficits against the 1970s’ low debt but persistent deficits, inflation, and high rates. The useful question is why deficits rise and whether markets expect them to persist. Structural deficits can raise inflation expectations and yields and crowd out growth; a productivity boom can improve revenues and buy time. This lens avoids both complacency and mechanical debt thresholds (Ch 5, Appendix).

## Scenario-Weighted Portfolio
**When to use**: Two macroeconomic paths have material probability and opposite asset-class implications.
**How**: Define scenarios, attach probabilities, map each to growth/inflation/rates/returns, then choose modest allocation changes that narrow the outcome range.
**Trade-offs**: Downside protection costs some upside and may lag if the favored scenario dominates.

Davis’s “divide and conquer” analogy leads to more value and non-U.S. equity, plus government and corporate bonds, while retaining broad U.S. equity and low-cost funds. The goal is risk management, not tactical outperformance. In the book’s simulations, the Megatrends-Aware Portfolio lowers the probability of a negative real return in the Deficits Drag path, while giving up some upside in the AI Transforms path. The allocation is an illustrative moderate-risk example, not a personal recommendation (Ch 8, Ch 9).

## Active-Core / Passive-Satellite
**When to use**: An investor accepts active risk and can evaluate manager skill, cost, and patience.
**How**: Keep most assets in broad index funds; reserve a minority for low-cost, skilled managers; diversify across sectors and countries; assess whether the manager can find rare winners or mispriced bonds.
**Trade-offs**: Active selection can add return but raises manager, concentration, fee, and underperformance risk.

The book’s skewed-return evidence makes the index core essential because a handful of stocks account for most long-run wealth creation. Its illustrative active-passive portfolio keeps 55–65% indexed and 35–45% active, with 15–20% active equity and roughly 20–25% active bonds within the total portfolio example. No reliable quantitative screen identifies future winners in advance, so active exposure requires judgment and the ability to tolerate long lags. Active strategies are a supplement, not a substitute for diversification (Ch 8, Ch 10).
