# Chapter 2: AI and Two Simple Truths

## Core Idea

The chapter’s two truths are that jobs contain multiple tasks and that technology can improve the quality of work, not merely save time. A job disappears most readily when its small set of critical tasks is overwhelmingly automatable, as with telephone operators whose work became call routing through ACD and IVR systems. Fund accounting took the other path: data entry and routine calculation were automated, while analysis, controls, exception handling, and scale expanded, and the occupation grew. This two-dimensional automation/augmentation lens corrects the debate’s “lump of labor” assumption that the total amount of human work and human wants is fixed. Davis therefore expects AI to cause substantial disruption in task composition without implying general dystopian unemployment. His modeled examples make nursing and financial advice mostly augmentative, programming more automatable, and electrical field work minimally affected without advanced robotics. By 2035, the chapter’s estimate is an average automation rate near 20%, with more than 60% of occupations gaining augmentation and roughly 80% of jobs below 50% automation. AI arrives at a useful time because it can offset part of the labor-force drag from roughly 16 million Baby Boomer retirements, although transformation still requires new applications beyond current task assistance.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Multiple Tasks per Job**: **When to use**: estimating an occupation’s exposure to AI. **How**: list its critical tasks; a job is vulnerable when most of its value is concentrated in automatable tasks.
- **Automation versus augmentation**: **When to use**: evaluating whether technology reduces employment or raises the value of work. **How**: score each task for removal, support, or quality improvement; then infer how the role evolves.
- **Tasks, not jobs**: **When to use**: discussing AI’s labor-market impact. **How**: analyze the task mix across occupations instead of treating a job title as an indivisible unit.
- **Disruption, not dystopia**: **When to use**: communicating realistic AI consequences. **How**: allow meaningful retraining, task shifts, and some job losses while distinguishing those from mass unemployment.

## Key Concepts

- **Automation**: technology performs a task previously performed by a person.
- **Augmentation**: technology acts as a “co-pilot” that raises output quality, scale, or insight.
- **Lump-of-labor fallacy**: assuming the amount of human work and the number of desired products/problems are fixed.
- **Power tool**: technology that enables workers to serve more people or produce better outcomes.
- **Allocative efficiency**: improving the matching and flow of resources, such as beds, patients, or medications.
- **Task correlation**: similarity between generic task profiles used to infer how related occupations may be affected.
- **General-purpose technology**: technology whose effects spread into new industries and social life, not just one occupation.
- **Technical proficiency**: ability to understand and use technology while recognizing its capabilities and limits.

## Mental Models

- **Phone operator versus fund accountant**: automation can erase a role dominated by routine tasks or free a role to do higher-value work.
- **Co-pilot, not replacement**: the right comparison for many service occupations is a more powerful complement to human judgment.
- **One day per week**: a 20% average automation estimate is material task change, not a promise of a three-day weekend.
- **Occupation diagnosis**: robust job outlook can coexist with significant productivity gain when augmentation dominates.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treat automation percentage as job-loss percentage**: task exposure is not the same as occupation elimination when other tasks remain.
- **Assume emotional work is all that remains after AI**: financial advisors, for example, may use freed time for more complex planning, not merely behavioral coaching.
- **Assume technical degrees alone guarantee safety**: the chapter’s advice is continual skill development, AI fluency, critical thinking, and social/emotional capability.

## Worked Example

The fund-accounting story shows the mechanism concretely. In the 1950s, accountants collected prices, wrote them into ledgers, and calculated fund NAVs with adding machines; by the late 1980s, printouts and manual entry still consumed much of the process. Data feeds and software removed those chores, but accountants did not vanish: they moved toward controls, deeper audit research, exception investigation, and new capital-market problems. Vanguard grew from 45 accountants serving 45 funds to 75 accountants serving 500 funds, while U.S. accounting employment rose from under 400,000 in 1950 to more than 1.4 million. The same technology family eliminated most telephone-operator work because routing dominated that job’s short task list.

## Occupation Diagnosis (book’s estimates)

| Occupation | Main AI channel | Productivity impact by 2035 | Job outlook |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Nurse | EHR automation, triage, diagnostics, care augmentation | ~20% | Robust |
| Financial advisor | Administrative automation and personalized planning augmentation | ~20% | Robust |
| Computer programmer | Code generation and testing automation | ~55% | Decline or move upstream |
| Electrician | Physical, field, and relationship work beyond current AI/robotics | Minimal | Robust and in demand |

Nursing examples include up to two hours per shift less EHR entry, approximately 30 minutes per patient per shift from triage tools, and possible error reduction. Financial advice may gain 1–2 hours per day from paperwork automation and make personalized tax, spending, and asset-allocation optimization more scalable. Programmers spend about 45% of time on programming/testing; the chapter cites early productivity gains of about 26% for GitHub Copilot and 55% more lines of code for CodeFuse, while noting limited evidence and transition into data science. Electricians remain constrained by on-site fabrication, installation, repair, and coordination unless AI is joined to much more capable robotics.

## Education Guidance from the Chapter

The chapter recommends balancing passion with aptitude and market value, researching labor-market outcomes and lived work, building durable technical proficiency, cultivating social and emotional intelligence outside the classroom, and expecting lifelong learning. Passion is presented as intellectual curiosity and self-growth, not as a command to ignore pay or suitability. STEM remains valuable even if its wage premium changes, because technology fluency will be ubiquitous. The broader rule is to treat a degree as a foundation, since earnings differences accumulate through skill growth during the years after graduation.

## Key Takeaways

1. Decompose jobs into tasks before estimating AI exposure.
2. Look for augmentation as well as automation; quality and scale can rise.
3. Expect disruption in most occupations, but not automatic mass unemployment.
4. Use physicality, judgment, relationship work, and task correlation to compare occupations.
5. Treat AI as a potential demographic support, not merely a labor threat.
6. Judge transformation separately from productivity assistance.
