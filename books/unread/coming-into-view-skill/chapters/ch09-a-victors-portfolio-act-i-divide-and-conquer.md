# Chapter 9: A Victor’s Portfolio, Act I: Divide and Conquer

## Core Idea

Chapter 9 uses Nelson’s victory at the Battle of the Nile to turn the book’s two-path outlook into portfolio construction. French Admiral Brueys defended one expected approach and left his shoreward side exposed; Nelson divided his fleet and attacked from two paths, accepting controlled risk to avoid dependence on one forecast. Davis treats this as the investor’s choice between a portfolio built for a single status quo and a portfolio that recognizes both AI transformation and fiscal drag. The U.S.-only 60/40 portfolio remains a strong historical anchor, averaging about 8% nominal and 4% real annual returns since 1900, but its decade results vary sharply with megatrends. In the optimistic AI path, higher growth supports solid bonds and roughly 9–10% stock returns, while value stocks can benefit as a general-purpose technology diffuses beyond early technology leaders. In the downside path, structural deficits raise yields and bond income but weaken stock earnings and valuations; inflation shocks temporarily reduce stock/bond diversification. Davis’s illustrative `Megatrends-Aware Portfolio` modestly increases value, non-U.S. equities, government bonds, and corporate bonds, keeping broad U.S. equity at the core. Its aim is to narrow the range of outcomes and reduce downside risk, not to win every scenario or provide a free lunch.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Divide and conquer**: **When to use**: two plausible macro paths have different winners. **How**: allocate across exposures that respond to each path rather than concentrating in the currently favored one.
- **60/40 as reference anchor**: **When to use**: evaluating a long-term balanced portfolio. **How**: use the historical stock/bond mix as a benchmark, then test whether scenario probabilities justify moderate deviations.
- **Scenario-weighted allocation**: **When to use**: downside and upside risks are asymmetric. **How**: map scenario probabilities to expected asset-class returns and optimize for a narrower range, not just a higher mean.
- **Diffusion/value effect**: **When to use**: a general-purpose technology is spreading through the economy. **How**: look beyond technology leaders to lower-valued consumers of the technology and account for creative destruction among incumbents.

## Key Concepts

- **60/40 portfolio**: 60% stocks and 40% bonds, used as the book’s U.S.-only benchmark.
- **Scenario dispersion**: spread of potential portfolio outcomes, not just the average return.
- **Real return**: nominal return after inflation.
- **Earnings yield**: stock valuation measure used in the Big Four and return framework.
- **Discount rate**: rate used to value future cash flows; higher rates pressure stock prices.
- **Value stocks**: lower-relative-valuation companies that may benefit from technology diffusion.
- **Creative destruction**: new entrants and innovation eroding incumbent returns while expanding productivity.
- **Megatrends-Aware Portfolio**: diversified passive allocation designed to hedge the two central scenarios.
- **Maximum drawdown**: severe simulated or historical decline used to compare downside risk.

## Mental Models

- **Two battle lines**: one allocation can face two paths simultaneously, just as Nelson’s fleet attacked both flanks.
- **Income versus price appreciation**: bonds and value dividends can carry more of the return when valuation growth is uncertain.
- **Technology diffusion, not technology ownership**: the eventual beneficiaries of a GPT extend across industries.
- **Narrow the range**: risk management means reducing the bad outcome even if the best outcome becomes somewhat smaller.

## Anti-patterns

- **Bet the whole portfolio on U.S. growth stocks because AI is promising**: early technology leaders can be challenged as the technology diffuses and competition rises.
- **Avoid bonds because deficits may raise rates**: higher yields can eventually improve bond income and real returns, even though volatility rises during adjustment.
- **Optimize only for average return**: scenario dispersion and the probability of a negative real return matter for spending goals.
- **Call modest allocation shifts a new “sure thing”**: the portfolio deliberately accepts opportunity cost in the upside case.

## Future Market Scenarios

### AI Transforms: Smoother Sailing

The book’s simulations show solid returns for both stocks and bonds in the AI Transforms path. Bond yields rise mainly because higher trend growth raises real rates, not because fiscal inflation runs away; positive real yields resemble the 1990s more than the 1970s. Stocks center around 9–10% annual returns when real GDP is near 3%, although the valuation contribution remains a wildcard. The 1990s’ 18.2% stock return illustrates why: only 10.8 percentage points came from earnings and dividends, with the rest from valuation expansion that later reversed. If AI changes fundamentals, valuation need not revert mechanically to a long-run average. Value stocks may surprise because healthcare, finance, manufacturing, and transportation can capture the technology after the first wave of technology-company enthusiasm, while new entrants can erode incumbent returns.

### AI Disappoints, Deficits Dominate: Rougher Seas

In the downside path, bond yields rise as investors demand compensation for persistent structural deficits and inflation expectations; higher yields offer more income but bring more bond volatility. The Federal Reserve raises short-term rates to protect credibility, so the episode is not a full repeat of the 1970s, but growth is slower. Stocks face weaker earnings growth, higher discount rates, and likely falling valuation sentiment, producing a near “lost decade” through the early 2030s. Diversification between stocks and bonds is temporarily weaker when deficit-led inflation shocks move both, then can recover as monetary policy responds. The book notes that the S&P 500’s 2025 valuation assumptions required extraordinary future earnings growth; if AI fails to deliver, disappointment can compound the pressure.

## Worked Example: Allocation and Scenario Results

### Illustrative All-Passive Allocation

| Investment type | U.S.-only 60/40 | Megatrends-Aware Portfolio |
|---|---:|---:|
| U.S. broad stock market | 60% | 25–30% |
| U.S. value | 0% | 15–20% |
| U.S. growth | 0% | 0% |
| Global ex-U.S. equity | 0% | 10–20% |
| U.S. government bonds | 0% | 15–20% |
| U.S. corporate bonds | 0% | 20–30% |
| U.S. broad taxable bond market | 40% | 0–10% |
| Total equity / total bond | 60% / 40% | 50–60% / 40–50% |
| Active funds/ETFs | 0% | 0% |

The book’s modeled 2027–2037 results show the purpose of the tilt. Under `Deficits Drag` with only 10% upside probability, annual return is 3.60% and negative-real-return probability 38.20% for traditional 60/40 versus 5.00% and 19.40% for the Megatrends-Aware example. At the probability-weighted row, the figures are 6.00% and 17.70% versus 6.30% and 9.00%. Under the AI Transforms row, traditional 60/40 is 7.60% with 4.60% negative-real probability versus 7.10% and 2.60% for the aware portfolio. The illustrative maximum drawdown in the Deficits Drag scenario falls from −15.4% to −9.8%, showing the cost and benefit of the design.

## Two Ways to Stay the Course

Option A makes no allocation adjustment and is valid for investors unwilling to accept active performance risk or for those who believe current market pricing—roughly 80–90% probability of transformative AI in the book’s year-end 2024 calculation—is right. Option B accepts modest active risk in allocation and increases value and non-U.S. equity and modestly increases bonds, using low-cost index funds. International exposure reduces dependence on recent U.S. growth leadership; corporate and municipal bonds can offer a useful risk/return trade-off in either path. Neither option is a free lunch: Option B reduces downside but can give up upside, while Option A fully retains the benchmark’s existing scenario exposure.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use the 60/40 as a benchmark, then examine its scenario exposures.
2. Diversify across both paths rather than selecting one certain forecast.
3. Expect value and non-U.S. stocks to matter if technology diffuses broadly or U.S. growth disappoints.
4. Higher bond yields can be a benefit after an adjustment, not only a threat.
5. Optimize for risk and outcome range when the portfolio funds real spending goals.
6. Treat the Megatrends-Aware allocation as an illustrative, moderate-risk case.
