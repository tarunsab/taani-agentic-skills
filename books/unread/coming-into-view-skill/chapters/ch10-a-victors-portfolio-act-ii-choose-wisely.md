# Chapter 10: A Victor’s Portfolio, Act II: Choose Wisely

## Core Idea

Chapter 10 asks whether active funds and ETFs can supplement the risk-mitigating portfolio from Chapter 9. The Indiana Jones “choose wisely” scene represents both the attraction and the danger: a skilled choice can find a valuable opportunity, but a wrong concentrated choice can destroy the result. Davis’s central evidence is the skewed distribution of stock returns: a small minority of companies accounts for nearly all long-run wealth creation, making it difficult for active managers to identify the needles in advance. Broad index funds remain the core because owning the whole haystack ensures exposure to rare winners, including winners outside the United States. Active management can still have a role when managers are skilled, low-cost, diversified enough for their mandate, and held patiently through periods of underperformance. The book’s illustrative active-passive portfolio keeps 55–65% indexed and assigns 35–45% to active funds, with a minority of both equity and bond exposure active. Equity managers may find cross-sector winners; bond managers may have a somewhat less extreme selection problem and can emphasize security selection while managing macro risk. There is no reliable quantitative framework that identifies future winners with certainty, so the active allocation is a conviction-based supplement, not a replacement for indexing or a promise of excess return.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Needles in a haystack**: **When to use**: evaluating concentrated active strategies or individual security selection. **How**: estimate how skewed market outcomes are, then compare the odds of finding rare winners with the cost and concentration risk.
- **Index core**: **When to use**: the future winners are hard to identify ex ante. **How**: hold broad, low-cost funds that capture the full market and its small set of exceptional winners.
- **Choose wisely with patience**: **When to use**: adding active exposure. **How**: require evidence of skill, reasonable cost, long horizon, and willingness to endure lumpy returns.
- **Scenario-aware active selection**: **When to use**: selecting active funds within a megatrend-sensitive portfolio. **How**: ask whether a manager’s process can find AI beneficiaries, non-technology winners, or mispriced bonds under each scenario.

## Key Concepts

- **Skewed distribution**: a return pattern in which a tiny number of investments generate most of the wealth.
- **Needle**: an unusually successful stock or investment that drives a disproportionate share of market returns.
- **Haystack**: the broad market held by an index fund.
- **Active risk**: deviation from an index or strategic allocation in pursuit of excess return.
- **Manager skill**: repeatable investment judgment, not simply a past period of outperformance.
- **Concentration**: holding a small number of securities, increasing both upside and selection risk.
- **Zero-sum game**: before costs, one investor’s active outperformance corresponds to another’s underperformance; costs make the average active dollar worse.
- **Security selection**: choosing individual bonds or stocks rather than only adjusting broad market exposure.

## Mental Models

- **Own the room’s chalices**: indexing protects against missing the rare winner when the correct choice is unknowable.
- **Small denominator, large consequence**: one or two stocks can materially change a market’s return.
- **Active as satellite**: the core protects coverage; satellites express a limited, evidence-based conviction.
- **Patience as a filter**: a manager who cannot survive multi-year lag cannot realize lumpy long-term gains.

## Anti-patterns

- **Pick concentrated funds because recent returns look obvious**: the next needles are not known in advance and the distribution is extremely skewed.
- **Confuse active conviction with skill**: no reliable quantitative framework guarantees that a chosen manager will outperform.
- **Ignore costs in bonds**: the zero-sum logic applies to investment-grade corporate, municipal, and government bonds as well as stocks.
- **Replace the index core with active bets**: even skilled managers can miss the few companies that drive most wealth creation.

## Worked Example: How Rare Are the Winners?

The chapter cites research covering 29,078 publicly traded U.S. companies between December 1925 and December 2023. Fewer than 100 companies accounted for about one-third of U.S. stock-market wealth, and only roughly 4% of all stocks accounted for virtually all long-run returns above Treasury bills. More than half of stocks failed to deliver a positive lifetime return over cash, even with dividends reinvested. Globally, only about 2.4% of 64,000 companies since 1990 generated nearly all wealth created in global stock markets, reported at $75.7 trillion. This is why a broad index can outperform a concentrated active portfolio even when the active manager is intelligent: the index automatically owns the winners, while the active portfolio must identify them and avoid the losers.

| Investment type | U.S.-only 60/40 | Megatrends-Aware passive | With top-quartile, low-cost active funds |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| U.S. broad stock market | 60% | 25–30% | 20–25% index / 15–20% active |
| U.S. value | 0% | 15–20% | 10–15% index/active mix |
| Global ex-U.S. equity | 0% | 10–20% | 5–15% index/active mix |
| U.S. government bonds | 0% | 15–20% | 5–10% index / 20–25% active |
| U.S. corporate bonds | 0% | 20–30% | 10–15% index/active mix |
| Broad taxable bonds | 40% | 0–10% | 0% |
| Total passive / total active | 100% / 0% | 100% / 0% | 55–65% / 35–45% |

The table is an illustrative range, not a formula. The book’s active equity share is deliberately diversified across possible sectors rather than restricted to value, growth, or international funds. In the AI Transforms path, an active growth manager might seek early-stage winners while avoiding incumbents, while a value manager might look for AI beneficiaries outside technology; these are qualitative considerations, not quantitative solutions. For bonds, the author starts with roughly 20–25% of the total 40–50% bond allocation active, emphasizing security selection in large corporate or municipal markets and using duration or credit adjustments to manage macro risk. The index share stays high because even a skilled manager can miss the small set of next winners.

## What “Choose Wisely” Requires

The author says no reliable quantitative framework can identify outperforming managers ahead of time. His own active exposure rests on long experience, a research process combining science and judgment, low fees, a small number of managers, and the willingness to hold them long term. Active funds may lag for years before producing lumpy gains, so the investor must be able to fund goals without abandoning the process at the worst moment. Bond selection may offer more opportunities than stock selection in some markets, but it remains competitive and cost-sensitive. The disciplined conclusion is to use active funds only where the investor can explain the source of skill and accept the possibility of being wrong.

## Key Takeaways

1. Keep broad index funds as the portfolio’s core.
2. Treat active management as a concentrated search for rare winners.
3. Require skill evidence, modest cost, diversification, and patience.
4. Search across sectors and countries rather than assuming transformation winners are technology stocks.
5. Apply cost and zero-sum reasoning to active bonds too.
6. Accept that selection is a leap of faith, not a guaranteed model output.
