# Chapter 6: Megatrends Around the World

## Core Idea

Chapter 6 uses the 1904 Saint Louis World’s Fair as an early-warning analogy: the countries that visibly demonstrated electricity’s commercial uses were not guaranteed winners, but the exhibits offered clues about who was moving into the next technological phase. Davis builds a qualitative dashboard for ten large economies across AI capability, globalization/Idea Multiplier strength, population growth and aging, and projected structural deficits. The dashboard shows the United States and China far ahead in current AI depth, India strong in research and population growth, and countries such as Canada, Australia, the United Kingdom, and South Korea benefiting from strong idea exchange despite more modest direct AI investment. It also shows why a demographics-only or debt-only lens misses opportunities: Germany and Japan have headwinds but potential gains from more AI adoption, while China combines AI leadership with severe demographic and fiscal pressure. The chapter distinguishes Phase I investment and capability-building from Phase II, when complementary software, processes, and products make a technology transformative. Japan’s shift from hardware leadership to software lag during the PC revolution demonstrates that Phase I leadership need not survive the transition. The practical use is to read country prospects as a mosaic of interacting megatrends, with the United States and China likely remaining dominant while several other economies carry upside-surprise potential.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Megatrends Dashboard**: **When to use**: comparing countries under a common future technology. **How**: score AI investment/companies/research, idea exchange, population growth, aging, and structural deficits together.
- **World’s Fair signal**: **When to use**: looking for early evidence of national technological readiness. **How**: treat demonstrations and ecosystems as imperfect but informative snapshots, then test whether institutions, capital, education, and entrepreneurship can scale them.
- **Phase I to Phase II**: **When to use**: judging whether a technology will diffuse into transformation. **How**: distinguish early investment and cheaper hardware from widespread complementary applications, processes, products, and services.
- **Mosaic over single ranking**: **When to use**: a country looks strong or weak on one dimension. **How**: combine technology, ideas, demography, and fiscal capacity; look for offsetting tailwinds and headwinds.

## Key Concepts

- **AI vibrancy**: aggregate strength of a country’s AI ecosystem, including capital, companies, infrastructure, and research influence.
- **Phase I**: technology investment and capability development before broad economy-wide effects.
- **Phase II**: widespread adoption that creates new processes, products, services, and productivity gains.
- **Megatrends Dashboard**: qualitative country comparison across four major forces.
- **Idea Multiplier rank**: relative strength in generating and absorbing cross-border innovation.
- **Structural deficit proxy**: projected primary and cyclically adjusted deficit/surplus used to gauge fiscal dynamics.
- **Upside surprise**: future performance stronger than a narrow consensus or one-dimensional lens expects.
- **Home-field advantage**: local concentration of firms and institutions that can accelerate early adoption, but not guarantee later leadership.

## Mental Models

- **Exhibit floor as radar**: visible capability can foreshadow a coming phase, even when it is partly hype.
- **Hardware-to-software handoff**: leadership can move when the valuable layer of a technology changes.
- **Strength with a shadow**: the same country can lead in AI and face aging or fiscal drag.
- **Large-economy impact**: absolute capability and scale matter for global influence, not only per-person rankings.

## Worked Example: Japan’s Phase Transition

At the 1983 COMDEX “Geek Week,” more than 1,400 companies displayed PC hardware while Microsoft showed an early graphical interface. Japan was then the technology and economic superpower, with real GDP growth above 4% and formidable electronics firms; contemporary observers expected it to dominate the 1990s. The trade-show signal suggested a different next phase because the PC’s transformation would come from software and Internet-linked applications, areas where Japanese firms lagged. Japanese IT companies were reported as 40% more productive than U.S. counterparts in the mid-1980s, but 60% less productive by the mid-1990s as the valuable layer shifted. A weak banking system and “zombie” companies also mattered, so the chapter does not claim software alone caused Japan’s lost decades. It uses the episode to show that early leadership is not enough: countries must adapt from Phase I capabilities to Phase II applications.

## Limits and Application

The dashboard is a broad mosaic, not a precise country forecast. Its AI scores draw on Stanford’s Global AI Vibrancy Tool, globalization on the author’s calculations, fiscal dynamics on IMF projections, and demographics on UN and World Bank data, all with different measurement limits. Absolute-dollar measures favor large economies over smaller ones, although the chapter notes that per-capita rankings can look different. A World’s Fair can mix hype with reality, and high AI research or investment does not ensure commercialization, institutional capacity, or fiscal reform. Use the dashboard to find questions and potential surprise candidates—such as the United Kingdom, Canada, Germany, India, and Singapore—not to replace current country research.

## Key Takeaways

1. Read technology, ideas, demographics, and fiscal dynamics together.
2. Distinguish early capability from broad Phase II transformation.
3. Watch for leadership shifts when hardware gives way to software or services.
4. Treat U.S. and Chinese AI strength as real but exposed to opposing headwinds.
5. Use idea exchange as a potential upside signal for countries with less direct AI investment.
