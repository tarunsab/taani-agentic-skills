# Chapter 4: Rule 3 — Define Love Before You Think It, Feel It, or Say It

## Core Idea

The words “I love you” are emotionally important but semantically unstable: one person may mean lifelong commitment, another may mean affection, sex, friendship, or a momentary feeling. Because the phrase does not itself promise exclusivity, children, effort, or a shared future, Shetty asks readers to define love through conversations and observable behavior rather than declarations alone. He adapts the Bhakti tradition’s staged movement toward divine love into four relational phases: attraction, dreams, struggle and growth, and trust. Each phase can look and feel like love, but later phases test whether the early feeling can survive reality. The phases can recur and need not unfold in a rigid order. The practical standard of deep romantic love is liking the person’s personality, respecting their values, and wanting to help them pursue their goals while building a life together.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Four Phases

| Phase | What it supplies | What to test |
|---|---|---|
| **Attraction** | Intrigue, chemistry, and the wish to know more. | Whether interest survives learning who the person is. |
| **Dreams** | Fantasies about a shared future. | Whether dreams can become realistic rhythms, boundaries, and choices. |
| **Struggle and Growth** | Disappointments and differences that reveal compatibility. | Whether the pair will resolve, accept, or honestly leave a problem. |
| **Trust** | Safety built by repeated congruence and repair. | Physical, mental, and emotional reliability over time. |

### The Three-Date Rule

Use three intentional dates or conversations to explore, in sequence, whether you like the person’s **personality**, respect their **values**, and want to help them toward their **goals**. Date One emphasizes enjoyment, curiosity, and a mixture of light and gently revealing questions. Date Two explores how the person handles challenges, risk, decisions, pride, and meaningful experiences without turning the conversation into an interrogation. Date Three opens discussion of dreams, change, teachers, and future direction. The sequence is a focusing device, not a deadline; each dimension will appear across many interactions, and the deeper question is whether you want these qualities in your daily life, not whether the other person is identical to you.

### Reciprocal escalating self-disclosure

Vulnerability should be mutual and gradual. Share enough to invite the other person to know you, notice whether they respond with curiosity, and increase intimacy only as trust develops. Oversharing everything at once can overwhelm a person, while refusing all vulnerability keeps attraction at the level of fantasy. The reciprocal pace also reveals whether both people can listen, respect hesitation, and make room for two inner lives.

## Key Concepts

- **Attraction**: A focused desire to know a particular person, supported by reward and arousal chemistry.
- **Dreams**: Fantasies about what a relationship could become.
- **Struggle and growth**: The phase in which differences require resolution, adaptation, or a decision to leave.
- **Trust**: Gradual confidence formed through congruent thoughts, words, actions, and repair.
- **Physical trust**: Feeling safe, present, and cared for.
- **Mental trust**: Trusting the partner’s reasoning even when every decision is not shared.
- **Emotional trust**: Trusting the partner’s values and treatment of people.

## Source Examples and Evidence

Shetty uses a chair ordered online as an analogy for attraction: an object can look perfect in a picture and still be uncomfortable in reality, just as a person’s presentation does not reveal what daily partnership feels like. He cites research associating deep conversations with greater well-being and describes attraction chemistry as involving dopamine and norepinephrine, while serotonin changes contribute to early anxiety and passion. He also uses research on delayed reward, boundaries, and physical contact to caution that intense early bonding can obscure red flags. The point is not to distrust chemistry, but to allow time and observation to test what chemistry cannot tell you.

## Practical Application

Replace uncertainty with explicit, kind questions: discuss how often to talk and meet, whether each person wants exclusivity, how much space is needed, and what future aspirations matter. Build a rhythm that includes time alone, time together, time with mutual friends or family, and time with each person’s own friends; one sample week divides those categories rather than treating the partner as the only source of connection. Boundaries may concern sex, time, attention, and emotional pace. Once committed, use a monthly relationship check-in to name one highlight, one challenge, and one realistic thing to work toward together.

## Worked Example

A source-faithful first-month sequence is to begin with questions about favorite places, recurring books or films, current interests, and what the person loves to do; then explore a meaningful challenge, an unusual decision, or what they would do with enough money not to need a job; then ask about a dream, life-changing moment, or teacher. Answers are exchanged rather than collected like interview data. A sample weekly rhythm leaves one night alone, three together, two with mutual friends or family, and one with separate friends. The structure does not predict success; it creates enough time, space, and clarity for the relationship to be seen.

## Anti-patterns

- **Declaration as contract**: Treating “I love you” as proof of shared commitment without asking what it means.
- **Relationshopping**: Turning an endless checklist into a search for a perfect person who meets every need.
- **Mind-reading**: Expecting a partner to know the desired gift, amount of space, pace, or reassurance without a conversation.
- **Permanent attraction**: Repeating chemistry and avoidance of struggle instead of allowing a bond to deepen.

## Key Takeaways

1. Define love in behavior, values, goals, and agreements—not only in a phrase.
2. Use attraction as an invitation to investigate, not as proof of compatibility.
3. Prefer creating a workable life together to demanding identical values, tastes, or goals.
4. Treat trust as accumulated and repairable, but impossible to sustain through secrets, dishonesty, or gaslighting.

## Connects To

- **Rule 1**: Solitude clarifies the personality, values, and goals one brings into dating.
- **Rule 4**: Trust creates the exposure in which partners can become mutual teachers.
- **Rule 5**: Shared rhythms must leave room for each person’s purpose.
- **Rule 6**: Struggle becomes productive when the issue, not the partner, is the opponent.

## Scope & Limitations

The phases are a lens for discussion, not a diagnostic scale or a guarantee that a relationship will progress. Shetty explicitly allows different forms of love, long-distance arrangements, friends-with-benefits, and peaceful co-parenting after divorce; the model should not erase those distinctions. Similarity is not required, and the “three dates” are not a rigid dating schedule.
