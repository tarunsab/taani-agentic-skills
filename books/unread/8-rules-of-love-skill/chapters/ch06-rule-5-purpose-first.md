# Chapter 6: Rule 5 — Purpose Comes First

## Core Idea

Putting one’s purpose before a partner sounds selfish only when purpose is confused with grabbing resources or attention. Shetty uses **dharma** for the intersection of passion, skill, and service—the reason an activity matters, whether or not it is a paid job. A relationship actually contains three relationships: each person’s relationship with the other and each person’s relationship with their own purpose. If either partner abandons a calling, the resulting emptiness can become envy, dependence, or resentment, which then burdens the couple. Pursuing purpose gives a person energy, identity, and stability to bring into love, while supporting a partner’s purpose makes the relationship a cooperative “we” rather than a zero-sum contest. The rule is not “always choose work over family”; it is to protect purpose deliberately, communicate trade-offs, and revisit agreements as circumstances change.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Dharma and the four pursuits

The Vedic order is **dharma** (purpose), **artha** (work and finance), **kama** (pleasure and connection), and **moksha** (liberation and spiritual connection). Dharma comes first because it gives meaning and direction to how money, pleasure, and relationships are pursued. It can be parenting, volunteering, a creative practice, community work, or a job; the defining feature is a felt connection among talent, passion, and service. Purpose is a journey, so being actively engaged in finding and practicing it already counts as living it.

### The Pyramid of Purpose

1. **Learn** — study the interest and skills involved.
2. **Experiment** — run low-pressure tests to discover what fits.
3. **Thrive** — build consistency, routines, expertise, and measurable results.
4. **Struggle** — use obstacles, failures, and uncertainty as information and growth.
5. **Win** — celebrate outcomes without confusing the brief pinnacle with the whole journey.

The author’s Formula One calculation illustrates why winning cannot be the only reward: years of training and racing greatly outweigh minutes on a podium. If results are weak, return to learning or experimentation; do not treat a setback as proof that purpose has disappeared.

### Four ways to manage two purposes

| Strategy | Best fit and cost |
|---|---|
| **Pursue after hours** | Protect financial stability while a purpose develops gradually. |
| **Give one purpose priority** | Make an explicit, time-limited household choice when one purpose has an urgent demand; honor the sacrifice. |
| **Take turns** | Alternate periods in which one person carries more income or household work so each can pursue a demanding path. |
| **Go all in on both** | Possible when both are established and finances/household logistics can support less shared time; requires a strong “we” mindset. |

## Key Concepts

- **Dharma**: Purpose expressed through passion, capability, and service.
- **Eudaimonia**: Satisfaction associated in the book with meaning and direction.
- **Hedonia**: Satisfaction associated with pleasure, status, or personal gain.
- **Time confetti**: Small, scattered fragments of time made usable by batching tasks and removing distractions.
- **Mindful love**: Support that notices effort, adjusts agreements, and avoids coercion.

## Source Examples and Evidence

Shetty contrasts hedonia with eudaimonia research: pleasure may increase positive feeling while purpose is linked in the cited studies to better stability, health markers, and resistance to self-esteem swings from social-media likes. He tells how Sal Khan moved from tutoring a cousin to creating Khan Academy by learning, experimenting, and following the service dimension of his interest. Brigid Schulte’s time-use audit revealed 27 hours hidden in fragmented attention, enough to create longer blocks for learning. Aimee and Marco’s story shows how each person pursuing a meaningful project can reduce resentment about touring and absence, while Susanna and Graham show the long cost of silently serving another person’s purpose.

## Practical Application

Start with questions about passions, forgotten interests, hidden talents, and roles that reveal strengths such as organizer, energizer, empathizer, or analyzer. Find a mentor, ask concrete logistical and emotional questions, and convert answers into calendar actions. Use a spare-time worksheet to reallocate a consistent amount of entertainment or other non-purpose time toward learning, after discussing the plan with a partner. When a partner is searching, ask what is important, what they need, and what support is wanted; create low-risk experiments, find mentors, protect time, and celebrate small progress. Replace force or fake hype with a positive nudge, practical agreements, and a date for revisiting the plan.

## Worked Example

Keith and Andrea could not simultaneously fund two demanding pursuits while parenting, sleeping, and maintaining the household. They agreed that Andrea would spend three years completing naturopathic training while Keith worked a local teaching job and remained available for the children. Once her practice had a steady base, they shifted the arrangement so Keith could devote more time to competitive triathlon training. They later used smaller seasonal turns for continuing education and training. The example keeps the sacrifice visible, uses explicit timelines, and treats each purpose as equally important even when time is temporarily unequal.

## Anti-patterns

- **Purpose as competition**: Treating one partner’s financially larger or more advanced work as intrinsically more important.
- **Purpose abandonment**: Expecting a partner to give up their calling indefinitely and later resenting them for the resulting emptiness.
- **Control disguised as support**: Setting the partner’s goals, choosing their mentor, imposing a deadline, or doing the work for them.
- **False motivation**: Promising that everything will work immediately or using fear and guilt to drive a risky decision.
- **Uncommunicated sacrifice**: Changing household labor, money, childcare, or time without agreement and a review date.

## Key Takeaways

1. Identify the purpose that gives your work, money, and relationships meaning.
2. Follow the learning–experiment–thrive–struggle–win sequence and return to an earlier step when evidence requires it.
3. Protect each person’s purpose with explicit time, money, household, and emotional agreements.
4. Make the relationship a partnership in each other’s success rather than a demand that one person’s purpose disappear.

## Connects To

- **Rule 1**: Purpose is clarified in solitude and requires a life beyond the couple.
- **Rule 4**: The partner’s guru role is to support the other person’s path, not author it.
- **Rule 6**: Purpose conflicts become more workable when the couple can name a shared goal.
- **Rule 7**: Chronic loss of purpose or refusal to respect it may become a structural relationship rupture.

## Scope & Limitations

Shetty does not equate purpose with a career or recommend that everyone quit a secure job; the Sal Khan example is explicitly described as extreme. Financial stability, children, health, and household duties constrain the available strategies. “Purpose first” still requires presence, care, and review, and a couple may reasonably choose a temporary priority if the sacrifice is explicit, respected, and revisited.
