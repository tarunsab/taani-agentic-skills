## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Choose the Instant Pot for the gain you actually need
Use pressure cooking when a food benefits from accelerated hydration, hands-free cooking, or a long simmer compressed into a manageable cycle. The book repeatedly distinguishes “faster” from “more convenient”: the cooker still needs time to heat and depressurize, and some quick foods are better served by sautéing, steaming, or a short low-pressure cycle. Beans, lentils, sauces, rice, soups, and stews gain the most from pressure; delicate vegetables and already-cooked pasta need tighter timing. Treat the appliance as a multicooker whose useful functions include sauté, pressure cook, steam, yogurt, slow cook, rice, warm, and pot-in-pot baking.

### 2. Build flavor in stages, then finish after pressure
The recurring sequence is sauté or temper aromatics and whole spices, deglaze, add enough liquid, pressure cook, then add fragile flavor components after opening. Pressure and volume can dilute spices; resting lets flavors meld, while lemon/lime, herbs, fresh chile, cream, sugar, and garnishes restore contrast. In Indian sauces, pressure with little moisture can reproduce some roasting and caramelization through the Maillard reaction, but adding watery vegetables too early can destroy that effect. The recipes therefore separate sauce from tofu, roasted vegetables, greens, peas, cream, or garnish when texture and flavor demand it.

### 3. Control moisture to control texture and scorching
Use the recipe's liquid amount as a structural variable, not merely a hydration detail. Minimal liquid helps mushrooms and blended Indian sauces roast; enough liquid is essential for sealing; overly thick tomato or nut-butter mixtures can trigger “Burn.” Sautéed browned bits must be loosened before closing the lid, and the hot inner pot should be removed after cooking rice, pasta, sauces, or desserts so residual heat does not overcook or scorch the food. If a result is too thin, reduce on Sauté; if too thick, loosen with water, stock, or nondairy milk.

### 4. Match release method to the food
Natural release protects beans, lentils, soups, sauces, grains, and foods whose starch or liquid may spray through the valve. Quick release protects fast-cooking vegetables and other delicate ingredients from overcooking, but is risky for very liquid-heavy pots. Combination release—rest naturally for a specified interval, then vent—balances grain fluffiness and vegetable texture. Never open the lid until the float valve has dropped; “L” on the display is elapsed time, not proof that the pot is safe to open.

### 5. Use pot-in-pot to make a complete spread
PIP means a heatproof insert sits on a trivet above water or another food so multiple components steam at once. It is the book's answer to making rice above dal, cashews above a sauce, a frittata above mushrooms, or potatoes above a stew. Choose the insert material deliberately: stainless steel heats quickly, while glass or stoneware is gentler for some batters and potatoes; leave clearance around the insert and keep it below the lid. If the correct insert is unavailable, cook the components separately with the main dish's listed time.

### 6. Build vegan texture with neutral, modular ingredients
Cashew cream, nondairy yogurt, blended tofu, pumpkin seeds, coconut cream, starches, chickpea flour, lentils, mushrooms, jackfruit, soy curls, and seitan each solve different texture problems. Cashews supply neutral creaminess; seeds or tofu make useful nut-free substitutes; chickpea flour sets into a sliceable tofu and a firm egg-like batter; mushrooms and jackfruit provide savory bulk; lentils and beans supply body and protein. The substitutions are not always flavor-neutral: coconut can change the expected profile, coconut aminos differ from soy sauce in larger amounts, and the recipe notes decide which swap is most reliable.

### 7. Adjust for dietary needs without abandoning the flavor architecture
For soy-free cooking, replace tofu with chickpea tofu, beans, chickpeas, or vegetables; replace soy sauce with coconut aminos when it is a minor component. For gluten-free cooking, use tamari, gluten-free pasta/bread, rice or oat flour, and omit seitan where required. For nut-free creaminess, use pumpkin or hemp seeds, nondairy yogurt, blended tofu/beans, or coconut cream according to the recipe. For oil-free savory cooking, sauté with stock or dry-toast whole spices; baked goods can use applesauce, aquafaba, or nut butter, but the book cautions that oil sometimes carries tempering flavors.
