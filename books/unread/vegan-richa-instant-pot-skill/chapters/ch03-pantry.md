# Chapter 3: Pantry: Stocking Your Kitchen

## Core Idea

The pantry chapter treats accessibility and authenticity as compatible goals. Traditional spices create the intended regional flavor, but substitutions keep a recipe usable when an ingredient is unavailable; the author encourages finding the traditional item later rather than abandoning the dish. Dried legumes cost more time because they need soaking and pressure cooking, while cooked legumes can be frozen in roughly 1½-cup portions for up to three months. The most useful pantry is organized by function: legumes for body and protein, flours and starches for binding, grains for a base, fresh aromatics and greens for brightness, nuts/seeds and nondairy ingredients for creaminess, and spice blends for regional direction. Ingredient differences matter: chickpea flour takes more liquid than besan, white and brown basmati behave differently, tomato products vary in thickness, and soy sauce/tamari vary in salt and flavor. The chapter also emphasizes freshness and handling—whole spices last longer than ground ones, flour should be spooned and leveled, and savory nondairy milk should be plain and unsweetened.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Function-first stocking**: choose a legume, grain, aromatic, thickener, creamy ingredient, acid, and spice blend before chasing every specialty item.
- **Authenticity with a fallback**: use the named regional spice when possible; otherwise use the recipe's supported substitute and adjust heat/salt at the end.
- **Ingredient-role substitution**: replace flour by liquid behavior, a nut by creamy/binding capacity, and a soy sauce by salty-umami function while checking flavor changes.

## Key Concepts

- **Besan/gram flour**: flour from skinned brown chickpeas; nuttier and usually more finely ground than white chickpea flour.
- **Chickpea flour**: flour from white garbanzo beans; often needs more water than besan for the same batter consistency.
- **Basmati rice**: the book's default rice for many Indian meals, with brown basmati as a grain alternative.
- **Nutritional yeast**: inactive yeast used for cheeselike flavor.
- **Soy curls**: boiled/dehydrated soy pieces that rehydrate into a stringy chicken substitute.
- **Miso**: fermented soybean paste that supplies umami; chickpea miso is the soy-free option.
- **Kala namak**: Indian black salt used for eggy flavor.
- **Kasuri methi**: dried fenugreek leaves used for a sweet-bitter savory finish.
- **Amchoor**: dried mango powder used for sourness.
- **Paanch phoron**: Bengali five-spice blend built around mustard, cumin, fennel, nigella, and fenugreek.

## Pantry Map

| Need | Reliable book staples | Practical substitutions/limits |
|---|---|---|
| Protein/body | black beans, chickpeas, kidney beans, lentils, mung beans, split peas | cannellini/navy/pinto/black-eyed peas often interchange; timing still changes |
| Grain/base | white or brown basmati, quinoa, oats | quinoa can replace rice in many serving contexts, but not always with the same liquid |
| Binding/thickening | chickpea/besan, rice flour, cornstarch, tapioca, potato starch, arrowroot | flour choice affects texture; measure by spooning and leveling |
| Creaminess | cashews, almond flour, yogurt, coconut milk, tofu, pumpkin/hemp seeds | coconut can add a noticeable flavor; check the recipe note |
| Umami/acid | miso, soy sauce/tamari, vegan Worcestershire, tomato paste, vinegars, tamarind | coconut aminos work best when soy sauce is not the dominant flavor |
| Fresh contrast | ginger, garlic, chile, tomatoes, lemon/lime, cilantro, mint, spinach/chard | fresh herbs may be garnish but can be structurally important in regional dishes |

## Worked Example

To stock for a week of recipes, freeze cooked legumes in recipe-sized portions, keep white basmati and quinoa for quick grain bases, and prepare one neutral cream plus one spice direction. A jar of garam masala can move from an Indian curry to roasted vegetables, soup, or even cookies; a jar of barbecue or taco seasoning can move from sauce to lentil filling. This is the chapter's practical idea of a pantry: a small set of cross-compatible components that preserves the recipe's flavor architecture while reducing last-minute shopping.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treat all curry powder as authentic Indian seasoning**: the author distinguishes generic British/Western curry powder from garam masala and recipe-specific spices.
- **Swap coconut aminos for a large amount of soy sauce without checking**: the flavor changes when soy sauce is a major component.
- **Use sweet/flavored nondairy milk in savory food**: it can distort the intended seasoning.
- **Assume all chickpea flours hydrate alike**: besan and white chickpea flour differ in grind and liquid need.

## Key Takeaways

1. Stock by ingredient function and regional flavor direction.
2. Freeze cooked legumes in portions that match the recipes.
3. Keep whole spices or small amounts of fresh ground spices for better flavor.
4. Read each recipe's substitution note when changing flour, cream, soy sauce, or tomato product.
