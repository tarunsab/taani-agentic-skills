# Chapter 9: Curries & Stews

## Core Idea

This chapter deliberately leaves Indian curry behind and uses the Instant Pot to translate global curry and stew traditions into flexible vegan meals. The common thread is not a single spice blend but a base of aromatics, a concentrated seasoning profile, a legume or plant protein, and vegetables chosen for the pressure window. Some recipes are brothy, some are thickened with flour or blended beans, and some rely on coconut milk, nut butter, or a post-cook puree for body. The cooker supplies the long simmer that makes tagine, red beans, or étouffée cohesive, while a short sauté or reduction protects the identity of the roux, nut butter, or fresh greens. The variations are substantive: Japanese curry powder is milder and sweeter than Indian curry powder, Jamaican curry can be homemade from everyday spices, and Cajun seasoning controls both heat and salt.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Cuisine translation by function**: identify the local role of roux, curry powder, mole, nut butter, or Cajun seasoning, then reproduce its flavor and thickening function with vegan ingredients.
- **Long-cook versus quick-finish separation**: pressure-cook beans, lentils, or hardy vegetables; add peas, greens, coconut/nut butter, lime, and herbs later when they are fragile or likely to scorch.
- **Blend-to-thicken**: puree part of a curry or beans after pressure to create a sauce without adding more starch or cream.
- **Scorch-risk management**: dense flour or nut-butter mixtures need attention, adequate liquid, and sometimes staged addition; scraping the bottom is part of the recipe.
- **Serving-context contrast**: rice, plantains, bread, lime, pickles, and fresh herbs are not decorative extras but balance the stew’s salt, heat, and richness.

## Recipe Cards

### Japanese Curry

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free and gluten-free options. Sauté onion, ginger, and garlic, push them aside, and make a quick roux with oil and flour before stirring in mild Japanese curry powder and optional turmeric. Add carrot, potato, chickpeas, tomato paste/ketchup, Worcestershire or soy sauce, water, and salt; high pressure for 6 minutes (7 for large pieces) with natural release cooks the vegetables, then applesauce/grated apple and peas go in on Sauté. Blend ½ cup of the curry if a thicker roux-like body is wanted, and serve over rice with sesame, pickled radish/ginger, and scallions. Rice flour and tamari make it gluten-free, coconut aminos make it soy-free, and soy curls, vegan chicken, or seitan can replace chickpeas subject to their dietary constraints.

### Cannellini & Lentil Jamaican Curry

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Sauté onion, garlic, and chile, bloom Jamaican curry powder and turmeric, then add red lentils, canned cannellini/chickpeas/kidney beans, coconut milk or stock, water, and salt. High pressure for 3 minutes with natural release is enough for the lentils; add chopped spinach afterward and simmer on Sauté for 2–3 minutes. Lemon, cilantro, cayenne, sugar, and additional water let the same dish move from a thick curry to a soup. The homemade Jamaican blend combines coriander, cumin, mustard, onion powder, thyme, parsley, cayenne, black pepper, allspice, cinnamon, nutmeg/clove, and ginger; Scotch bonnet or habanero controls the heat.

### Chickpea Sweet Potato Spinach Curry

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Toast cumin, then sauté onion, ginger, garlic, coriander, garam masala/curry powder, turmeric, cinnamon, pepper, and cayenne before cooking down tomato into a saucy base. Add cooked chickpeas, sweet potato or pumpkin, ¾ cup water, and salt; high pressure for 10 minutes with natural release softens the root and integrates the spices. Fold in spinach, lemon, and optional coconut milk, coconut cream, nondairy creamer, or cashew cream on Sauté. Berbere, Cajun seasoning, or another blend can replace garam masala, and other beans/vegetables can substitute while preserving the brothy everyday character.

### Lentil Tagine Instant Pot

**Yield:** 2 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Sauté garlic, ginger, onion, carrot, cumin, coriander, turmeric, cayenne, black pepper, cinnamon, salt, and bay leaves, then stir in brown/green lentils, tomato, butternut squash/sweet potato/eggplant/zucchini, spinach, and 1¾ cups water. High pressure for 18 minutes with natural release makes a sweet-savory Moroccan-style stew without a separate earthenware tagine. Taste after opening and finish with cilantro and lemon. The vegetable assortment is interchangeable, but it should be cut to a size that can tolerate the lentil timing; soft greens are included in the pressure phase here because the dish is intentionally stew-like.

### Corn Chowder

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Sauté garlic, onion, and celery, add fresh or thawed frozen corn, then potatoes, stock, nondairy milk, celery salt, and black pepper. High pressure for 12 minutes with 5 minutes natural release, followed by blending half the soup, creates a chowder that is creamy without requiring flour. Let it sit for 10 minutes, add red pepper flakes, and finish with green onion, jalapeño, charred corn, lime, vegan Cheddar/nutritional yeast, or corn chips. For a richer version, steam ½ cup cashews above the soup, blend them with part or all of the chowder, and use Sauté for 4–6 minutes to thicken.

### Étouffée

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, with a gluten-free option. Drain and shred young jackfruit, then make a brown roux in coconut oil and flour for 5–7 minutes before blooming Cajun/Creole seasoning, thyme, and paprika. Add garlic, onion, bell pepper, celery, jackfruit, thick mushrooms, crushed tomato, bay, stock, and salt, scrape the base, and pressure cook at high for 30 minutes; the source’s written method vents after the cycle rather than specifying a natural-release interval. Taste, add optional Old Bay, and finish with parsley and lemon over rice. Beans make it heartier, hearts of palm/soy curls/more mushroom replace jackfruit, and rice flour plus oat flour provide the gluten-free roux; the decisive limitation is that roux color and Cajun seasoning carry much of the flavor, so undercooking either yields a flat stew.

### Cajun-Style Red Beans & Rice

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Sauté onion and garlic, then bloom bay leaves, Cajun seasoning, hot sauce, thyme, smoked paprika, optional liquid smoke, onion powder, salt, and pepper before adding celery and red/green bell pepper. Add soaked small red beans or kidney beans and 2 cups stock/water, then cook at high pressure for 35 minutes for small red beans or 45 for kidney beans with natural release. Sample the beans, re-cook 10–15 minutes if necessary, blend about one cup to thicken, and simmer briefly with adjusted Cajun seasoning before serving over rice. The recipe distinguishes small Louisiana red beans from kidney beans, suggests vegan sausage, and uses pickled jalapeño, parsley, green onion, and hot sauce as fresh counterpoints.

### West African–Inspired Peanut Stew

**Yield:** 6 servings; soy-free and nut-free options. Sauté onion and Scotch bonnet/habanero, puree tomato, garlic, ginger, tomato paste, cumin, coriander, and pepper, then add part of the peanut/almond butter, roasted peanuts, soy curls or chickpeas/lentils, vegetables, stock, salt, and lime. Pressure cook high for 12 minutes for soy curls or 8 for lentils/chickpeas, release naturally, fold in the remaining nut butter, and simmer spinach briefly on Sauté. The staged nut-butter method is critical: a full dense quantity can congeal at the bottom and trigger Burn, while a small amount flavors the pressure phase and the rest melts into the finished stew. Sunflower-seed butter plus sunflower/pumpkin seeds makes it nut-free, and the stew is served with rice, cilantro, and more lime.

## Worked Example

Translate a new curry by separating three questions: what supplies the dish’s signature aroma, what ingredient sets the pressure time, and what should be added after pressure for contrast. For Japanese curry, the answer is a flour-and-curry roux, potatoes/carrot/chickpeas, and apple/peas; for Jamaican curry, it is curry spices, red lentils/beans, and fresh greens/lemon. For a long red-bean or étouffée cook, protect quick herbs and garnish while letting the beans or jackfruit become tender, then blend a portion if body is lacking. Nut butter and flour are special cases because they can scorch, so their quantity and placement are part of the safety design rather than optional stylistic details.

## Anti-patterns

- **Use a generic curry time for every cuisine**: legumes, roux, potatoes, and wild vegetables have different pressure requirements.
- **Put all nut butter at the bottom before pressure**: stage part early and fold the rest in after opening.
- **Skip the roux or seasoning bloom in étouffée/Japanese curry**: the pressure cycle cannot recover that missing toasted flavor.
- **Trust bean timing without sampling**: kidney and red-bean age, soak, and variety affect tenderness.
- **Add delicate greens at the beginning by habit**: most of these recipes use a post-cook fold or short Sauté wilt.

## Key Takeaways

1. Global curries are modular systems of aromatics, signature seasoning, pressure-tolerant solids, and fresh finishes.
2. Thickening can come from roux, blended beans/vegetables, lentils, coconut, or nut/seed butter—but each has different scorch risk.
3. Pressure depth and fresh garnish should be designed together.
4. When adapting, set the time by the slowest legume or root, not by the name of the curry.
5. Rice, plantains, pickles, lime, herbs, and hot sauce complete the flavor balance.
