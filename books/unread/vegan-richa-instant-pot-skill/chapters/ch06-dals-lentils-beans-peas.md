# Chapter 6: Dals, Lentils, Beans & Peas

## Core Idea

In this section, “dal” names both a family of lentils and the finished preparation, so the recipes range from brothy everyday soups to one-pot rice meals and bean curries. The shared architecture is simple: choose a legume whose cooking time matches the vegetables, build flavor with a tempering or masala, add enough water for pressure, and choose a release that preserves the intended texture. Dals generally retain enough spice flavor that plain water works; cream is optional rather than a requirement of Indian home cooking. Whole spices, ground spices, the timing of garlic/onion, and a finishing tadka can make the same lentil feel like a different dish. The section also makes the pressure cooker a fermentation and sprouting appliance, not only a pressure vessel, and uses PIP to make a complete lentil, vegetable, and rice meal together.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Dal fry versus dal tadka**: fry the spiced base before the lentils, or cook the dal separately and pour a fresh whole-spice tempering over it at the end.
- **Legume-to-texture matching**: use short-cooking red lentils for quick curries, soaked whole beans for long cooks, and longer pressure time when a porridge or melt-in-the-mouth texture is wanted.
- **Tempering as contrast**: toasted seeds, garlic, onion, chile, and fresh herbs added at different stages provide aromatic lift without needing a richer liquid.
- **Layered one-pot meal**: put dal in the main pot and rice or squash in covered inserts when pressure times overlap.
- **Yogurt-function transformation**: controlled warm incubation ferments idli/dosa batter and encourages sprouts, with ventilation and hygiene treated as part of the method.

## Recipe Cards

### Dal Fry (Spiced Indian Lentil Soup with Rice)

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Temper mustard and cumin, then cook hing, onion, ginger, garlic, chile, coriander, turmeric, cayenne, tomato, lemon, and a spoonful of water until the mixture is saucy. Reserve about half the tomato base, add the remaining water and a split red/yellow lentil mix, and place soaked basmati with 1¼ cups water in a foil-covered tall-trivet dish above it; high pressure for 10 minutes followed by 10 minutes of natural release cooks both components. Fold the reserved base and cilantro into the dal, reduce briefly on Sauté if it is thin, and correct with lemon and salt. Coconut milk can replace ½–1 cup of the dal water for a richer version, but the recipe’s everyday style does not require cream.

### Mung Dal Kitchari (Split Mung Bean & Rice Porridge)

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Rinse and soak rice and petite yellow or red lentils separately while sautéing cumin or mustard, onion, garlic, chile, ginger, bay/curry leaves, coriander, cumin, garam masala, turmeric, cayenne, tomato, and vegetables such as cauliflower, carrots, beans, peas, potatoes, or squash. Add 2 cups water and the soaked grains, then pressure cook at high for 4–6 minutes for a porridge texture or at low for 3 minutes for rice that remains distinct; let pressure release naturally for 5 minutes. Finish with cilantro and lemon, adding up to 2½ cups water for a saucier dish. The brown-rice/green-mung variation uses 3½ cups water and 20 minutes, with coconut or cashew cream optional after cooking.

### Lobia Pulao (Black-Eyed Pea Rice Pulao)

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free and gluten-free, with a nut-free option. Soak black-eyed peas and brown basmati separately, then toast mustard, cumin, cloves, and peanuts or cashews before adding onion, ginger, garlic, chile, tomato, bell pepper, turmeric, coriander, garam masala or biryani masala, and cayenne. Add the drained legumes/rice and 1¾ cups water, then cook at high pressure for 18–20 minutes and release naturally. Lemon, cilantro, and red onion provide the fresh finish, with pappadums or kachumber as suggested accompaniments. Sunflower seeds or roasted chickpeas replace nuts; green mung, adzuki beans, lentils, small kidney beans, or non-Indian blends such as Cajun or Berbere can change the profile, though the substitute must tolerate the same cook time.

### Dhan Sak (Lentil Vegetable Stew with Caramelized Onion)

**Yield:** 2 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Combine mixed split lentils, cauliflower/eggplant/zucchini/squash or other vegetables, greens, ginger, garlic, chile, turmeric, salt, dhania-jeera powder, and 2½ cups water, then pressure cook at high for 3 minutes and release naturally. Separately toast mustard and cumin, then cook a full cup of onion with garlic and a pinch of salt until golden; the well-caramelized onion is the recipe’s main flavor payoff. Mash the dal and vegetables for a traditional texture or leave them chunky, stir in half the tempering, and garnish with the rest, lemon, and cilantro. Dhania-jeera powder is made by toasting coriander and cumin seeds, grinding them, and adding pinches of cinnamon, clove, and black pepper; it can also be used as a finishing dust.

### Black-Eyed Pea & Mung Bean Stew

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Soak black-eyed peas and green mung beans for at least two hours or overnight, then temper cumin, bay, dried red chile, onion, ginger, and garlic before adding coriander, garam masala, turmeric, tomato, optional celery, salt, and water. The beans cook together at high pressure for 20 minutes, followed by 10 minutes of natural release and a careful vent; the mung beans soften faster and give the stew a creamy background. Adjust salt and spice after opening and garnish with cilantro, serving it with rice, flatbread, or crackers. The author’s substitutions include black beans, brown/green lentils, adzuki beans, split peas, chickpeas, or other beans, but the cooking time should follow the slowest legume.

### Kadala Curry (Brown Chickpea Coconut Curry)

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Soak brown chickpeas overnight, then pressure cook them with water until tender, about 40 minutes at high pressure with a natural release; the author notes that older chickpeas may need longer. Toast grated coconut with coriander, cumin, fennel, fenugreek, mustard, red chile, cinnamon, cloves, cardamom, pepper, and coconut oil, blend the mixture with water, and reserve part of the masala. Temper mustard, curry leaves, hing, onion, garlic, ginger, tomato, turmeric, cayenne, and salt, add the cooked chickpeas and blended sauce, and pressure cook again for 5 minutes before adding the reserved portion and more coconut. A final tempering of mustard, dried chile, and curry leaves gives the curry its South Indian aromatic edge; serve with rice, appam-style bread, or flatbread.

### Sambar (South Indian Split Pea Stew)

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Pressure cook toor dal or yellow mung with turmeric, salt, water, and vegetables such as eggplant, carrots, okra, squash, or drumstick until the lentils are soft; the book gives about 10 minutes for pigeon peas/yellow mung and about 20 for split peas, with natural release. Make the flavor base with oil, mustard and fenugreek, dried red chile, curry leaves, onion, garlic, ginger, tomato, sambar powder, and tamarind, then combine it with the cooked dal. Mash or blend part of the mixture for body while leaving vegetables visible, and finish with cilantro and lime. Sambar powder can be homemade from toasted coriander, cumin, chana dal, urad dal, dried red chile, fenugreek, pepper, cinnamon, cardamom, and cloves; a store blend is the practical shortcut.

### Paanch Phoron Dal (Indian Five-Spice Split Pea Soup)

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Cook split chickpeas or split peas with turmeric, salt, chopped potato or zucchini, water, and a five-spice tempering of mustard, cumin, fennel, nigella, and fenugreek; toasted coconut and red chile deepen the Bengali-inspired profile. The pressure time is about 14–16 minutes at high with natural release, or up to 18 minutes when the split peas are old or a softer soup is desired. Mash part of the dal and vegetables, then finish with the remaining tempering, lemon, cilantro, and optional coconut. If paanch phoron is unavailable, the book gives a homemade equal-part seed blend and allows the dal/vegetable balance to move toward a thicker curry or a thinner soup.

### Chickpeas with Coriander, Black Pepper & Chile Masala Sauce

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Soak chickpeas overnight and make a masala from coriander, cumin, mustard, fenugreek, whole and coarsely ground black pepper, red chile, cardamom, cinnamon, turmeric, paprika, and salt. Sauté the spice mix with curry leaves, onion, ginger, garlic, and tomato, then layer the soaked chickpeas on top with water rather than stirring a thick tomato mixture into the bottom. Cook at high pressure for about 45 minutes and release naturally, adding lemon, salt, and more pepper after tasting; older chickpeas may need another 10 minutes. The sauce is intentionally peppery and Madras-like, and it can be served over rice or used as a filling with flatbread; a little mashed chickpea thickens it without cream.

### Shakkarkand Wali Masoor Dal (Sweet Potato & Lentil Curry)

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Sauté onion, ginger, garlic, chile, turmeric, garam masala, cumin/coriander, and tomato, then add eggplant or another hearty vegetable, cubed sweet potato, soaked brown or green lentils, and 2 cups water. High pressure for 11–12 minutes with natural release softens both the lentils and sweet potato, after which spinach is folded in and lemon or cayenne adjusts the finish. Add water or nondairy milk if the consistency is too thick; extra cooking produces a creamier lentil base. The recipe explicitly supports oil-free onion cooking in water and substitutions with Berbere, Baharat, harissa, or Jamaican curry blends.

### Red Lentil Butternut Squash Curry

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Temper mustard, then cook onion, ginger, garlic, chile, turmeric, garam masala, black pepper/cayenne, and tomato with water before adding cubed butternut squash, coconut milk, salt, and split red lentils. Pressure cook at high for 3 minutes and quick-release immediately so the squash stays recognizable; fold in spinach once the lid is open. Lime, red pepper flakes, and cilantro cut through the coconut richness, while water instead of coconut milk makes the dish more dal-like. Green or brown lentils need 10–11 minutes, and doubling the recipe calls for 1¼ cups water rather than a simple 2x liquid scale.

### Sabut Masoor, Lauki & Chawal (PIP Brown Lentils, Squash & Rice)

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Build a tomato-onion masala for soaked brown lentils in the main pot, reserve a little of the onion mixture for bottle gourd/summer squash, and put rinsed basmati with water, salt, and oil in a foil-covered stacking container above it. Place the squash container under the rice container on a trivet and cook the complete stack at high pressure for 19 minutes with natural release. Fluff rice first, check the squash, and serve the three components together with cilantro and lemon; the squash can finish on a skillet if it remains firm. If only rice or squash is being made, the remaining single insert is covered and pressure-cooked with the lentils, preserving the same modular architecture.

### Golden Spinach Dal (Onion- & Garlic-Free)

**Yield:** 4 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Temper mustard, cumin, nigella, optional fennel, and fenugreek in oil, then briefly bloom turmeric and cayenne before adding split red lentils or petite yellow mung, salt, and water. Cook red lentils at high pressure for 6 minutes, or mung dal for 10, and release naturally; fold in mature spinach or quick-cooking greens after opening and use Sauté for about 2 minutes if needed. The recipe’s allium-free flavor depends on nigella’s onion-like note and fenugreek’s garlic-like note rather than a direct substitute. A chopped tomato may be added with the greens, and the finished dal can be eaten as soup or spooned over rice.

### Lentil or Bean Sprouts

**Yield:** 2–3 cups; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Rinse and sort mung beans, lentils, black-eyed peas, chickpeas, or black beans, soak them in hot water overnight, drain, and place them in a steamer basket over 1 cup water. Remove the sealing ring to prevent odor, leave the lid vented, and use Yogurt for 12 hours; another 8 hours on the counter or in the pot extends the sprout length, with rinsing every 8–12 hours. Refrigerate the finished sprouts for up to four days and rinse before eating or using them in dal. Kidney beans are specifically excluded, and the recipe treats sprouting as a moist, ventilated process rather than as pressure cooking.

### Sprouted Mung Bean or Lentil Breakfast

**Yield:** 2 servings; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Bloom cumin or coriander, turmeric, cayenne, and hing, then soften onion and tomato before adding sprouted mung/lentils, salt, and water. Cook at high pressure for 2 minutes for mung or lentil sprouts, or 6–8 minutes for chickpea/other bean sprouts, then release naturally for 5 minutes before venting. Serve the lightly spiced sprouts over vegan-buttered toast with cilantro, red onion, and lemon; cucumber, apple, tomato, chaat masala, black pepper, or vegan cheese can change the breakfast format. The short cook preserves some bite, which is the point of steaming sprouts instead of turning them into a smooth dal.

### Idli & Dosa Batter

**Yield:** about 12 idlis; soy-free, nut-free, gluten-free. Soak husked urad dal, mung dal, and fenugreek for at least six hours, soak idli or short/medium-grain rice separately, then blend the dal fluffy and the rice to a slightly gritty paste before combining by hand. Ferment the mixed batter under a glass lid on Yogurt for 12–14 hours until light and bubbly, then add salt and refrigerate up to a week. Steam filled idli plates over 1½ cups water for 14 minutes, or use a foil-covered oiled cake pan for 20 minutes; for dosas, spread ¼–⅓ cup batter on an oiled hot nonstick skillet and cook until the edges release and brown. If urad or fenugreek is unavailable, another split lentil plus a pinch of active dry yeast is the fallback, but colder rooms and over-fermentation remain texture variables.

## Worked Example

Use Sabut Masoor, Lauki & Chawal as the section’s complete-meal pattern. Start a brown-lentil tomato masala in the main pot, reserve a small portion to season the squash, and stack rice above squash on a tall trivet so three textures finish at the same time. The 19-minute high-pressure cycle is chosen for the brown lentil rather than for the rice, so the rice is protected by its covered insert and the squash is kept separate from the liquid. After natural release, fluff the rice, check the vegetable, and plate all three with lemon and cilantro. This same reasoning lets a dal carry a PIP rice or vegetable side whenever the pressure-time window is compatible, but delicate vegetables should not be placed in the main pot for the full bean timing.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treat every legume as interchangeable**: use soaking, age, and the slowest ingredient to set the cook time.
- **Mix a thick tomato masala into the bottom without deglazing**: layer chickpeas on top when a recipe calls for it and scrape the pot before sealing.
- **Use quick release for every dal**: red-lentil squash benefits from quick release, while beans, brown lentils, and many brothy dals need natural release for texture.
- **Assume cream is required**: most everyday dals are designed for water; add coconut or cashew only when the desired richness calls for it.
- **Ferment or sprout with a sealed, scented ring**: vent the lid, remove the ring where directed, and rinse sprouts regularly.

## Key Takeaways

1. Dal is a flexible preparation whose texture and flavor are controlled by legume choice, tempering order, water, and release method.
2. Use PIP when the main legume’s pressure time can carry a covered rice or vegetable insert.
3. Let whole spices and finishing tadkas provide aromatic contrast even in water-based dishes.
4. For beans, test tenderness after the natural release and allow for older or unsoaked legumes.
5. Treat fermentation, sprouting, and hygiene as separate process controls from pressure cooking.
