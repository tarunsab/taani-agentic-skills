# Epilogue: The Price of the Acquisition

## Core Idea

After Systrom and Krieger left, Instagram continued to grow, monetize, and absorb Facebook’s measurement culture while presenting itself as a separate experience. Hiding like counts was framed as relief from competition, but professional accounts and analytics kept users inside the same system of performance and benchmarking. The company expanded messaging, recommendations, ads, and notifications, while regulators reconsidered whether Facebook’s purchase of Instagram had removed a major competitor. Frier’s closing argument is that technology is not a neutral amplifier: defaults, metrics, ranking, verification, and moderation determine which behaviors become easy and profitable. Harms involving self-harm content, drug sales, scams, misinformation, and dangerous influence were often handled reactively because their absence was hard to count, even as revenue made the acquisition look increasingly successful. The price of the deal was therefore borne less by the buyers and sellers than by users living inside a system that made personal visibility a business model.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Remove the visible metric, inspect the incentive** — Hiding likes may reduce comparison in the interface without changing the underlying status economy.
- **Technology is designed** — Every default and measurement rule distributes attention, agency, and risk; “amplification” does not remove responsibility.
- **Measure what should not happen** — Safety and well-being require proxy measures, qualitative review, and proactive ownership because absence is difficult to observe.
- **Evaluate acquisition externalities** — Include user-level effects and lost alternatives when judging a deal that increases corporate scale.

## Key Concepts

- Hidden like counts
- Professional accounts and analytics

- User cost of platform power

## Mental Models

1. **The incentive iceberg:** visible interface change above the water; ranking, analytics, ad economics, and corporate goals below it.
2. **The absence problem:** harm prevented is hard to report, so organizations tend to prioritize measurable growth over unobserved safety.

## Anti-patterns

- Celebrating a metric change as a well-being intervention without testing behavior and emotional outcomes.
- Treating scale as evidence of neutrality or inevitability.
- Using statistical rarity to dismiss harms that are individually severe or systematically concentrated.
- Reviewing a platform acquisition only through buyer and seller gains.

## Worked Example

Instagram’s test of hiding likes reduced the public visibility of one comparison signal, but the platform simultaneously encouraged users to convert to professional accounts with richer analytics. A user could see fewer likes while receiving more information about reach, audience, and performance, preserving the logic of self-optimization in a different form. The contrast captures the epilogue’s argument: changing what users can see is not the same as changing what the system rewards. The same company that questioned whether it was good for people remained dependent on the attention and commercial activity generated by the system.

## Distinctions

Removing a visible metric is different from changing the ranking, analytics, and commercial rewards underneath it. Calling technology an amplifier is different from recognizing that designers choose the defaults that determine what gets amplified. Revenue success is different from user welfare, and the two can move together or in opposite directions.

## Limitations

The epilogue evaluates the consequences of a long development through the period covered by the book; later policy or product changes are outside its evidence. It presents serious cases to show the cost of scale, but those cases do not quantify every user’s experience. The acquisition critique is an interpretation of tradeoffs, not proof that one alternative corporate history would have avoided all harms.

## Practical Application

For a well-being intervention, measure behavior and outcomes before and after the visible change, including whether users simply migrate to professional analytics or other status signals. Review ownership of ranking, recommendations, moderation, and monetization together because harm can move between surfaces. Include users’ lost alternatives and uncounted costs in platform or acquisition evaluations.

## Key Takeaways

- Interface relief is not incentive reform.
- Platform design decisions create social and political consequences at scale.
- Safety needs authority and measurement even when the desired result is an event that never occurs.
- Acquisitions can create value for founders and shareholders while narrowing users’ alternatives.
- The final test of a social platform is not only whether it grows, but what kind of behavior its growth makes normal.
