# Chapter 5: Move Fast and Break Things

## Core Idea

Facebook’s operating culture made growth a near-moral objective: instrument every action, run experiments, and keep whatever increased a target metric. Instagram’s founders valued a different hierarchy—community first, simplicity, and inspiration—and worried that Facebook would turn a crafted visual product into another engagement machine. The integration therefore became a series of boundary negotiations over terms of service, moderation, advertising, links, hiring, and competitive launches. Facebook supplied scale and intelligence, but its contractors and growth teams could also flatten the human context that made Instagram trusted. Instagram responded by using its original strengths—fast, visually coherent products, careful advertising, and relationships—to launch video, ads, and other features that defended the product against Vine and Snapchat. The chapter’s mechanism is a recurring tradeoff: borrowed infrastructure accelerates a product, while borrowed incentives can quietly redefine what success means.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Mission boundary inside a parent company** — Translate a product’s values into explicit decision rules before the parent’s defaults become the operating system.
- **Manual-first monetization** — Use high-touch review to protect a premium experience while learning what advertisers and users will accept.
- **Competitive response through the user need** — Match a rival’s underlying behavior, not just its visible interface, and ship at a speed the market can absorb.
- **Liaison as cultural infrastructure** — A trusted bridge can resolve ambiguity across companies, but cannot replace clear authority.

## Key Concepts

- Facebook’s A/B-testing and growth culture

## Mental Models

1. **The metric substitution:** a parent’s measurement system enters a subsidiary, then the measured behavior becomes the de facto mission.
2. **The resource-and-rule bargain:** every shared capability buys speed but also imports someone else’s defaults, incentives, and dependencies.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating an apology as a substitute for explaining who owns user content and what the policy actually permits.
- Scaling moderation through distance without preserving escalation paths and local context.
- Letting the parent’s growth requests become a roadmap without testing whether they serve the product’s job.
- Assuming competition requires copying a feature immediately rather than identifying the social pressure that made the feature attractive.

## Worked Example

When Instagram launched 15-second video in six weeks, it answered Vine’s creative format with a product that preserved Instagram’s filters, feed, and existing audience. The launch reached a million videos quickly and gave Vine’s creators a reason to move. Later, the first Michael Kors ad was reviewed manually and tuned for visual quality; the early test suggested that a better-looking ad could increase advertiser spending rather than merely constrain it. These launches show how a small team used speed and craft together, while the terms-of-service episode shows the cost when the parent’s legal language outran the subsidiary’s relationship with users.

## Distinctions

“Move fast” can mean shortening a learning loop, while Facebook’s slogan also tolerated breaking things as a cost of metric improvement. Shared infrastructure is distinct from shared culture: the former can increase reliability, while the latter can replace a product’s values. Manual-first monetization is likewise a learning stage, not a promise that every ad can be reviewed forever.

## Limitations

Instagram could move quickly because its team was small, its product surface was focused, and Facebook supplied distribution and infrastructure. The same response may fail where video, moderation, or legal risk is more complex. The chapter’s examples show tradeoffs rather than a universal rule that speed or human review always wins.

## Practical Application

Separate a product’s non-negotiable values from the parent or platform capabilities it may borrow. For a new monetization surface, review early examples by hand, define quality and disclosure criteria, and automate only after measuring both advertiser demand and user trust. For a competitive launch, set a short experiment window while preserving the core interaction that existing users understand.

## Key Takeaways

- Values matter only when they affect product, policy, and resource decisions.
- A parent company can provide leverage without being a neutral partner.
- Manual review is expensive but can establish a quality bar and a learning loop for later automation.
- Competition is often won by making an adjacent behavior feel native to the existing community.
- Data about competitors can improve strategy while also encouraging a culture of permanent defensive maneuvering.
