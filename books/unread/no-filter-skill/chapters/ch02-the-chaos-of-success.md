# Chapter 2: The Chaos of Success

## Core Idea

The first crisis at Instagram was not lack of demand but the operational and social responsibility created by sudden demand. Krieger and Systrom had to turn a tiny team, fragile servers, and informal community practices into a company without losing the feeling that users were participating in a shared creative space. They split responsibilities according to complementary strengths: Systrom represented the product and its public aesthetic, while Krieger focused on infrastructure and reliability. Community staff such as Joshua Riedel and Jessica Zollman made a deliberate choice to model and curate constructive behavior, answer difficult users, and handle abuse rather than treat the feed as a purely mechanical output. The chapter’s lesson is that a community is a product surface: it needs taste, moderation, rituals, and trusted people, not just servers and growth charts.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Build the community, not just the app** — When user meaning and norms create retention, invest in the people and rituals that make those norms visible.
- **Default good, intervene on harm** — Encourage constructive behavior through curation and respond personally where automation or legal abstractions are insufficient.
- **Complementary founder partnership** — Divide leadership by real strengths and trust the other person’s domain instead of duplicating authority.
- **Selective participation over indiscriminate virality** — A product can grow by showing users what good participation looks like rather than maximizing every possible reshare.

## Key Concepts

- Infrastructure overload and support debt
- InstaMeets and community ambassadors
- Community evangelism and translation volunteers
- Suggested users as a high-leverage editorial surface
- Human moderation and Section 230 boundary questions
- Child-abuse evidence handling and law-enforcement liaison
- Celebrity attention without direct content payments
- Brand participation that blends into the community
- Community as a defensible asset

## Mental Models

1. **The norm-setting loop:** model behavior → reward visibility → users imitate it → the community becomes easier to govern.
2. **The trust stack:** reliable infrastructure + humane support + predictable norms + attribution for creators.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming a free product has no safety or support obligations because users are not paying.
- Outsourcing every difficult judgment to a legal minimum or an automated classifier.
- Buying celebrity activity so early that the product becomes a performance for money rather than a place people want to use.
- Scaling a reshare feature before understanding whether users want discovery, authorship, or personal connection.

## Worked Example

When Justin Bieber’s arrival overloaded the servers, the episode made Instagram’s reach obvious, but the company still refused to pay him to post. The team wanted celebrities to use the same product as ordinary people, with behind-the-scenes material rather than advertisements inserted as a separate class of content. Zollman’s work showed the harder side of scale: the community team dealt with abuse, self-harm, and illegal material while also organizing InstaMeets and helping users find their voice. The combination of refusal to purchase attention and willingness to spend human effort on community norms helped Instagram feel culturally coherent during its fastest growth.

## Distinctions

Community work here includes both positive norm-setting and difficult safety intervention; it is not the same as a marketing campaign. Curation is a way to model behavior, while moderation is a way to limit or respond to harm, and the two need different escalation paths. The founders’ refusal to pay for celebrity posts also differs from later relationship-based support: access and guidance can be incentives even when cash is not.

## Limitations

The early team could personally know important users and respond to edge cases because Instagram was still small by later standards. That model becomes expensive and selective as the community grows, and the book does not present human curation as a complete substitute for scalable safety systems. The reported cases are vivid examples, not a measurement of all moderation outcomes.

## Practical Application

Define the community behaviors a product wants to make normal, then give them visible examples, rituals, attribution, and support. Create a separate path for abuse and illegal-content escalation, including human review where context matters. Track response quality and creator trust alongside acquisition and engagement so community labor is not treated as invisible overhead.

## Key Takeaways

- Community quality is an operating capability, not a slogan.
- Curation can be an early form of product design and a way to teach users what belongs.
- Human judgment is expensive, but it often carries the trust that makes automated scale tolerable.
- The creator’s relationship to a post matters: attribution and authorship can be more valuable than raw virality.
- Complementary leadership is a scaling asset only when the partners preserve mutual trust.
