# Chapter 6: Domination

## Core Idea

Instagram and Facebook were not just different products; they expressed two different theories of competition. Systrom pursued craft, a coherent aesthetic, and a community of people whose behavior could teach the product what it was. Zuckerberg pursued strategic control: if a product could threaten Facebook’s time, data, or network, Facebook should build it, buy it, or make it less relevant. That doctrine explains the fast response to Vine, the attempts to buy or copy Snapchat, and the use of Onavo data to understand traffic between competing services. Instagram’s growth and monetization choices—video, ads, rectangular images, and selected Facebook requests—were negotiated inside that larger contest. The chapter’s limitation is that defensive competition can protect users from a rival in the short term while normalizing a market structure in which the largest platform decides which alternatives are allowed to mature.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Domination doctrine** — In a platform market, treat an emerging habit as strategically important before it looks like a revenue line.
- **Copy, acquire, or contain** — A dominant company can respond to a rival by reproducing the feature, purchasing the team, or limiting the rival’s access to distribution.
- **Earn the right to monetize** — Build enough user trust and frequency before adding ads, then use quality to protect the brand.
- **Jobs before interface** — When responding to a competitor, identify the user job—private relief, creative expression, discovery—not only the surface feature.

## Mental Models

1. **The threat clock:** user habit → network formation → attention displacement → strategic threat; revenue may arrive last.
2. **The platform control triangle:** build the substitute, own the substitute, or shape the distribution around the substitute.

## Anti-patterns

- Reducing “competition” to a feature checklist and missing the emotional problem a rival solves.
- Treating acquisition as proof that the market is open while the buyer simultaneously controls distribution and information.
- Adding monetization before the product has earned tolerance for commercial interruptions.
- Using competitive intelligence only to react, never to ask what the product itself should become.

## Worked Example

Snapchat’s disappearing media addressed a pressure Instagram had helped create: public posts were becoming too polished and permanent. Facebook first built Poke, then pursued an acquisition and copied elements of Snapchat when the deal failed. Instagram eventually launched Stories, acknowledging that the behavior was valuable even though the feature looked like a rival’s. The decision worked because the company combined the new behavior with Instagram’s existing network and celebrity relationships; it also showed how a dominant platform can absorb a competitor’s insight after resisting the competitor’s culture.

## Distinctions

Responding to a user job is different from reproducing a rival’s surface: Snapchat’s insight was relief from permanence, not simply a camera screen with a timer. A product can win a feature battle while the parent company increases concentration, so user benefit and market structure must be assessed separately. Competitive intelligence can reveal traffic patterns, but it is not the same as asking users what they need.

## Limitations

The domination framework describes Facebook’s strategic posture in the period covered by the book; it is not a complete theory of antitrust or innovation. The narrative cannot show what would have happened if Facebook had not copied, acquired, or constrained a rival. The success of Stories also does not prove that every imitation is defensible or that every rejected acquisition would have improved the market.

## Practical Application

When a competitor grows, write down the emotional or functional job behind its feature and the incumbent’s own strengths that should remain intact. Compare build, buy, and distribution responses, including effects on choice and independent innovation. Require a review that asks whether the move protects users, protects a network, or merely protects the dominant firm’s control.

## Key Takeaways

- Zuckerberg’s competitive philosophy treats emerging user behavior as a strategic asset or threat.
- Systrom’s quality philosophy can produce durable differentiation, but it is slower and more resource-intensive.
- The best competitive responses preserve the underlying job while adapting the product’s own language.
- Ads were more acceptable after Instagram had user scale and a visual quality standard.
- A company can win a feature battle and still lose independence if every important choice is made defensively.
