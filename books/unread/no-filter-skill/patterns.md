# Patterns

## Simplest Useful Version

**When to use**: A product idea combines several plausible behaviors but users are not repeating the whole bundle.

**How**: Observe which action users return to, remove adjacent features, and make the core loop fast and legible. Add constraints that improve the result or lower the embarrassment of participation. Re-test the reduced product with people who do not already understand the founder’s vision.

**Trade-offs**: Narrow scope can create clarity and speed, but it may hide future use cases or make the company dependent on a single behavior. Instagram’s reduction from Burbn worked because photographs were universal and existing networks supplied distribution; the pattern is not a license to ignore adjacent user needs forever.

## Constraint-Led Design

**When to use**: Users face too many choices, inconsistent quality, or a high social cost for creating something.

**How**: Choose a visible boundary—such as a square frame, a short video length, or a temporary post—and make the boundary produce a coherent result. Pair it with tools that lower the cost of trying, such as filters or fast loading. Treat the constraint as part of the product promise, not merely as an engineering limitation.

**Trade-offs**: Constraints create taste and reduce decision load, but can become precious doctrine. Instagram eventually accepted rectangular photographs and Stories when evidence showed that the original boundaries were blocking useful behavior.

## Seed Model Users

**When to use**: A new social product needs norms and examples before it has enough activity to feel alive.

**How**: Invite people with existing taste, networks, or community credibility. Give them a product that makes the desired behavior easy, then let their examples teach later users what belongs. Measure whether the seed group creates imitation and retention, not only launch publicity.

**Trade-offs**: Early tastemakers can establish trust quickly, but their preferences may become a gate. Instagram’s celebrities, photographers, and suggested-user lists created legitimacy and careers while also concentrating opportunity in editorial decisions.

## Community-First Curation

**When to use**: The product’s value depends on users believing that the space has constructive norms and worthwhile people.

**How**: Combine human selection, community rituals, support, and clear attribution. Use staff to model good participation and to handle edge cases that automation cannot understand. Preserve paths for users to report harm and for local context to reach decision-makers.

**Trade-offs**: Curation builds trust but does not scale cheaply or neutrally. As Instagram grew, its one-to-millions staffing ratio made exceptions difficult, and recommendations that felt editorial also became economic distribution.

## Manual-First Monetization

**When to use**: A young platform needs revenue without damaging the aesthetic or trust that drove adoption.

**How**: Wait for sufficient user value, review early commercial content by hand, and set a quality bar visible to both advertisers and users. Use early campaigns as experiments about whether quality increases demand, then automate only the parts that can preserve the learned standard. Keep commercial labels clear even when the creative is native to the feed.

**Trade-offs**: Manual review limits volume and can frustrate sales teams, while automation can lower quality or obscure responsibility. Instagram’s early ads showed that visual quality could increase spending, but later scale pressures made enforcement and disclosure harder.

## Copy, Acquire, or Contain

**When to use**: A rival product is creating a habit that could alter attention, identity, or network formation.

**How**: Identify the underlying user job, estimate the rival’s network trajectory, and choose among building a native version, acquiring the capability, or shaping distribution around it. Preserve the incumbent product’s strengths instead of importing a rival’s surface without context. Review whether the response protects users or merely protects market control.

**Trade-offs**: Fast imitation can keep users from leaving, but repeated copying reduces independent innovation and concentrates power. Instagram’s response to Snapchat succeeded as a product move while illustrating Facebook’s broader domination doctrine.

## Metric-to-Incentive Audit

**When to use**: A metric is being used to rank users, allocate money, set a roadmap, or define success.

**How**: Name the metric, identify who can optimize it, list cheap ways to game it, and trace the behavior it will make more common. Compare the metric with outcomes the product actually values, including trust, contribution, quality, and safety. Re-audit after each ranking, verification, or monetization change.

**Trade-offs**: Metrics create focus and make learning possible, but they simplify human outcomes and can reward the wrong proxy. Follower, like, comment, and engagement signals helped build Instagram’s economy while also encouraging bots, staged lives, and undisclosed advertising.

## Acquisition Gradient

**When to use**: A startup is negotiating or evaluating integration with a larger platform.

**How**: Map control over infrastructure, hiring, roadmap, navigation, data, integrity, and succession. Treat each shared capability as both a resource and an imported default. Put independence promises into decision rights and escalation mechanisms rather than relying on cultural goodwill.

**Trade-offs**: Shared systems reduce fragility and accelerate growth, but each step can make the product easier to absorb. Instagram gained infrastructure and reach while gradually losing control over staffing, integrity, and the meaning of independence.

## Proactive Integrity Ownership

**When to use**: Harms are severe, distributed, or difficult to count because success means that an event did not happen.

**How**: Give the product a named owner, dedicated capacity, escalation paths, and qualitative review alongside automated detection. Track leading indicators such as exposure pathways, response quality, and user trust, not only removals or engagement. Revisit the design when a feature creates a new abuse surface.

**Trade-offs**: This work competes with visible growth and may not produce an attractive dashboard, but reactive cleanup is more expensive and less credible. The book shows how Instagram’s distinct risks repeatedly lost priority inside Facebook’s centralized integrity model.
