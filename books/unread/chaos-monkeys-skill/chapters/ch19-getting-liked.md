# Chapter 19 — Getting Liked

## Thesis

In a competitive acquisition, carefully placed evidence of rival interest can
change the buyer's urgency, but the signal works only when timing and
credibility are controlled.

## Core Idea

The author meets Facebook's Ads group and demonstrates GrokBar, then implies
that Google may also be interested even though the backchannel is uncertain.
Because the Facebook contact recently left Google, he may not know what Google
is doing, making the suggestion more effective. Meanwhile Sacca resists
accepting Twitter's low initial number, and the founders keep both processes
alive. Facebook responds with intensive interviews on a short clock, turning
the acquisition from a casual conversation into a competitive decision. The
chapter presents negotiation as an information game: a buyer's fear of losing
the target can matter as much as the target's intrinsic product value. It also
shows the ethical edge—strategic ambiguity can create leverage, but a false
claim can destroy trust or trigger liability.

## Frameworks

- **Competitive-signal loop:** credible alternative → buyer urgency → faster
  diligence → improved terms or certainty.
- **Information asymmetry:** a counterparty's incomplete knowledge can shift the
  bargaining range, but only while the claim remains defensible.

## Key Concepts

- Rival-interest signaling
- Acquisition urgency
- Interview blitz
- Negotiation credibility

## Source Examples / Evidence

- A Facebook demo is followed by a prompt for daylong interviews within 48
  hours, a sharp change from exploratory contact.
- The author uses the possibility of Google interest to make Facebook treat the
  process as competitive.

## Distinctions

- **Signal vs. lie:** a true but selectively framed fact is different from
  inventing a bid.
- **Urgency vs. panic:** faster diligence can improve a process; rushed signing
  can worsen terms.
- **Buyer interest vs. buyer commitment:** an interview request is evidence of
  priority, not a purchase agreement.

## Limitations

The chapter is narrated by the person who benefited from the tactic and does
not reveal every counterparty's information. Negotiation behavior must respect
confidentiality, securities, fiduciary, and employment obligations.

## Practical Application

Maintain a verified deal-status ledger. When signaling competition, state only
what can be supported, separate confirmed interest from speculation, and ask
whether the buyer's next action improves price, certainty, or employee outcome.

## Worked Example

Two enterprise buyers are evaluating a startup. One knows the other has asked
for a technical diligence session, but not whether an offer exists. The founder
can truthfully say that there is a second active process and set a diligence
deadline, rather than claim a fictitious price. The buyer must then reveal its
own seriousness through a term sheet or accept losing priority.

## Key Takeaways

- Competitive tension is created through credible alternatives.
- Timing can convert a passive buyer into an active one.
- Strategic ambiguity has value only inside ethical and factual boundaries.
