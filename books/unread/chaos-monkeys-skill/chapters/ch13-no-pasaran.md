# Chapter 13 — ¡No Pasarán!

## Thesis

When litigation threatens runway, financing becomes a coalition-building
exercise: capital, counsel, investors, and commercial partners must be aligned
against the opponent's ability to wait the startup out.

## Core Idea

AdGrok's lawsuit makes investors hesitate and forces the founders to quantify a
previously vague "death clock." The author models legal costs over roughly a
year and a half, then admits that optimistic projections are being used to make
the company financeable despite the danger. Russell Siegelman leads a round at
a lower valuation cap, while Chris Sacca and other investors add enough cash to
extend the runway. Fenwick's willingness to accept a legal advance or equity
keeps the defense alive, and YC's network supplies a second form of leverage:
Paul Graham can threaten to make hostile venture firms less welcome in future YC
deal flow. Sam Altman's help connecting the issue to Microsoft's business
relationship creates an additional pressure point. The chapter frames founder
grit as two skills—obsessive focus and the ability to endure sustained
discomfort—while also showing that endurance works best when converted into a
structured coalition.

## Frameworks

- **Death-clock model:** runway equals cash divided by operating plus legal burn;
  update it under base, optimistic, and adversarial assumptions.
- **Reward friends, punish enemies:** ecosystems enforce norms through future
  access, not only through formal contracts.

## Key Concepts

- Litigation-adjusted runway
- Valuation cap negotiation
- Equity-for-services
- YC gatekeeping
- Coalition leverage

## Source Examples / Evidence

- The author estimates approximately $1.5 million in total legal cost over an
  18-month horizon and models when cash would run out.
- YC pressure reaches both the incumbent's venture backers and a Microsoft
  relationship important to the incumbent's survival.

## Distinctions

- **Forecasting vs. fundraising narrative:** a model can reveal likely death,
  while a pitch may emphasize the scenario in which the company survives.
- **Grit vs. denial:** endurance requires facing the burn rate; pretending the
  risk is absent is not resilience.
- **Network pressure vs. extortion:** legitimate ecosystem consequences should
  be factual and proportionate.

## Limitations

The chapter openly admits strategic optimism in its projections, so readers
should treat the numbers as a historical decision artifact, not a forecasting
template. Threatening access to a network can damage trust if the facts or
proportionality are wrong; legal and ethical review matters.

## Practical Application

Build a weekly litigation-adjusted cash model, separate legal from product
spend, and define a minimum financing threshold. Give each ally a concrete role:
capital, counsel, customer validation, reputational signaling, or access to a
decision-maker.

## Connections

The underlying legal asymmetry is introduced in [12 — Turning and Turning in
the Widening Gyre](ch12-turning-and-turning-in-the-widening-gyre.md), while
founder endurance is revisited in [15 — Victory](ch15-victory.md).

## Worked Example

If a startup has nine months of product runway but only four months once legal
fees are included, it must either raise enough to cover both or settle on terms
that preserve the business. A board packet should show the cost of waiting,
the cost of settlement, and the specific commercial relationship that could
change the opponent's incentive.

## Key Takeaways

- Legal risk belongs in the runway model from day one.
- Small companies gain power by coordinating people who control dependencies.
- Grit is a practiced operating behavior, not a romantic personality trait.
