# Chapter 37 — When the Flying Saucers Fail to Appear

## Thesis

An organization can protect a failed strategic theory through cognitive
dissonance, weak measurement, and political commitment, especially when the
theory promises a new revenue source.

## Core Idea

The author uses a classic cognitive-dissonance story to interpret Facebook's
Sponsored Stories and Open Graph experiments. Facebook's News Feed is treated
as sacred, while agencies buy Likes and struggle to prove business value;
Open Graph then asks outside companies to integrate and share data in exchange
for distribution. Sponsored Stories promise that social context will make ads
more effective, but the claimed lift is small when compared with ordinary
display benchmarks and partners do not see a compelling operational benefit.
Rather than abandoning the theory, Facebook pressures partners, expands the
program, and frames objections as a failure to understand the future. An
internal revenue target remains unmet, and later leadership acknowledges that
Likes alone will not sustain monetization. The chapter shows how a narrative
can survive contrary evidence when the institution has already invested status,
resources, and identity in it.

## Frameworks

- **Dissonance loop:** public prediction → weak evidence → reinterpretation or
  forced adoption → more sunk cost → stronger defense of the prediction.
- **Partner-miracle test:** list the external integrations and behavior changes
  required before treating a platform strategy as viable.

## Key Concepts

- Sponsored Stories
- Open Graph
- Social context
- CTR lift
- Cognitive dissonance

## Source Examples / Evidence

- The chapter compares a social-ad clickthrough lift from roughly 0.05% to 0.08%
  with ordinary display ranges that can be higher.
- Facebook expects a meaningful revenue contribution, but the partner and
  advertiser behavior needed to create it does not materialize.

## Distinctions

- **Statistical lift vs. economic lift:** a relative improvement can remain
  weak when the base rate is tiny.
- **Adoption vs. coercion:** partners integrating because a platform forces them
  is not proof that the product creates value.
- **Narrative persistence vs. evidence:** continued investment may reflect sunk
  cost or politics rather than new learning.

## Limitations

The chapter is a retrospective critique and does not reproduce all experiments,
segments, creative differences, or measurement assumptions. CTR is only one
outcome; brand effects, retention, and long-run value could differ.

## Practical Application

Before scaling a platform feature, state the base rate, absolute lift, partner
work required, adoption threshold, and kill criterion. Give independent teams
permission to challenge the narrative before the investment becomes identity.

## Worked Example

A publisher predicts that social endorsement will double ad performance. A
controlled test compares absolute conversions, not only relative CTR, and
includes partner integration cost. If the lift is small and adoption is forced,
the team should revise the hypothesis rather than multiply the rollout.

## Key Takeaways

- A small percentage lift can be economically weak.
- Partner behavior is a product requirement, not a footnote.
- Sunk-cost narratives need explicit kill criteria.
