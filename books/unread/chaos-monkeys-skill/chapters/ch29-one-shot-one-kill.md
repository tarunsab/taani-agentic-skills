# Chapter 29 — One Shot, One Kill

## Thesis

Scale raises the threshold for meaningful product impact, and a giant social
dataset can be an impressive record of behavior without being a useful record
of purchase intent.

## Core Idea

Facebook's data resembles a transcript of countless conversations, but much of
it is sarcastic, ambiguous, multilingual, or unrelated to a buying decision.
At Facebook's size, an experiment must move a large absolute amount of revenue
to matter—roughly a five-percent change in the chapter's illustrative framing,
or about $100 million on a $2 billion base. Small niche targeting ideas can be
interesting yet economically invisible, while a broad platform change can have
enough surface area to matter. The PM therefore acts like a portfolio manager,
allocating scarce engineering attention among uncertain bets under a high
impact bar. This creates a paradox: scale provides distribution and data, but it
also makes small improvements hard to notice and raises the cost of false
confidence.

## Frameworks

- **Absolute-impact threshold:** evaluate percentage lift together with the
  revenue or user base to which it applies.
- **Portfolio allocation:** fund a mix of high-confidence incremental work and
  fewer high-upside bets, with explicit stop rules.

## Key Concepts

- Commercial intent
- Scale threshold
- Niche vs. platform bets
- PM as portfolio manager
- Signal-to-noise ratio

## Source Examples / Evidence

- The chapter describes social text as rich in context but weak in direct buying
  intent.
- It uses a multi-billion-dollar revenue base to show why a five-percent change
  becomes the practical threshold for a meaningful business result.

## Distinctions

- **User scale vs. experiment sensitivity:** a large population does not ensure
  a small effect is strategically important.
- **Behavioral relevance vs. commercial intent:** talking about a topic is not
  the same as preparing to purchase.
- **Statistical significance vs. business significance:** a tiny lift can be
  real yet not worth the engineering and organizational cost.

## Limitations

The five-percent and $100 million figures are explanatory estimates, not a
universal decision rule. Large platforms can benefit from many small gains when
they compound or improve strategic positioning.

## Practical Application

For every experiment, state the minimum detectable effect, the absolute value if
it appears, and the decision it would change. Avoid promoting a statistically
interesting signal when the total economic impact cannot clear the investment
threshold.

## Worked Example

A new audience segment increases clickthrough by 2% but contributes only
$200,000 annually against a $100 million engineering program. It may still be
worth shipping if nearly free or strategically useful, but the decision should
not be justified by the percentage alone.

## Key Takeaways

- Large organizations need absolute-impact thinking.
- Social context is not the same as purchase intent.
- Portfolio discipline prevents every interesting signal from becoming a roadmap.
