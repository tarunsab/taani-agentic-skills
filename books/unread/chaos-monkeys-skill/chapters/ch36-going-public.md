# Chapter 36 — Going Public

## Thesis

An IPO converts a private mission-driven company into a public asset with a
visible price, exposing wealth stratification and forcing employees to relate
their identity to a market narrative.

## Core Idea

Facebook's IPO is accompanied by a mission speech and a strong sense of
corporate ritual, but the author reads the event through the tension between
collective purpose and private ownership. Early employees and founders hold
different economic positions from later hires, while a new class of wealthy
employees makes money talk socially taboo. The company lacks some ordinary
retirement benefits, so the IPO becomes a perceived pension for workers whose
wealth remains tied to Facebook stock. Managers and institutional structures
often align with owners even when employees are invited to identify with the
mission. Going public therefore changes not only liquidity but the moral and
social meaning of work: the company's public price becomes a daily judgment on
the labor and future of everyone inside it.

## Frameworks

- **Mission–ownership split:** compare the public story of collective purpose
  with who owns, controls, and can sell the underlying asset.
- **Wealth-layer model:** separate founders, early employees, later hires,
  managers, and public investors when analyzing incentives.

## Key Concepts

- Initial public offering
- Employee equity
- New-rich class
- Pension-by-stock
- Mission rhetoric

## Source Examples / Evidence

- The IPO creates a public stock price and makes wealth differences between
  early and late employees more visible.
- Employees are described as treating the stock outcome as a retirement-like
  resource in the absence of comparable benefits.

## Distinctions

- **Liquidity vs. wealth:** a quoted price is not the same as cash after lockup,
  tax, and volatility.
- **Mission membership vs. ownership:** identifying with a company's purpose
  does not provide equal control or economic upside.
- **Public narrative vs. private distribution:** celebration can coexist with
  uneven gains.

## Limitations

The chapter is a critical personal interpretation of Facebook's IPO culture,
not a cap-table or labor-economics study. Employee benefits and tax treatment
are historical and should not be generalized to all public companies.

## Practical Application

When evaluating equity compensation, model ownership layer, vesting, lockup,
tax, liquidity, and downside separately from salary and mission fit. For leaders,
make the distribution and risks of the IPO legible rather than relying on
celebratory language.

## Worked Example

Two employees each receive 10,000 shares, but one joined early with a low tax
cost and the other receives restricted stock near the IPO with a lockup. The
same quoted price creates different realized wealth; a fair comparison must
include basis, vesting, taxes, and sale window.

## Key Takeaways

- An IPO changes the organization's social and incentive system.
- Market price is a public narrative as well as a financial number.
- Mission language does not remove ownership asymmetry.
