# Chapter 42 — Initial Public Offering: A Reevaluation

## Thesis

The conventional IPO success story is misleading: a first-day price jump often
means the issuer underpriced shares and transferred value to banks and their
clients.

## Core Idea

The chapter rejects headlines calling Facebook's flat first day a failure and
explains how an IPO syndicate sets and distributes the initial price. Under a
firm commitment, banks take execution risk, but they also have incentives to
price the offering conservatively and create a visible opening “pop.” A normal
exchange opening uses order flow and liquidity to discover a price without
trying to manufacture a 20–30% jump, so the difference is an incentive problem,
not a law of nature. Facebook opens around $42 after being offered at $38 and
closes near the offer price, suggesting that the company extracted more value
from the syndicate and accepted less spectacle. The author's lesson is a
counterintuitive reading rule: a dramatic first-day gain can be a transfer from
founders and employees to bankers and wealthy clients, while a flat debut can
mean the issuer priced closer to fair market value.

## Frameworks

- **Issuer–bank incentive map:** compare the issuer's desire for proceeds with
  the bank's desire to reduce placement risk and reward clients.
- **IPO signal inversion:** interpret a first-day “pop” as possible underpricing,
  not automatic proof of demand or quality.

## Key Concepts

- IPO syndicate
- Firm commitment
- Best effort
- Opening pop
- Market price discovery
- Underpricing

## Source Examples / Evidence

- The chapter contrasts normal stock openings with the frequent IPO pattern of
  large first-day gains.
- Facebook's offering is described as opening above the offer and closing close
  to it, while paying a comparatively low syndicate fee.

## Distinctions

- **Demand vs. manufactured scarcity:** a price jump can reflect allocation and
  underpricing rather than unexpected public enthusiasm.
- **Issuer success vs. bank success:** a low-risk, high-pop placement may be
  good for the syndicate but costly for the company.
- **Opening price vs. long-term value:** first-day trading says little about
  durable performance.

## Limitations

The chapter is a polemic from a former banker and does not model every auction,
allocation, stabilization, or regulatory constraint. IPO outcomes vary by
market and period; the heuristic should prompt analysis, not replace it.

## Practical Application

When reviewing an offering, ask who sets the range, who receives allocations,
what price would maximize issuer proceeds, and what lockup or stabilization
rules apply. Judge success by proceeds, dilution, liquidity, and long-term
market quality rather than the opening-day headline.

## Worked Example

An issuer sells 10 million shares at $20 and the stock opens at $26. The
apparent success may mean $60 million of proceeds were left on the table. A
board should compare that cost with the value of certainty, broad ownership,
and stable trading rather than celebrating the percentage increase alone.

## Key Takeaways

- A first-day pop may indicate issuer underpricing.
- The party celebrating the IPO may not be the party capturing the value.
- Market mechanics and incentives matter more than headlines.
