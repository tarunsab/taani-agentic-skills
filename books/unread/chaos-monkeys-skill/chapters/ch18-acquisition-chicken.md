# Chapter 18 — Acquisition Chicken

## Thesis

An acquisition process is an auction only if the seller preserves optionality;
signing exclusivity too early lets the buyer set the price while founders and
investors may value the same exit very differently.

## Core Idea

Twitter initially offers roughly $5 million for AdGrok, but does not provide a
term sheet, leaving the founders uncertain whether the number is a real bid or
an anchoring maneuver. An adviser recommends creating an auction by contacting
Google, Microsoft, Facebook, and other possible buyers before accepting a
no-shop clause. The author treats a no-shop provision as a freeze on the
seller's leverage: once signed, alternative conversations become harder or
forbidden. The process also exposes a mismatch in return expectations—an angel
may accept a modest multiple while a venture fund needs a much larger outcome
to make its portfolio math work. The chapter's acquisition strategy is thus a
game of timing, credible alternatives, and incentive alignment rather than a
simple question of who offers the most flattering headline price.

## Frameworks

- **Optionality premium:** maintain multiple credible buyers until price,
  certainty, and terms justify exclusivity.
- **Portfolio-return lens:** evaluate an offer from each holder's cost basis,
  ownership, liquidity, and fund-level return requirement.

## Key Concepts

- No-shop clause
- Acquisition auction
- Anchor price
- Investor return multiple
- Fiduciary duty

## Source Examples / Evidence

- Twitter's initial $5 million indication is below what the author believes a
  competitive process could produce, and no term sheet accompanies it.
- Potential buyers include Google, Microsoft, and Facebook, with Facebook
  approached through a business-development contact.

## Distinctions

- **Indication vs. offer:** a verbal number without terms is not a binding bid.
- **Headline price vs. allocation:** cash, stock, retention, vesting, and
  investor payout can make equal totals economically different.
- **Founder value vs. fund value:** the same exit may be life-changing for a
  founder and immaterial to a venture portfolio.

## Limitations

The tactic can harm trust, violate fiduciary or contractual duties, and consume
management attention. The chapter reports a specific 2010–11 process; current
deal law and market practice require counsel.

## Practical Application

Before signing exclusivity, define a walk-away value, closing-certainty value,
employee-retention value, and investor minimum. Run parallel conversations only
when authorized, confidential, and genuinely credible; otherwise the auction is
bluff rather than leverage.

## Worked Example

A buyer proposes $8 million verbally and asks for 60 days of exclusivity. The
founders should ask for a term sheet with consideration type, vesting, escrow,
employee offers, investor treatment, and closing conditions. If another buyer
can reach diligence quickly, the first buyer must trade price or certainty for
the exclusivity it wants.

## Key Takeaways

- Optionality has value until the seller exchanges it for certainty.
- Model the distribution of proceeds, not just the top-line number.
- Parallel negotiations require legal, ethical, and operational discipline.
