# Chapter 49 — Man Plans and God Laughs

## Thesis

The outcome that saves a company may be an unplanned combination of product,
market timing, and distribution, while the path that looked strategically pure
can still become valuable without its original champion.

## Core Idea

The epilogue explains that Facebook's post-IPO rescue was not Custom Audiences
or FBX but News Feed ads on mobile, a product that combined an existing feed,
mobile inventory, and a sudden shift in usage. Mobile was structurally
different from desktop: third-party cookies and data brokers were weaker, apps
held siloed first-party data, and high-quality ad inventory was scarce because
many mobile apps monetized directly. Facebook's data and engaged placement were
therefore more valuable on mobile than the author's earlier critique of social
data would suggest. FBX later reached substantial new revenue with little
engineering investment, while Facebook Plus faded and Facebook bought growth
through Instagram and WhatsApp. The author closes by contrasting the founders'
financial outcomes, his tax and vesting losses, his mother's death, and his
decision to finish the book and pursue solo sailing—an ending that returns the
book's themes of pseudorandomness, ambition, mortality, and chosen autonomy.

## Frameworks

- **Post-hoc narrative correction:** separate the winning product's actual
  causal mechanism from the strategy leaders were debating beforehand.
- **Market-context fit:** data value depends on the surrounding ecosystem's
  identifiers, inventory, competition, and user behavior; it is not intrinsic.

## Key Concepts

- Mobile News Feed ads
- Facebook Audience Network
- App data silos
- Third-party cookies
- FBX revenue
- Instagram and WhatsApp acquisitions
- Narrative fallacy

## Source Examples / Evidence

- Mobile usage becomes the majority of Facebook usage in 2013, and mobile
  revenue roughly doubles year over year in the account.
- FBX reaches approximately half a billion dollars in revenue by early 2014,
  while requiring far fewer engineers than News Feed ads.

## Distinctions

- **Planned strategy vs. winning combination:** an outcome can be correct even
  when the original theory was wrong.
- **Desktop data vs. mobile data:** the same platform's information can have
  different value when cookies, app silos, and inventory change.
- **Revenue success vs. personal success:** a product may thrive while its
  builder loses wealth, health, or time.

## Limitations

The epilogue is retrospective and written before later platform, privacy,
regulatory, and market changes. Revenue figures and causal claims are the
author's historical account; the personal ending is not a universal model of
work, family, or independence. The supplied extraction drops four images and
includes back matter after the narrative; this note covers the epilogue only.

## Practical Application

After a strategic surprise, reconstruct the causal chain from market conditions,
product surface, data availability, and user behavior. Preserve the ability to
run multiple bets, but audit which assumptions were actually validated before
turning the winner into a repeatable playbook.

## Worked Example

A company debates a sophisticated targeting system while usage quietly shifts
to a new surface with less competing inventory and better engagement. Rather
than treating the debate as settled, the team measures the new surface's
incremental revenue, data quality, retention, and supply constraints. The
winning strategy may be the simple combination that fits the new context.

## Key Takeaways

- Success is often discovered after the fact.
- Data value is contextual, not an intrinsic property of a platform.
- A company can win while an individual contributor's path ends badly.
