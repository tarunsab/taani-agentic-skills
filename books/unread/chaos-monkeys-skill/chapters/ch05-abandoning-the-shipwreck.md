# Chapter 05 — Abandoning the Shipwreck

## Thesis

Leaving a failing startup is a negotiation over power, legal exposure, and
psychological captivity, not simply a personal choice to pursue a better idea.

## Core Idea

The founders resign from Adchemy after recognizing that the company's claimed
success does not match its product reality, and management responds with
retention pressure, intimidation, and attempts to exploit immigration
vulnerability. McEachen's family obligations and Zymnis's visa status make the
cost of exit unequal even though all three share the same diagnosis. The author
turns the episode into a set of cynical Silicon Valley axioms: investors buy
time and options, employees trade time for money, entrepreneurs mediate between
the two, culture lives in what is tolerated, and legal threats can function as
expensive bluffs. His larger claim is that startups are experiments conducted
with other people's money, while success retrospectively launders behavior that
would otherwise look abusive or incompetent. The shipwreck metaphor therefore
describes both the company and the social system that makes leaving it hard.

## Frameworks

- **Exit-cost asymmetry:** the same employer decision can impose different risks
  on a parent, visa holder, or worker without savings.
- **Power through ambiguity:** threats, contracts, and lawsuits need not be
  legally strong to alter behavior if the target cannot afford to test them.

## Key Concepts

- Retention harassment
- Immigration leverage
- Stockholm-syndrome dynamics
- Startup culture as tacit permission
- Amoral incentives and reputational forgiveness

## Source Examples / Evidence

- Adchemy threatens an immigration-related escalation after Zymnis leaves,
  despite legal uncertainty around the move.
- The founders are told in different ways that they are naïve even though the
  company has no credible core product or paying customers.

## Distinctions

- **Formal right vs. practical freedom:** having a legal argument is not the
  same as being able to pay for enforcement.
- **Loyalty vs. sunk cost:** staying can reflect fear, dependency, or habit
  rather than genuine belief.
- **Risk-taking vs. recklessness:** accepting uncertainty is different from
  transferring avoidable risk onto people with fewer options.

## Limitations

The axioms are deliberately provocative and should be read as the author's
interpretive lens, not as empirical laws of all startups. The legal and
immigration details are historical and summarized from one participant's view;
they are not legal advice.

## Practical Application

When leaving a high-pressure organization, inventory runway, contractual
restrictions, immigration or family constraints, data-retention obligations,
and references. Treat every threat as a claim that needs independent legal
review, but plan for the possibility that delay itself is the intended weapon.

## Worked Example

A company threatens a departing employee with a vague claim while the employee
has only a few weeks of savings. The practical response is to separate the
claim's legal merits from its immediate cash impact, seek counsel, preserve
records, and negotiate a clean exit while reducing the employer's ability to
hold immigration, payroll, or references hostage.

## Key Takeaways

- Exit decisions are constrained by unequal personal risk.
- Ambiguous legal pressure can be powerful even before a court rules.
- A startup's culture is revealed by what it does when people try to leave.
