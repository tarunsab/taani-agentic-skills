# Chapter 30 — Twice Bitten, Thrice Shy

## Thesis

The freedom gained from startup wealth can be pursued as an escape from fixed
obligations, but treating family and relationships as constraints reveals the
human cost of an entrepreneur's autonomy model.

## Core Idea

The birth of Noah prompts the author to revisit his father's warning that family
can create vulnerability and reduce freedom. He interprets work, cohabitation,
and financial dependence through a slavery metaphor, then chooses support and
visitation over a shared household while buying a sailboat with Facebook cash.
The boat becomes a physical symbol of mobility, self-direction, and the life he
imagines outside corporate control. At the same time, the chapter is not
evidence that detachment solves vulnerability; it shows how the author's
conceptual system prioritizes optionality over conventional security. The
useful analytical distinction is between financial independence and emotional
or relational responsibility, which cannot be reduced to the same balance sheet.

## Frameworks

- **Obligation and optionality:** fixed commitments narrow future choices but can
  also create meaning, stability, and shared capacity.
- **Freedom audit:** identify which constraints are financial, legal, emotional,
  or self-imposed instead of treating them as one category.

## Key Concepts

- Family vulnerability
- Financial independence
- Fixed obligations
- Support and visitation
- Sailboat as autonomy symbol

## Source Examples / Evidence

- The chapter follows the birth of Noah and the author's choice to maintain
  child support and visitation without cohabitation.
- A 37-foot boat becomes both a practical home and a declaration of autonomy
  after the Facebook deal.

## Distinctions

- **Independence vs. isolation:** reducing financial dependence does not remove
  the duties created by relationships.
- **Flexibility vs. avoidance:** mobility can enable a life, or help avoid hard
  obligations.
- **Author's rationale vs. universal prescription:** the memoir presents a
  personal self-justification, not a general family model.

## Limitations

The chapter's relationship decisions are private, ethically contested, and
reported from one perspective. The slavery analogy is rhetorically powerful
but can obscure the difference between coercion, responsibility, and chosen
commitment.

## Practical Application

When evaluating a career or exit, list both the freedom gained and the people
affected. Plan financial support, time, communication, and care explicitly;
independence should increase capacity to meet obligations, not erase them.

## Connections

## Worked Example

A founder receives a liquidity event and wants to leave employment immediately.
An autonomy plan can include a cash reserve, support commitments, health and
care arrangements, and a realistic maintenance budget for the desired lifestyle.
That preserves optionality without pretending other people are merely costs.

## Key Takeaways

- Financial freedom and relational responsibility are different dimensions.
- Optionality is valuable but not morally neutral.
- Personal operating systems deserve the same explicit planning as company ones.
