# Chapter 14: Nutrition 3.0: You Say Potato, I Say “Nutritional Biochemistry”

## Core Idea

The chapter rejects nutrition tribalism and replaces the word “diet” with nutritional biochemistry: food molecules interact with genes, metabolism, the microbiome, physiology, and each person’s behavior. Nutrition 3.0 is personalized, data-informed, and judged partly by sustainability rather than by ideological purity. The first questions are whether someone is overnourished or undernourished, undermuscled or adequately muscled, and metabolically healthy or not. Most people with metabolic dysfunction need lower energy intake, more adequate protein, and exercise, but a corrective intervention for disease may differ from a maintenance pattern for a healthy person. The chapter’s humility is deliberate: the science is weaker and more confounded than nutrition headlines suggest.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Nutrition 3.0: nutritional biochemistry over diet labels.
- Corrective nutrition versus maintenance nutrition.
- Epidemiology versus clinical trials, and efficacy versus effectiveness.
- Individual variation in adherence and metabolic response.
- Bradford Hill criteria as a way to interrogate causal claims.

## What We Sort of Know About Nutritional Biochemistry (and How We Sort of Know It)

Food-frequency questionnaires, healthy-user bias, confounding, and small effect sizes make nutritional epidemiology especially fragile. The chapter contrasts a weak association such as a hazard ratio around 1.17 for red or processed meat with the much larger risk signal associated with smoking, arguing that the former cannot bear the certainty commonly assigned to it. David Allison’s mouse experiment shows how even genetically similar animals can produce a misleading observational association inside a randomized cohort when healthier, hungrier mice eat more and live longer. Clinical trials improve causal inference but face adherence, duration, sample-size, and control problems; the Women’s Health Initiative’s “low-fat” group ate about 28 percent of calories from fat versus 37 percent in controls, so the real-world prescription was not a stark low-fat test. PREDIMED supplied stronger hard-outcome evidence for an olive-oil- or nut-nudged Mediterranean pattern in a high-risk population, but it still does not establish one perfect diet for everyone.

## Mental Models

The Dunning–Kruger curve represents the danger of high confidence with little nutritional knowledge. The efficacy/effectiveness distinction separates what a diet can do under ideal adherence from what telling real people to follow it accomplishes. A “signal versus noise” filter prioritizes large, reproducible, mechanistically coherent effects over sensational small associations.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating an epidemiological association as a prescription or a prohibition.
- Assuming the average weight-loss result describes every individual.
- Confusing two similar diets in a trial with proof that a category does not work.
- Using labels such as vegan, keto, or carnivore to avoid measuring the actual outcome.

## Worked Example

PREDIMED’s roughly 7,500 high-risk participants received olive oil, nuts, or a lower-fat control pattern and showed lower major cardiovascular events in the nudged Mediterranean arms. That is meaningful evidence for a context, but the population’s risk, the intervention’s food substitutions, adherence, and the study’s design all matter. A person should ask what changed in their own apoB, glucose, energy balance, muscle, and ability to sustain the pattern rather than import the headline wholesale.

## Key Takeaways

Use simple guardrails—avoid too many or too few calories, eat sufficient protein and essential fats, obtain micronutrients, and avoid pathogens and toxins—then personalize the details. Correct metabolic dysfunction without sacrificing lean mass. The chapter’s strongest recommendation is epistemic: stay humble, measure meaningful outcomes, and avoid turning uncertain nutrition research into religion.
