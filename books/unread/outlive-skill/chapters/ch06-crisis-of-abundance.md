# Chapter 6: The Crisis of Abundance: Can Our Ancient Genes Cope with Our Modern Diet?

## Core Idea

The chapter describes metabolic dysfunction as a disease of surplus energy, not simply a problem of body size. Excess fuel first fills limited glycogen stores and subcutaneous fat, then spills into visceral and ectopic sites such as the liver, where it promotes inflammation and insulin resistance. Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) can be silent and can progress to steatohepatitis (NASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and type 2 diabetes, while the same metabolic environment raises risk for cardiovascular disease, cancer, and neurodegeneration. Obesity is an imperfect proxy: some people with larger bodies remain metabolically healthy, while some normal-weight people carry substantial risk. The strategic lesson is to measure metabolic function early rather than wait for an HbA1c threshold or visible obesity.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Metabolic continuum: hyperinsulinemia → prediabetes → NAFLD/NASH and/or type 2 diabetes.
- Glycogen as a limited short-term store; fat as the safer long-term energy store until storage capacity is exceeded.
- Spillover and ectopic fat as a mechanism linking energy surplus to disease.
- Metabolic syndrome as a cluster of risk markers rather than a synonym for obesity.

## Key Concepts

The chapter uses the “fatty liver in a non-drinker” story to show that a person can have liver disease without alcohol being the primary cause. It cites NAFLD as affecting more than one in four people globally and emphasizes that normal liver enzymes do not guarantee optimal metabolic health; before cirrhosis, the liver can often recover if the underlying surplus is corrected. Its metabolic-syndrome checklist includes elevated blood pressure, high triglycerides, low HDL, increased waist circumference, and elevated fasting glucose, with three or more criteria sufficient for the label and no requirement for obesity. These are screening concepts, not self-diagnosis thresholds.

## Energy Storage and Fructose

The body can store only a limited amount of glycogen—roughly 1,600 calories in the chapter’s estimate, mostly in muscle—so persistent surplus is directed toward fat. Insulin helps manage that traffic, and subcutaneous fat can buffer excess until its capacity is exceeded; then visceral and ectopic deposition increases. The discussion of fructose argues that large doses, especially in liquid form, can rapidly consume cellular ATP, increase uric acid, stimulate hunger and fat storage, and exploit an evolutionary adaptation that once favored survival during scarcity. Whole fruit behaves differently from sweetened drinks because fiber, chewing, and dose change the exposure; the broader cause remains sustained energy surplus rather than a single villainous molecule.

## Detecting Metabolic Dysfunction

Fasting glucose and HbA1c can miss early insulin resistance because glucose may remain normal while insulin rises to keep it there. The chapter therefore discusses fasting and post-meal insulin, a 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test with repeated samples, triglyceride-to-HDL ratios, VLDL, uric acid, homocysteine, inflammatory markers, and ALT as pieces of a fuller picture. It cites strong associations between hyperinsulinemia and later cancer, Alzheimer’s disease, and cardiovascular death, while the exact magnitude and causal pathways should be interpreted cautiously. Dynamic measurements are useful because they reveal how the system handles a challenge rather than only its resting state.

## Mental Models

The “overflowing storage tank” model explains why weight alone is an incomplete readout. Metabolic health is a continuum with compensations that can conceal damage until the system loses control. “Ancient genes versus modern abundance” describes a mismatch: mechanisms that once protected against famine can become harmful when calorie-dense food and inactivity are constant.

## Anti-patterns

- Waiting for diabetes before acting on years of hyperinsulinemia or liver fat.
- Using BMI or body weight as the only metabolic measure.
- Blaming fructose in isolation while ignoring total energy intake and liquid calories.
- Treating a normal ALT, glucose, or HbA1c as proof that all metabolic systems are healthy.

## Worked Example

A normal-weight person with high fasting insulin, elevated triglycerides, low HDL, and fatty liver demonstrates why obesity is not the diagnosis. The relevant question is whether energy is being stored safely and whether insulin must keep rising to control glucose. A plan would need individualized assessment of eating pattern, activity, sleep, medications, and other causes—not a generic “eat less” slogan.

## Key Takeaways

Metabolic dysfunction is upstream of several Horsemen and is often detectable before a formal disease label. The chapter supports earlier measurement, adequate movement, better food quality, and sustainable energy control, but not a single universal diet or unmonitored laboratory program. Claims about specific biomarkers and disease multipliers are book-level associations and require clinical context.
