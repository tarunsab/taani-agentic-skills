# Cheatsheet

A compact study aid for applying the book’s reasoning. It is not medical advice.

## Start With the Objective

Ask: “What do I want to remain able to do in my last decade?” Convert the answer into capacities:

| Desired capacity | Measure or train |
| --- | --- |
| Walk, hike, recover | Aerobic efficiency / Zone 2 |
| Handle a hard effort | VO2 max / intervals |
| Carry, lift, rise | Strength, grip, hinge, power |
| Avoid falls and injury | Stability, balance, eccentric control |
| Think and relate | Sleep, vascular/metabolic health, emotional skills |

## The Medicine 3.0 Filter

1. What is the long biological prologue?
2. What is the risk of waiting or doing nothing?
3. How does the evidence apply to this person rather than the average study subject?
4. Is the target lifespan, healthspan, or both?
5. What measurement would change the next decision?

## Domain Rules

- **Cardiovascular:** Think apoB particles × years of exposure; add Lp(a), blood pressure, smoking, metabolic health, family history, and calcium in context.
- **Metabolic:** Do not use weight or HbA1c alone; consider insulin response, triglycerides/HDL, liver markers, waist, activity, sleep, and body composition.
- **Exercise:** Cover base, ceiling, muscle/bone, and stability. More intensity is not automatically more longevity.
- **Nutrition:** Solve the actual problem; protect protein and lean mass; judge the pattern by outcomes and adherence, not its label.
- **Sleep:** Give enough opportunity, keep a fixed wake time, protect darkness/coolness, reduce alcohol and late stimulation, and screen persistent problems.
- **Emotional health:** Notice warning signs early, widen the distress window, use opposite action, and seek therapy or urgent help when safety is at stake.

## Evidence Smell Test

Treat a claim cautiously when it has a tiny effect size, relies on food-frequency recall, confuses correlation with causation, lacks replication, or turns a biomarker change into a longevity promise. Prefer convergence across human outcomes, experiments, mechanisms, and natural experiments—but keep the uncertainty.

## Red Flags

Do not turn these notes into self-prescribing: rapamycin/metformin, hormone therapy, lipid drugs, fasting protocols, supplements, cancer screening, genetic testing, or sleep medication. Symptoms, abnormal results, pregnancy, kidney/liver disease, eating-disorder history, severe insomnia/apnea, chest pain, neurological change, or emotional/suicide risk require qualified medical care. The book’s numerical examples are context-dependent landmarks, not universal targets.
