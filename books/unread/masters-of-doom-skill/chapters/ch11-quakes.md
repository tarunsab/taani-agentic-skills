# Chapter 11: Quakes

## Core Idea

“Quakes” expands the book’s question from “how do you make a game?” to “why do people need artificial worlds at all?” Drawing on Plato, Huizinga, McLuhan, the Holodeck, the Metaverse, and cyberspace, the chapter treats play as a meaningful human function that lets people escape, learn, recharge, and connect. Carmack’s Quake ambition is the technical expression of that belief: a full 3-D world with networked groups, not merely another set of fast rooms. Romero responds with an even richer design—Quake, the hammer, the Hellgate Cube, and a world with personality—then publicly promises a movement before the team has settled the plan. Doom II, DWANGO, mod packs, clans, and military uses show that the social and commercial world around id is already expanding faster than the core company. Quake’s development becomes a collision between this expansive ecosystem and Carmack’s desire for a small, technically focused team. The chapter’s limit is that virtual-world aspiration does not solve ordinary coordination; a community can be large while the team making its infrastructure is exhausted and directionless.

## Frameworks Introduced

The first framework is **games as necessary play**: an artificial world is valuable because it creates a bounded experience of agency and meaning, not because it copies reality perfectly. The second is the virtual-company model, in which a game engine, mod tools, servers, and communities let many people contribute around a small core. The third is the small-team ceiling: beyond a useful size, communication overhead, entropy, and conflicting incentives can make a project slower and less coherent. Apply the framework by separating the scale of the player ecosystem from the scale of the development core, then explicitly funding the coordination layer between them.

## Key Concepts

- **Homo Ludens** — Huizinga’s image of humans as players whose games carry meaning beyond immediate utility.
- **Holodeck/Metaverse** — imagined alternate spaces that motivate increasingly immersive technology.
- **Quake vision** — full 3-D, Internet play, and a world that could support groups rather than one player alone.
- **DWANGO** — hosted modem play that turns a game into a pickup arena across distance.
- **Mod ecosystem** — fan-made levels and projects become a distributed extension of id’s work.
- **Small-team ceiling** — adding people can reduce efficiency and quality when the work cannot be cleanly decomposed.

## Mental Models

Think of a virtual world as three layers: the simulation, the access network, and the social rules players create around it. A breakthrough in only one layer produces a fragile experience. Think of a growing community as a second company whose work is unpaid but not free; it needs tools, norms, servers, and attention. Think of team size as a threshold, not a linear dial: an extra specialist helps only when the ownership gap is clearer than the communication cost. The story’s tension comes from confusing a large world with a large team.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating immersion as visual complexity while neglecting response, access, and social play.
- Announcing a future world before its scope, technology, and ownership are aligned.
- Scaling the staff to compensate for an unclear design or absent coordination.
- Using fan enthusiasm as evidence that the internal team has enough capacity to deliver.
- Assuming a mod community can replace product direction rather than complement it.

## Worked Example

DWANGO shows how a player need can reveal a missing layer in the product. Doom supports modem-to-modem play, but Bob Huntley and Kee Kimbrell want a pickup-game experience in which a player can dial a shared service and meet others. They build a prototype in about six weeks, find Romero at the Doom II event, and demonstrate a social interface that id had imagined but not implemented. Romero immediately experiences the value by playing from Dallas against Kee in Houston. The service turns an individual game into an ongoing networked place, and id’s later Quake ambitions inherit that expectation. The practical lesson is to watch for repeated workarounds by users; they may be the next product layer.

## Key Takeaways

- Play can be a serious source of meaning, agency, and recovery.
- Immersive products need technical, access, and social layers to reinforce one another.
- A small core can support a large ecosystem only when interfaces are deliberately designed.
- Scale the organization only when the coordination problem is named and owned.
