# Chapter 4: Pizza Money

## Core Idea

“Pizza Money” explains how a tiny advance and a low-overhead distribution model give the team enough runway to become independent. The chapter contrasts Carmack’s present-focused intensity with Romero’s tendency to carry the past forward and imagine the future; both orientations matter, but they create different reactions to risk, family, deadlines, and publicity. A borrowed-PC weekend produces the Commander Keen prototype, while Scott Miller’s shareware plan converts a compelling first episode into orders that quickly grow from a small advance to a dependable monthly income. The economics are simple enough to fit the team: a cheap or freely circulated first taste, no inventory-heavy retail launch, and payment for the remaining episodes. The money buys equipment and freedom, but the team’s moonlighting and Softdisk’s attempted partnership reveal an unresolved question about who owns the value created with shared resources. Id is born on February 1, 1991, after the staff revolt collapses the 50/50 proposal and leaves a contract requiring one Gamer’s Edge game every two months.

## Frameworks Introduced

The chapter introduces **pizza money** as a seed-capital threshold: a project does not need venture funding if early revenue can pay for food, hardware, and the next cycle of work. It also shows shareware as a behavioral funnel: remove the cost of trying, make the first episode generous, and let the player choose continuation. The time-orientation contrast is a useful collaboration lens; a present-focused builder may protect momentum while a future-oriented designer protects possibility, but either can become insensitive to obligations. Finally, the Softdisk dispute teaches that independence is not only an economic condition; it is a negotiated relationship with the institution whose equipment, payroll, or audience enabled the breakthrough.

## Key Concepts

- **Pizza money** — enough early revenue to fund tools and continued work without outside capital.
- **First-episode funnel** — a bounded opening game creates trust and appetite for paid continuation.
- **Low-overhead distribution** — BBS and disk circulation avoid much of retail’s inventory and marketing cost.
- **Present versus future orientation** — Carmack protects current focus; Romero hoards possibilities and plans ahead.
- **Id formation** — the Softdisk dispute turns a project team into an independent company.

## Mental Models

Treat the shareware first episode as both a demo and a financing instrument. If the first slice is valuable enough to spread and distinctive enough to create desire, each player can become a low-cost sales lead. Treat early revenue as a loop rather than a prize: cash pays for machines, machines increase output, and output creates the next episode. The loop fails if the team confuses cash flow with organizational maturity; id has revenue before it has learned how to handle ownership, workload, or relationships.

## Anti-patterns

- Spending the first revenue on status before securing the next production cycle.
- Announcing a future movement or empire before the current episode has earned trust.
- Treating a contributor’s borrowed resources as free because the result is exciting.
- Believing that a 50/50 partnership repairs a conflict whose roles and incentives are still unclear.

## Worked Example

Commander Keen shows how a $2,000 advance can become strategic independence. Scott Miller asks for a game by Christmas, and Tom Hall supplies the memorable Billy Blaze premise while Carmack, Romero, and Adrian make the engine, tools, and art fit together. The December 14 upload gives players a complete but bounded experience, and the remaining episodes create a reason to pay. Orders rise rapidly, helped by Scott’s family handling the manual process, and the team’s monthly income reaches the level where new PCs and uninterrupted work are possible. When Al Vekovius discovers the night work, the economic success does not settle the relationship; it exposes the gap between Softdisk’s payroll and id’s emerging value. The practical lesson is to design the revenue loop and the ownership agreement at the same time.

## Key Takeaways

- Small recurring revenue can be more liberating than a large one-time investment.
- A good first episode is a product, a test, and a distribution engine.
- Growth money should first buy capability and continuity.
- Independence requires explicit agreements about tools, time, credit, and ownership.
