# Patterns

## Enemy-defined identity
**When to use**: A community becomes unusually cohesive during an external crisis.
**How**: Map the prior factions, identify the threat, name the leader or institution created, and check whether unity survives the threat.
**Trade-offs**: Cohesion can enable defence, but it can also justify exclusion, obedience, and expansion.

Across the book, Rome, Vikings, France, Russia, and Germany make separate tribes or states feel like one flock. The mechanism is political salience: danger makes a shared label more useful than local differences. The same identity can outlive the material threat as a patriotic myth. Test whether the new unity became an institution or merely a memory of standing together.

## Chronicle and legend triangulation
**When to use**: A famous event depends on a vivid story from a late, partisan, or hostile source.
**How**: Record the source, date, position, purpose, material evidence, competing versions, and what remains uncertain.
**Trade-offs**: Caution loses a clean story but prevents disputed detail from becoming false certainty.

The Night of the Long Knives, Harold's arrow, Cnut's tide, Alfred's cakes, Joan's trial, and Elizabeth's speech are all memorable because later narratives give them a clear moral. The book preserves their usefulness while marking gaps in archaeology, chronology, eyewitness status, or motive. A story can be culturally important without every detail being historically secure. Keep reported, plausible, and certain claims visibly separate.

## Coalition durability
**When to use**: Rivals form an alliance against a common enemy.
**How**: Identify the shared threat, each member's private goal, the command arrangement, the post-victory settlement, and the likely fracture point.
**Trade-offs**: Temporary cooperation can win a battle but leave succession, land, or legitimacy unresolved.

Cassivellaunus, Boudicca, Alfred, Brian Boru, and the Western allies all show different versions of coalition. The enemy-of-my-enemy rule lowers the cost of cooperation, but it does not erase old rivalries. A victorious champion may become resented, as the chapter says of Arminius, or a settlement may divide the territory rather than create one state. The postwar institutional design is the real test of unity.

## Moat and logistics
**When to use**: An island or geographic barrier is treated as proof of national exceptionalism.
**How**: Trace landing sites, tides, weather, ships, food, cavalry, retreat, communications, and the enemy's need for allies.
**Trade-offs**: Geography can protect a state while limiting its ability to project power.

The Channel repeatedly shapes Roman, Spanish, French, and German campaigns. Storms and tides can destroy an invasion, but they can also isolate a defending army or block its escape. Deary's examples show that “British weather” is an outcome of material conditions, planning, and chance, not a moral force. Use geography to explain the result before using character or destiny.

## Empire mirror
**When to use**: A national story condemns an enemy's conquest, slavery, or extraction.
**How**: Name the enemy's practice, find the home nation's analogue, follow the profit or authority, and compare who bears the cost.
**Trade-offs**: Comparison can expose hypocrisy, but it must not erase differences in scale, intent, or context.

The book places Roman, Spanish, Dutch, British, American, and German violence in a shared moral frame. The mirror does not say all episodes are identical; it asks whether “civilization,” law, or divine sanction is being used selectively. The chapters repeatedly connect wealth in ports, companies, and courts to people who were enslaved, starved, displaced, or left without care. Use the pattern to audit patriotic moral contrasts.

## Memory-cost ledger
**When to use**: A country celebrates a victory, hero, anthem, or national character.
**How**: Pair the public symbol with the absent workers, civilians, prisoners, poor, colonized people, and institutional failures.
**Trade-offs**: A fuller memory may reduce comforting pride but preserves the actual distribution of sacrifice.

Nelson eclipses Jack Crawford, Armada glory hides sick sailors, and Blitz Spirit can hide panic, class privilege, black markets, and abused evacuees. The method does not deny courage; it asks what the heroic frame makes hard to see. Public memory is a selection system, not a neutral census of contribution. Keep the celebrated act and its human cost in the same note.

## Adaptive defence
**When to use**: A stronger enemy wins when you fight on its preferred terms.
**How**: Change terrain, tempo, formation, fortifications, rotation, supply, or the role that absorbs the largest risk.
**Trade-offs**: Adaptation may sacrifice territory or short-term glory for survivable resistance.

Alfred retreats and builds fortress Wessex; Caratacus and the Picts prefer guerrilla methods; Paulinus defeats Boudicca by choosing a narrow gorge. The pattern treats war as a system rather than a duel between heroes. It can fail when leaders insist on open battle, misread the terrain, or lack the institutions to sustain the adaptation. Use it as a counter to courage-only explanations.

## Reclassification through contact
**When to use**: A former enemy is becoming a neighbour, colleague, ally, or family member.
**How**: Identify the old category, create repeated shared activity, notice ordinary competence, and revise the political story without erasing the past.
**Trade-offs**: Contact can humanize an opponent, but unresolved harm still requires memory and accountability.

France becomes an ally, German dynasties rule Britain before Germany becomes an enemy, Paisley and Adams share power, and Bert Trautmann becomes a respected Manchester City goalkeeper. These transitions show that hostile categories are maintained by institutions and stories, not by immutable peoples. Contact supplies a different source of evidence: the former enemy as a person in a shared practice. Reclassification is strongest when it changes incentives as well as feelings.
