# Chapter 4: France

## Core Idea

France is Britain's longest recurring enemy in the book because the relationship moves from Norman conquest to dynastic rivalry, revolution, Napoleonic war, and eventual political partnership. The Norman Conquest made English landholders subjects of French-speaking rulers, but it also created a new problem: kings had possessions on both sides of the Channel and had to choose between their continental and insular interests. The Hundred Years' War supplied Britain with some of its most durable underdog myths, while Joan of Arc helped turn a collection of regional loyalties into a French national identity. Later conflicts show the same pattern in reverse: French revolutionaries and Napoleon made Britain more cohesive, but British victory stories often conceal luck, exaggeration, class cost, and failed invasions. The enemy finally becomes an ally and European partner, demonstrating that enmity is historically reversible.

## Historical Sections

### William, Hastings, and the conquest of England

Norman Duke William inherited a culture of Viking travel and conquest, and the chapter presents his childhood danger and mutilation of Alençon leaders as evidence of a ruler who used terror strategically. The Norman account says Harold Godwinson promised William the English throne after being captured in 1064, but Harold had every reason to make a promise under duress and later deny it. At Hastings in 1066, the story of minstrel Taillefer, the Norman feigned retreat, and Harold's arrow in the eye became national memory; the arrow is visible in the Bayeux Tapestry but not securely established by contemporary documentary evidence. Guy of Amiens and William of Malmesbury give different accounts of Harold's death, and both sources have reasons to praise William or were writing later. The conquest was nevertheless real: Norman lords received Saxon land, castles and cathedrals made domination visible, and William's Harrying of the North used burning, killing livestock, and starvation to punish resistance.

### Conquest spreads, then becomes English

Resistance was fragmented: Hereward the Wake held the Isle of Ely for a time, Scotland's Malcolm III supported Edgar the Ætheling before submitting, and the Welsh fought through alliances and rebellion. Strongbow's intervention in Ireland in 1170 began an Anglo-Norman supremacy that would last for centuries, while Welsh leaders sometimes enlisted Vikings against English power and paid for victory with captured people sold into slavery. Edward I eventually destroyed Welsh independence through campaigns, the kidnapping of Eleanor, the death of Llywelyn, castles, new English towns, and the Statute of Rhuddlan of 1284. The chapter treats this as integration by coercion rather than a consensual British union. By the time Henry IV became the first king generally said to speak English as his first language, the Norman conquerors had been assimilated into an English political identity, leaving England ready to define a new enemy across the Channel.

### The Hundred Years' War and the underdog story

When Philip VI confiscated Aquitaine in 1337, Edward III claimed the French throne, beginning the war that the book calls the Hundred-and-sixteen Years' War because fighting ran across 116 years. At Crécy in 1346, English longbows benefited from terrain, rain, the French crossbows' wet strings, and French knights attacking uphill; at Poitiers in 1356, French mistakes and English defence led to the capture of King John. A storm near Chartres forced Edward III to make peace in 1360, showing that weather could defeat armies more decisively than national character. Agincourt in 1415 became the clearest “plucky English and Welsh versus massed French” tale, but Deary reduces the numbers, questions the origin of the V-sign story, notes that Welsh archers were a minority of the army, and disputes a large prisoner massacre. Shakespeare's Henry V made the patriotic version memorable, but his employment by monarchs is a reason to test the rhetoric against contracts, estimates, and contemporary records.

### Joan of Arc and French national identity

Joan of Arc's importance is political as much as military. In the book's account, the nineteen-year-old shepherdess claimed angelic instruction, led French forces, and helped Prince Charles become Charles VII; her leadership gave scattered regional populations a reason to think of themselves as French. Captured by Burgundy and sold to the English, she was tried by French priests and declared a witch because the English could not accept that providence might support their enemy. She was burned, later became a saint in 1920, and remains a French symbol, although Deary undercuts the English villain story by asking why Charles VII did not ransom or rescue the woman who gave him his crown. The French wars failed to create a unified Britannia but helped create France as a political identity. The lesson is that national formation may depend on a story of common liberation more than on the simple outcome of a battle.

### Revolution, Napoleon, and the Channel

The French Revolution spread the language of liberty, equality, and fraternity while the guillotine and Robespierre's Terror made British observers reconsider revolutionary romance. Wordsworth first celebrated the revolution, Coleridge condemned Britain's opposition, and both represent the way political judgment changes as violence becomes visible; Dickens's “best and worst of times” is the more balanced summary. Napoleon rose through force, using grapeshot against royalist rebels, then treated Britain as Europe's principal rival. In 1797 a French force landed at Fishguard, but gales, drunkenness, poor troops, empty British cannon, and local resistance led by the cobbler Jemima Nicholas forced a surrender; no foreign army has landed on Britain since, in the book's deliberately broad formulation. Nelson's victories at Aboukir Bay and Trafalgar, followed by Waterloo, turned French conflict into British hero-making, but the chapter keeps the accidents, illness, and enemy competence visible.

### From enemy to partner

Germany's rise changed the Anglo-French relationship: France, once Britain's principal rival, became an ally in the Franco-Prussian crisis and the two world wars. After 1945, the Common Market was designed to make another European war less likely, but Britain initially declined to join and Charles de Gaulle vetoed its application twice. Britain eventually entered the European Community in 1973, only to leave after the 2016 vote of 52 to 48 per cent. Deary ends with de Gaulle's distinction: patriotism puts love of one's own people first, while nationalism puts hatred of other people first. The historical use is to ask whether a former enemy is being kept alive for identity reasons after the material conflict has ended.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Conquest-to-assimilation**: Track how a foreign ruling elite becomes local while its coercive institutions and memory remain.
- **Underdog myth audit**: Compare heroic national stories with numbers, source dates, terrain, weather, and the treatment of prisoners.
- **Enemy replacement**: When an old rival becomes an ally, identify the new threat that makes the alliance politically possible.
- **Moat without destiny**: Treat the Channel as a logistics variable that raises invasion cost but does not prove exceptionalism.

## Key Concepts

- **Normans**: Viking-descended rulers from northern France who conquered England in 1066.
- **Harrying of the North**: William's punitive campaign of devastation after northern rebellion.
- **Hundred Years' War**: Anglo-French conflict over territory and the French throne, presented here as 116 years of fighting.
- **Crécy / Poitiers / Agincourt**: Battles used to examine English tactics and later underdog mythology.
- **Joan of Arc**: French military and religious symbol associated with the emergence of French national identity.
- **French Revolution**: 1789 upheaval whose ideals and violence reshaped European politics.
- **Fishguard**: Failed 1797 French landing defeated by weather and local Welsh resistance.
- **Entente Cordiale**: 1904 agreement marking the end of nearly a millennium of Anglo-French belligerence.

## Mental Models

- Use **two-bank politics** when a ruler holds territory on both sides of a border and cannot optimize for both.
- Think of **myths as coalition glue**: an inaccurate story can still create a shared identity.
- Use **enemy substitution** to explain sudden alliances after a new power changes the threat map.

## Anti-patterns

- **Arrow certainty**: turning a disputed detail into the causal explanation for a national defeat.
- **Plucky exceptionalism**: using victory by a smaller force to erase terrain, weather, technology, or French mistakes.
- **Peace-by-memory**: keeping a former enemy permanently hostile in rhetoric after institutions have integrated.

## Worked Example

Agincourt is a source-fidelity exercise. The familiar version says a tiny English army and heroic Welsh archers defeated an enormous French host, with the V sign and a massacre of common prisoners completing the legend. Deary instead points to modern estimates of roughly 12,000 French against 8,000 English and Welsh, records that Welsh soldiers were a small minority, and notes that ransom contracts covered prisoners of different ranks. The victory remains significant, but its meaning changes when the numerical and documentary claims are separated from Shakespearean nationalism.

## Key Takeaways

1. The Norman Conquest made Britain vulnerable to a ruler with interests on both sides of the Channel.
2. English military myths often convert contingency into character: weather, terrain, mistakes, and sources disappear behind “pluck.”
3. Joan of Arc matters because she helped regional communities imagine themselves as France.
4. France's transition from hereditary enemy to ally and EU partner is a direct test of whether national hostility is permanent.
