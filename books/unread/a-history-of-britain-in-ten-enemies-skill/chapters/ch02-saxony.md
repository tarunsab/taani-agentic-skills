# Chapter 2: Saxony

## Core Idea

The Saxon chapter begins with a mercenary arrangement that becomes a settlement and then a conquest. After Rome withdrew, Vortigern lacked a standing army and invited Hengist and Horsa from Saxony to fight the Picts and Scots; the traditional account says the hired warriors used the opportunity to seize Kent and expand. Deary marks the story as a mixture of fact and fiction, especially because archaeology does not prove Hengist and Horsa existed, but he keeps it because the betrayal became part of British memory. Anglo-Saxon kingdoms eventually shaped the English language and political map, while Celtic communities retreated west or across the sea. Christianity briefly supplied a shared framework, yet the story of Augustine's mission also shows how religious unity can be imposed through violence.

## Historical Sections

### From Roman vacuum to Saxon settlement

Around 410 the Roman withdrawal left southern Britain without the identity and military structure that had connected it to the empire. Gildas, a hostile source, presents Vortigern as incompetent and says he hired Hengist and Horsa, who landed in Kent in 449 and were rewarded with land at Thanet. The story then turns on payment, land hunger, and Vortigern's alleged marriage to Rowena, Hengist's daughter; the author's point is that a foreign force invited as protection can acquire a permanent stake. When the mercenaries were not paid, they plundered neighbours, and a peace conference on Salisbury Plain became the “Night of the Long Knives.” The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle reports that three hundred British elders were killed and Vortigern ransomed territory, but the Chronicle is later, West-Saxon in emphasis, and mixes annalistic record with legend and factual error.

### Seven kingdoms and the Arthur problem

Over the following centuries Anglo-Saxon migration and conquest produced seven kingdoms: Northumbria, Mercia, Wessex, East Anglia, Essex, Kent, and Sussex. The Britons who were not absorbed moved toward Wales or Ireland, and Saxon expansion halted near Hadrian's Wall, where northern resistance remained dangerous. Deary contrasts the uncertain evidence for Vortigern with the cultural confidence attached to Arthur, Merlin, the Round Table, and a sleeping army that will return in Britain's hour of need. This is not a claim that Arthur is impossible; it is a reminder that national hero status depends on narrative power rather than equal evidentiary standards. A society can preserve an inspiring resistance story while losing the institutional unity needed to resist in practice.

### Augustine and the violent edge of unity

The conversion of Æthelbert of Kent after Augustine's arrival in 597 made Kent the first Christian kingdom in England and gave the expanding Saxon order a shared religion. Welsh Christians, however, followed different saints and calendars, baptized differently, and even wore their hair differently, so doctrinal and cultural difference became a political dispute. When Augustine refused to stand to greet the Welsh representative, the Welsh rejected his authority; Augustine then called on King Æthelfrith to attack Chester and the nearby monastery of Bangor. The account says hundreds of monks were killed, with only fifty surviving, though the source tradition and dating are contested in the footnote. Deary's broader point is that a religion can unify a state by creating common practice while also deepening the hostility of communities excluded from the chosen version.

### Language as a later boundary

The chapter closes by linking the old Saxon conflict to modern language politics. Welsh remained spoken in Wales, Scotland, Ireland, and Brittany even as English became the principal language of the British Isles, but comparatively few Celtic words entered English. A twentieth-century campaign for bilingual Welsh road signs eventually made Welsh appear first on new signs, giving public language a symbolic place in the struggle over belonging. The common enemy is no longer simply the Saxon invader; it can be the English-speaking Welsh person who treats Welsh as secondary. This illustrates how an old conquest can survive in everyday institutions and how a shared language can unite one group while dividing another.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Mercenary blowback**: Before hiring outside force, model how it may acquire land, leverage, and a local constituency.
- **Chronicle reliability test**: Identify date, authorial location, political bias, genre, and the distance between event and record.
- **Religious unity with a boundary**: Ask who receives the new common identity and who is classified as deviant.

## Key Concepts

- **Hengist and Horsa**: Legendary or semi-legendary Saxon leaders in the traditional invasion story.
- **Vortigern**: British ruler said to have invited Saxon mercenaries after Rome's withdrawal.
- **Night of the Long Knives**: The reported massacre at a peace conference that transferred British territory.
- **Anglo-Saxon Chronicle**: Important but biased and uneven source for early English history.
- **Heptarchy**: The seven-kingdom structure used in the chapter to describe Anglo-Saxon England.
- **Augustine of Canterbury**: Roman missionary whose conversion work helped establish Saxon Christianity.
- **Bilingual signs**: Modern language policy used as an example of historical memory in public space.

## Mental Models

- Use **invitation-to-occupation** when a security solution changes who owns the terrain.
- Treat **national heroes as evidence of memory**, not automatically evidence of biography.
- Think of **language policy as institutionalized history**: signs make a hierarchy visible every day.

## Anti-patterns

- **Mercenary blindness**: assuming a hired fighter has no political interests of its own.
- **Myth/evidence collapse**: treating Arthur and Vortigern as equally documented because both are memorable.
- **Unity by erasure**: calling a kingdom unified when its religious or linguistic minorities are merely subordinated.

## Worked Example

The Night of the Long Knives works as a source-criticism exercise. The traditional narrative is memorable because its concealed knives, staged friendliness, massacre, and ransom have a clear dramatic arc. Yet its main written witness, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, was compiled centuries later, favoured West Saxon concerns, and includes legends and errors. A careful reader can preserve the event as a powerful memory while labelling the specific details as reported tradition rather than equally secure fact.

## Key Takeaways

1. A power vacuum invites both local rulers and foreign specialists to compete for legitimacy.
2. A mercenary contract can become a land transfer and then a new political order.
3. Religious conversion can integrate kingdoms while producing a new internal frontier.
4. The Arthur contrast shows that national memory often applies different evidentiary standards to heroes and villains.
