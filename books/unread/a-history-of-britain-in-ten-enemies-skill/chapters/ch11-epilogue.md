# Epilogue: The story Britain tells about itself

## Core Idea

The Epilogue answers the Introduction's question about British superiority by tracing it to symbols, school history, patriotic songs, empire, and repeated victory stories. Deary argues that British nationalism can mistake imperial reach for moral worth and turn selective memory into a citizenship test. He also insists that this is not uniquely British: students in many countries overestimate their nation's contribution to world history, with British and Russian students especially high in the survey he cites. The ten chapters instead show that Britain shares migration, violence, ambition, religious conflict, and political change with the people it fought. The practical conclusion is to see enemies historically: the same group can become ally, neighbour, or family, and tomorrow remains the most important day because identities are not fixed.

## Historical Sections

### From holy island to citizenship test

William Blake's vision of England as a sacred landscape is treated as national poetry rather than literal history; Jesus did not become proof that Britain was a divinely chosen country. Modern nationalist rhetoric can sound more inclusive by asking all religions and colours to be proud of one flag, but the chapter says this often means accepting entry only on condition of behaving in a prescribed British way. Citizenship tests then privilege historical knowledge over ordinary life, even though public figures such as David Cameron can fail basic questions about patriotic songs. The test is therefore not only about facts. It helps define which version of the past a citizen is expected to admire.

### Empire, anthem, and inflated contribution

The Epilogue contrasts Rule, Britannia! and Land of Hope and Glory with the violence of empire. “Rule the waves” and “wider still and wider” celebrate expansion even though British power was built through conquest, slavery, and suppression of people described as less civilized. A 2006 BBC survey found that 55 per cent of English respondents preferred Land of Hope and Glory to the national anthem, while the chapter links its imperial language to Cecil Rhodes's desire to extend British rule. Deary's 2018 cross-country student survey produces a combined estimate of 1,156 per cent contribution to world history; British students claimed 55 per cent and Russian students 61 per cent. These figures are used less as a scientific measure than as a demonstration of national narcissism and the distortions produced by education.

### Perspective and reversible enemies

The Epilogue refuses the easy comparison in which French regicide, German genocide, or Spanish conquest proves Britain is better, because Britain also executed monarchs, destroyed Tasmanian communities, and built an empire through violence. A nation can learn to condemn an enemy's crime while excluding its own analogous acts from the story. The book recommends a less misty-eyed history that recognizes common genes, common ambitions, and common failures across borders. Frank Borman's view of Earth from the Moon supplies the scale shift: from far away, national divisions look less natural than the shared world. Tony Blair's boast that Britain was the greatest nation on Earth is returned to the framework of the Introduction and shown as a claim requiring evidence, not a fact.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Nationalism audit**: When a country claims specialness, compare its symbols, material history, and treatment of outsiders.
- **Comparative hypocrisy check**: Test a moral contrast by asking whether the same standard is applied to the home nation.
- **Perspective shift**: Move from the national scale to the planetary or human scale when inherited categories appear absolute.
- **Enemy reversibility rule**: Before treating hostility as permanent, look for past transitions from enemy to ally, friend, or family.

## Key Concepts

- **Misty-eyed history**: Nostalgic national history that removes contradiction, violence, and contingency.
- **English nationalism**: The Epilogue's focus on imperial grandeur and the belief that England is unusually important.
- **Citizenship test**: A mechanism that turns selected historical knowledge into a condition of belonging.
- **National narcissism**: Inflated belief in a country's contribution or superiority.
- **Comparative hypocrisy**: Condemning another nation for conduct one overlooks at home.
- **Planetary perspective**: Seeing human commonality from outside the nation-state frame.

## Mental Models

- Use **claim/evidence separation** whenever a patriotic sentence sounds like a conclusion.
- Think of **national memory as a curated museum**: ask what is displayed, what is in storage, and who paid for the building.
- Use **tomorrow's reversibility** to resist policies that assume today's enemy will always be one.

## Anti-patterns

- **Anthem literalism**: treating patriotic lyrics as historical evidence.
- **Outsider-only guilt**: using foreign crimes to avoid examining domestic violence.
- **Fixed-identity politics**: assuming language, food, appearance, or birthplace makes a permanent enemy.

## Worked Example

The Epilogue's comparison exercise asks whether Britain is superior to France, Germany, or Spain because each has a famous record of violence. The author answers by placing British executions, colonial destruction, and imperial conquest beside those foreign examples. The comparison does not claim every history is identical; it shows that the moral test changes when the subject changes from “them” to “us.” That is the book's final practical move: apply one standard across national boundaries.

## Key Takeaways

1. National songs and citizenship rituals teach a version of history; they do not prove it.
2. Britain is not uniquely prone to national self-importance, but its imperial story gives that self-image a powerful inheritance.
3. Comparative history is useful only when it includes the home nation's own violence.
4. The enemy of yesterday can be the ally or family member of tomorrow; the useful historical question is what changed.
