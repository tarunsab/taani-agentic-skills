# Introduction: The flock, the wolf, and the idea of Britain

## Core Idea

The Introduction supplies the book's governing metaphor: people form “flocks” called nations, but internal disputes become secondary when a threatening “wolf-pack” appears. Shared danger can produce a leader, a common flag, and a stronger sense of belonging; victories can then feed self-belief and encourage a community to see itself as better than other flocks. Deary uses Britain to ask whether its reputation for being special is deserved and where that belief came from. His answer will be historical and deliberately irreverent: the actions of ancestors are tested against evidence, but patriotic stories are also exposed as selective memories. The practical lesson is to treat enemy-making as an identity technology with both cohesive and expansionist effects.

## Historical Sections

### Britannia as an invented guardian

The name Britain reaches back through the Celtic “Pretani,” while the Roman invasion helped produce Britannia, a warrior woman who could symbolize protection, defiance, or acceptance of Roman rule. Later symbols such as Trident and Rule, Britannia! reuse that image even though the song was written in 1740, when Britain did not yet command the seas in the simple way the lyrics claim. Deary places the song beside Shakespeare's “sceptred isle” language to show how cultural memory can turn a history of conquest into a story of invulnerability. The patriotic image is not evidence of ancient national continuity; it is a later construction that makes Britain appear naturally destined to rule.

### Britain was made by migration

The prehistoric story undercuts modern claims about a pure indigenous people. Humans crossed from continental Europe when land bridges existed, left and returned during ice ages, and later arrived as farmers and Beaker people who changed population and burial practices. The Introduction mentions long barrows, mortuary houses, henges, Stonehenge, Bronze Age trade, hill forts, and the growth of Celtic communities, but it repeatedly marks uncertainty about who exactly counted as “Celtic.” The point is not that every archaeological question is solved; it is that Britain has always been a place of movement, mixture, adaptation, and replacement. A modern policy that appeals to an original, unmixed “homeland” is therefore contradicted by the historical sequence the book presents.

### Celts, evidence, and the first enemy

Roman writers described Celtic sacrifice, sacred groves, severed heads, and wicker figures, but the Introduction warns that these accounts also served Roman propaganda by making the invader look civilized. Archaeology supports some practices, such as unusual burials and the preserved Lindow Man, while leaving the reasons uncertain; competing explanations include rebirth, containment of dangerous power, and saving scarce burial space. Even the Lindow Man's death is interpreted rather than observed directly, with evidence of repeated violence compatible with sacrifice but no definitive explanation. This is the book's first source-critical pattern: distinguish material evidence, hostile testimony, later legend, and a confident national story. Celtic tribalism is then presented as a vulnerability that Rome can exploit, though the later chapters show that every conquering power has its own violence.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Flock-and-wolves model**: Use when a group is defining itself through an outside threat; map what internal differences are being suppressed and who gains authority.
- **National myth audit**: Separate symbol, lyric, legend, and political use from the historical evidence that could support them.
- **Migration baseline**: Before accepting a purity or indigeneity claim, reconstruct who arrived, when, and what evidence establishes continuity.

## Key Concepts

- **Nation**: A human flock organized around shared place, language, and culture.
- **Britannia**: A personified island and later symbol of British protection and defiance.
- **Pretani**: A name the Introduction attributes to Celtic inhabitants of Great Britain.
- **Beaker people**: Later farming migrants associated with bell-shaped pottery and changes in Bronze Age Britain.
- **Lindow Man**: A preserved body used to discuss evidence and uncertainty about Celtic sacrifice.
- **Source bias**: The distortion introduced when evidence comes from a hostile, interested, or much later observer.

## Mental Models

- Use **enemy-defined identity** when a community becomes unusually cohesive during a crisis.
- Think of **national symbols as historical artifacts**, not transparent windows onto the past.
- Use **confidence intervals for stories**: record what is certain, plausible, disputed, and invented.

## Anti-patterns

- **Purity by nostalgia**: treating a mixed migration history as proof of an original unmixed nation.
- **Hostile-source literalism**: repeating an enemy's description of a rival as if it were neutral observation.
- **Victory-as-virtue**: assuming that survival or military success proves moral superiority.

## Worked Example

The Introduction's Stonehenge vignette models how to handle a famous object. Several explanations are listed, from an Egyptian connection to aliens, but the author says the purpose remains uncertain. The securely reported episode is that Cecil Chubb bought Stonehenge at auction in 1915 and gave it to the nation in 1918, receiving a knighthood. The example teaches a useful separation: uncertainty about why a monument was built does not prevent confidence about a later act of ownership and public transfer.

## Key Takeaways

1. Shared enemies can create nations, but they can also create superiority myths.
2. Britain is presented as a history of migration and mixture, not racial or cultural purity.
3. Keep archaeological evidence, hostile chronicles, legend, and later patriotic memory in separate columns.
4. The first enemy matters because it reveals the book's recurring question: did conflict unite Britain, or merely give later Britons a story about unity?
