# Chapter 7: United States

## Core Idea

The United States begins as a British settlement project and becomes a former colony that fights Britain twice. The chapter refuses to treat the American Revolution as a clean story of liberty: settlers displaced Native Americans, used slavery, accepted disease as a weapon, and fought other European empires before resisting British taxes. The immediate dispute involved military costs, monopoly, taxation, and colonial claims to rights, while French money and German mercenaries shaped the outcome. The War of 1812 then shows the new nation testing its military confidence against Britain, using privateers and alliances with pirates. By the twentieth century, common enemies made Britain and the United States allies, but the relationship also reflected American industrial power, profit, aid, and financial leverage rather than a simple cultural brotherhood.

## Historical Sections

### Colonization, disappearance, and Native dispossession

The failed Roanoke colony left only the word “Croatoan,” but later Jamestown settlers survived an equally severe crisis by eating dogs, rats, snakes, horses, and in one reported case a dead settler's wife. The Pilgrims arrived with shoes and boots but not the tools needed to farm or fish, so Native Americans supplied practical help. Colonial charters were granted to English lords without asking the people already living on the land, while European diseases reduced Virginia's Native population by around 90 per cent during the seventeenth century. The chapter also recounts deliberate biological warfare: General Jeffrey Amherst offered blankets from a smallpox hospital to Native American enemies in 1758. In King Philip's War and other conflicts, settlers and Native peoples massacred one another, but the unequal trajectory was westward expansion and near-destruction of Native communities.

### The road to independence

By the 1760s Britain controlled the east, France the middle, and Spain the west; the 1763 peace left Native Americans promised that British settlers would not move west, a promise the chapter treats as hollow. Colonists wanted local armies and political rights, while Britain wanted them to help pay for imperial defence through the Sugar Tax and Stamp Act. Troops in Boston made the conflict worse: after being stoned, soldiers killed five people in the event called the Boston Massacre, and competing accounts turned a street confrontation into moral theatre. The Tea Act then protected the East India Company's monopoly and tried to rescue the company after its policies had contributed to the Great Bengal Famine, in which seven to ten million Bengalis are said here to have died. The Boston Tea Party, the Coercive Acts, and the first fighting at Lexington and Concord made a tax and trade dispute into a war for political separation.

### War, alliances, and selective liberty

George Washington led the military rebellion, and the Declaration of Independence of 1776 announced that “all men are created equal” while, as the chapter notes, excluding Native Americans, black people, and women from its practical promise. The war involved sea attacks, farmers capturing the Margaretta, and the American Turtle, an early submarine whose gunpowder charge failed to sink the Eagle but frightened its crew. Britain offered freedom to enslaved people who joined its army, while the American side offered land to Hessian mercenaries who changed sides; French arms and money supplied the rebels because France wanted to weaken Britain. Britain recognized defeat in the Treaty of Paris in 1783, but the chapter's moral point is that revolutionary liberty coexisted with slavery, land seizure, and promises made over territory the rebels did not own. A national founding story can therefore contain both genuine constitutional change and exclusionary violence.

### The War of 1812 and hired outsiders

The United States declared war in 1812 amid expansion, British impressment of American sailors, and the wider struggle against Napoleon. Privateer Captain William Allen fought HMS Pelican after the Argos had captured 27 ships, but surrendered after losing his mast and leg. American attempts to take Canada led to a huge ammunition explosion at Toronto, and a British retaliation burned Washington's public buildings; the war formally ended in December 1814, but news arrived too late to stop the Battle of New Orleans. General Andrew Jackson enlisted the French pirate Jean Lafitte after rejecting and then revising earlier efforts to suppress him. Lafitte's men helped defend the city and received pardons, showing that the new republic could treat outlaw violence as useful when national survival required it.

### From enemy to indispensable ally

The chapter presents the War of 1812 as formative for American identity but not as a British unifying crusade; Britain had simultaneous conflicts with Spain, France, and domestic Luddites. The United States then expanded across Native land and took half a million square miles from Mexico in a war General Ulysses Grant called unjust. Cecil Rhodes's dream of reuniting Britain and America into one Anglo-Saxon empire failed, but two world wars created a durable “special” relationship. American industry and finance profited from both wars, and the Marshall Plan supplied over $15 billion to rebuild Western Europe after US bombing had destroyed much of the infrastructure. Britain's final repayment in 2006 makes the alliance a relationship of gratitude and dependence as well as shared language and memory.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Founding-myth double ledger**: Record the liberty a revolution claims alongside the groups excluded from it.
- **Settler-colonial sequence**: Charter land, ignore existing sovereignty, spread disease or violence, then narrate expansion as settlement.
- **Alliance-price model**: Include foreign money, mercenaries, pirates, and postwar debt in the cost of victory.
- **Special-relationship audit**: Distinguish cultural affinity from strategic alignment, industrial advantage, and financial dependence.

## Key Concepts

- **Roanoke**: Failed English colony remembered through the unexplained word “Croatoan.”
- **Jamestown**: Early colony whose survival crisis reveals the material fragility of settlement.
- **Great Bengal Famine**: 1770 famine linked in the chapter to East India Company priorities and failed relief.
- **Boston Massacre**: 1770 confrontation whose competing narratives intensified colonial resistance.
- **Boston Tea Party**: 1773 destruction of East India Company tea in protest against monopoly and taxation.
- **American Turtle**: Early American submarine used against a British warship.
- **Jean Lafitte**: Pirate whose force helped defend New Orleans after receiving a pardon.
- **Marshall Plan**: US postwar aid program that also positioned America as a financial centre of Western recovery.

## Mental Models

- Use **rights-versus-reach**: ask who is included in a universal claim and who is outside its political community.
- Think of **war as a coalition market**: states buy loyalty with freedom, land, money, weapons, or pardons.
- Use **aid-debt symmetry**: relief can rebuild a partner while also creating influence over its future choices.

## Anti-patterns

- **Revolutionary innocence**: assuming anti-imperial language cancels slavery or settler conquest.
- **Tax-only explanation**: reducing independence to a bill without the military, monopoly, famine, and sovereignty context.
- **Alliance sentimentality**: treating the later British-American relationship as equal friendship without tracing power and repayment.

## Worked Example

The Boston Tea Party connects economic policy to political identity. Britain used the Tea Act to rescue the East India Company and preserve its monopoly, even though the company's earlier extraction in Bengal had contributed to mass starvation. Colonists then destroyed 342 chests of tea, Britain imposed the Coercive Acts, and local protest became armed resistance. The example shows how a relatively narrow commercial measure can become explosive when people already distrust the authority imposing it.

## Key Takeaways

1. Colonial independence did not erase the violence and exclusion built into settlement.
2. British taxation was a trigger within a larger system of monopoly, war finance, Native dispossession, and slavery.
3. Revolutions depend on purchased and borrowed support as much as on declared ideals.
4. The later alliance with the United States united Britain against common enemies while placing Britain in long-term financial and strategic dependence.
