# Chapter 9: Ireland

## Core Idea

Ireland is the book's longest example of enemy-making becoming inherited structure. Ireland had sophisticated prehistoric cultures, Celtic kingdoms, and a distinctive Christian-monastic tradition, but Norman, English, and British rulers repeatedly converted political control, religion, land, and food into instruments of domination. Plantations and famine made conquest material rather than merely constitutional, while the Easter Rising, partition, the IRA, the Troubles, and Bloody Sunday carried the memory into the twentieth century. The chapter does not treat Irish violence as one-sided: Irish factions fought each other, attacked civilians, and sought foreign allies, while British repression, plantation, and institutional failure hardened resistance. Its final movement is toward peace, showing that even ancient hatreds can be reduced when political arrangements and religious certainties change.

## Historical Sections

### Early Ireland, saints, and kings

People settled Ireland 12,000–14,000 years ago, and Stone Age passage tombs at Newgrange, Knowth, and Dowth show engineering and astronomical skill older than Stonehenge or the pyramids. Celtic arrivals around 500–300 BC held power for roughly a millennium, organized into six kingdoms with warrior elites, while Rome never occupied Ireland as it occupied England. Palladius arrived as the first papal missionary in 431, followed by the much more celebrated Patrick; by 563, Columba helped export Irish Christianity into Scotland and England. Saints and monasteries also supported early hospitals, but monastic communities were not uniformly peaceful: wars between monasteries killed hundreds, and King Fedelmid robbed religious houses before monks helped kill him. The chapter's pattern is that a sacred institution can build cultural connection while remaining embedded in ordinary power struggles.

### Vikings, high kings, and Norman entry

Viking attacks began in 795 and desecrated monasteries, yet Irish political unity remained intermittent. By 846 Malachy MacMulrooney had emerged as a high king, but the title was not hereditary enough to stabilize the island. Strongbow's arrival in Leinster in 1170 gave Henry II a foothold, and Henry claimed papal support through Laudabiliter, a bull some historians think was forged or invented. Henry made his son John Lord of Ireland; Norman rulers built castles, roads, towns, and bridges and introduced English law, but they never conquered the whole island and their rule could be brutal and exploitative. The chapter therefore distinguishes infrastructure from legitimacy: building institutions can accompany occupation rather than consent.

### Famine, the Pale, and plantation

The famine beginning in 1315 combined relentless rain, failed crops, Scottish invasion, and the destruction or seizure of remaining food; chroniclers describe years of hunger, disease, cold, and later plague. In the fifteenth century, English authorities fortified Dublin as the Pale and described Irish people outside it as “wild” enemies, turning a geographic boundary into a racialized and political one. Tudor governments used executions, terror, and eventually plantations to replace Catholic Irish landowners with English and Scottish Protestant settlers. The Plantation of Ulster required fortified houses, agricultural clearance, and Protestant families; by the end of the seventeenth century more than 100,000 settlers had arrived, leaving a demographic and religious division that outlasted the policy. Land transfer, religion, and security thus reinforced one another in a cycle that made compromise harder.

### Cromwell and the Great Famine

Oliver Cromwell's nine-month campaign after the execution of Charles I became an enduring Irish memory. The chapter recounts massacres at Wexford and Drogheda, the killing of priests, the use of priestly clothing to mock victims, and the death of Catholic commander Sir Arthur Aston, who was beaten with his own wooden leg. English Catholics sometimes fought on the Irish side, but the dominant experience was coercive Protestant rule and resistance. The Great Famine of 1845 then exposed a different form of domination: absentee landlords collected rent, the British government responded slowly and inadequately, food continued to leave Ireland, and at least a million people died while another two million emigrated. Evictions, workhouses, typhus, and “coffin ships” turned administrative choices and market relations into national trauma.

### Rebellion, partition, and the Troubles

Wolfe Tone's United Irishmen drew Protestants and Catholics into a common anti-English movement inspired by the French Revolution; storms delayed French support, and Tone died after a failed attempt to cut his throat before execution. Robert Emmet's 1803 rebellion collapsed when only eighty men and one ladder appeared to attack Dublin Castle. Home Rule was delayed by the First World War, the 1916 Easter Rising was met with fifteen executions, and the 1919 settlement created an Irish Free State in the south while Northern Ireland remained in the United Kingdom. Catholics in Ulster faced discrimination, and the IRA's 1939–40 S-Plan bombed infrastructure in Britain, killing seven and injuring 962; the campaign hardened British resolve. The “Troubles” from 1968 involved the IRA, Loyalist groups, British troops, internment, and escalating violence; Bloody Sunday in 1972 killed fourteen Catholic protestors after soldiers fired 108 live rounds, and a later inquiry reversed the initial justification.

### Peace and the end of inherited hatred

The miscarriage of justice against Judith Ward, who spent seventeen years in prison after police withheld exonerating evidence, further damaged trust. The Good Friday Agreement of 1998 formally ended the conflict, although the Omagh bombing that August killed 29 people and strengthened the argument for peace. Deary closes with Ian Paisley and Gerry Adams, former enemies who met, shook hands, and formed a power-sharing government in 2007. The movement from Cromwell to power sharing does not excuse the violence or make the past irrelevant; it shows that political arrangements can change the incentives that keep identity locked into hostility. The chapter's practical conclusion is that the oldest enemy relationship can end when people stop treating inherited categories as destiny.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Occupation-to-memory chain**: Track how land, law, religion, food, and punishment turn conquest into an inherited grievance.
- **Plantation logic**: Treat settlement, demographic replacement, and religious policy as one security strategy rather than separate events.
- **Repression feedback loop**: Test whether executions, internment, or collective punishment weaken a movement or expand its support.
- **Peace-through-reclassification**: Look for institutional arrangements that let former enemies share power without requiring identical histories.

## Key Concepts

- **Laudabiliter**: Papal bull used by Henry II to justify intervention in Ireland; its authenticity is disputed.
- **Pale**: Fortified English-controlled zone around Dublin; source of the phrase “beyond the pale.”
- **Plantation of Ulster**: Transfer of Catholic Irish land to English and Scottish Protestant settlers.
- **Cromwellian conquest**: 1649 campaign remembered for massacres and coercive rule.
- **Great Famine**: 1845 disaster involving crop disease, landlord eviction, inadequate relief, and mass death/emigration.
- **Home Rule**: Demand for Irish self-government within or outside the United Kingdom.
- **Easter Rising**: 1916 republican revolt whose executions intensified anti-British feeling.
- **Bloody Sunday**: 1972 British Army shooting in Derry that killed fourteen Catholic protestors.
- **Good Friday Agreement**: 1998 settlement that formally ended the Troubles.

## Mental Models

- Use **land-plus-law analysis**: ask who owns the land, which law applies, and who can enforce it.
- Think of **demography as policy memory** when a settlement program changes the religious and political balance for generations.
- Use **reconciliation as a design problem**: make power sharing possible even when narratives remain incompatible.

## Anti-patterns

- **Single-cause Ireland**: explaining conflict only through religion or only through nationalism.
- **Infrastructure absolution**: treating roads, castles, or legal systems as proof that occupation was benevolent.
- **Security-through-collective-punishment**: assuming civilian suffering will remove political resistance.

## Worked Example

The Plantation of Ulster illustrates how a short-term security plan can create a durable conflict structure. Rebellious Catholic lords lost land; English and Scottish Protestant settlers received it and were required to bring families, clear farms, and build fortified houses. The policy was intended to pacify rebellion and spread Protestantism, but it also created a large settler population with different interests from the displaced Irish. Centuries later, the same demographic and religious boundary shaped Home Rule, partition, and the Troubles.

## Key Takeaways

1. Irish conflict cannot be understood without combining religion, land, law, famine, and political representation.
2. Repression and delayed justice often turn a local event into inherited national memory.
3. British and Irish violence both occurred, but unequal authority made British policy especially consequential.
4. The Paisley–Adams transition shows that enemies can become governing partners when institutions change the payoff to continued hostility.
