# Chapter 5: Spain

## Core Idea

Spain is remembered in Britain through the Armada, but the chapter begins by showing that British wealth was entangled with Spanish conquest and slavery. Columbus's voyages established a system of enslavement and extraction that Spanish colonizers expanded, while English traders and Elizabeth I later copied the profit model through Hawkins, Drake, and attacks on Spanish shipping. The Armada of 1588 was a real English success, but the chapter treats it as one episode in a longer sequence of failed Spanish invasions, weather accidents, political mistakes, and later British myth-making. Spain and Britain alternated between religious enemies, commercial competitors, and licensed partners in the slave trade. The practical lesson is to audit the moral and material costs hidden by a “finest hour” story.

## Historical Sections

### Columbus and the inherited business

Deary presents Columbus not as a neutral explorer but as an agent of Spanish wealth-seeking. His diary, as quoted in the book, focuses on finding gold, conquering people with a small force, and ruling them; kidnapped Arawak people were transported in terrible conditions, and forced labour and disease later destroyed their population. When American slaves became scarce, the Spanish turned to Africa, establishing a transatlantic trade that lasted centuries. Britain later became a major participant, and the 1792 distribution of slaving ships—22 in London, 42 in Bristol, and 131 in Liverpool—makes the wealth network concrete. The chapter insists that modern abhorrence of slavery must be applied to Britain's own enrichment, not used only to condemn Spain.

### Drake, Hawkins, and state-backed piracy

Francis Drake began with John Hawkins on early English slave-trading voyages, attacking Portuguese slave ships and selling people in the Spanish Caribbean. Elizabeth I then licensed Drake's 1577–80 circumnavigation, which functioned as a cover for raiding Spanish ports and galleons in the Pacific. The author describes Drake's use of terror against a captured sailor to obtain information, then notes that investors including Elizabeth may have received a return of 4,600 per cent; Drake was knighted in 1581. A second expedition in 1585 lost 750 men and returned less treasure than its cost, showing that piracy was a high-risk investment rather than effortless heroism. The distinction between pirate and patriot depended on which crown received the profit.

### Philip II and the Armada of 1588

Religious conflict sharpened the rivalry after Philip II's marriage to Mary Tudor and Elizabeth's Protestant succession. Philip supported plots involving Mary Queen of Scots, while Elizabeth's intelligence network helped expose them; Mary's execution removed a possible Catholic focus but did not remove Philip's desire to invade. The 1588 Armada had to meet the Duke of Parma's force in Flanders, and Philip's instructions prevented an early strike at vulnerable Plymouth or a secure English anchorage. English ships were smaller and faster, their large guns could fire more often, and the Spanish fleet was then scattered by fireships, damaged steering, explosions, ammunition shortages, and storms. Gravelines on 8 August and the northward journey around Scotland and Ireland ended the invasion, but the chapter says the result depended on operational mistakes and weather as well as English fighting.

### The Armada was not one event

Philip launched further armadas in 1596 and 1597, both wrecked or driven back by storms; a fourth reached Ireland in 1601 to support the Earl of Tyrone but surrendered after the rebellion failed. The Thirty Years' War then placed Britain and Spain within a wider Catholic-Protestant conflict that killed millions through combat, famine, plague, and destruction, while the Channel protected Britain from much of the continental suffering. Later, the 1713 asiento licensed British merchants to sell slaves in Spanish America, showing that former enemies could cooperate when profit aligned. The War of Jenkins' Ear grew from a disputed 1731 encounter in which Spanish commander Juan de León Fandiño injured or cut Captain Robert Jenkins's ear; Parliament revived the incident years later as a symbol of national insult. The British then attacked under Spanish colours and were defeated by the one-eyed, one-armed, one-legged Don Blas de Lezo, complicating any claim to uniquely fair play.

### Myth, welfare, and memory

The chapter ends by showing how national memory polishes the Armada. Drake's delayed bowls game, Elizabeth's Tilbury speech, and the exact timing of her appearance are treated as legends or later records rather than transparent eyewitness facts. The English fleet commander Lord Effingham reported that victorious sailors died in streets, barns, and outhouses because there was nowhere to house them; soldiers returned to unemployment. Gibraltar and future naval conflict keep Spain present as a possible enemy, but the Armada remains Britain's preferred heroic symbol. The memory is therefore selective twice over: it celebrates a successful defence while hiding the human cost, and it condemns Spanish imperial violence while benefiting from the same imperial economy.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Empire mirror**: When condemning an enemy's extraction, inspect whether one's own state copied or profited from the same system.
- **Heroic victory audit**: Reconstruct the result through logistics, weather, timing, weapons, allies, and post-battle treatment.
- **Enemy-to-market shift**: Watch for moments when ideological enemies make commercial agreements that reveal shared incentives.
- **Memory-cost ledger**: Pair every celebrated national victory with who was impoverished, sickened, enslaved, or forgotten.

## Key Concepts

- **Columbian slave system**: The Spanish conquest-and-enslavement model the chapter presents as a precursor to later Atlantic slavery.
- **Francis Drake**: English privateer, slave trader, circumnavigator, and raider celebrated by Elizabeth I.
- **Flota de Indias**: Spanish West Indies/Silver Fleet convoy system carrying colonial wealth.
- **Spanish Armada**: Philip II's 1588 invasion fleet, defeated by English action, logistical errors, and weather.
- **Fireships**: Burning vessels used to scatter the Spanish fleet at Calais.
- **Asiento**: Contract licensing British merchants to sell slaves in Spanish America.
- **War of Jenkins' Ear**: Anglo-Spanish conflict named after a disputed severed-ear incident.

## Mental Models

- Use **profit provenance**: trace money from conquest to investor, state, port, and public symbol.
- Think of **weather as an actor** in maritime war, not background scenery.
- Use **myth delay**: ask how long after an event a famous speech or anecdote was first recorded.

## Anti-patterns

- **Armada monoculture**: treating 1588 as Spain's only invasion attempt or Britain's automatic command of the seas.
- **Privateer sanitization**: calling state-approved piracy clean because a monarch shared the proceeds.
- **Selective moralism**: condemning Spanish slavery while omitting British participation in the same trade.

## Worked Example

The 1588 Armada shows how to reconstruct a national “finest hour.” Spain had a large fleet and a plan to join Parma, but command restrictions prevented an early strike, English ships harassed the rear, fireships forced panic, and damaged anchors left the fleet exposed. Wind and storms then drove the retreat around Scotland and Ireland. The English victory is real, but the worked sequence shows why “British weather saved us” is incomplete: Spanish planning, English naval design, and chance interacted.

## Key Takeaways

1. Spanish conquest provided models and wealth streams that British traders later exploited.
2. Drake's patriotism and piracy were the same activity viewed from different national positions.
3. The Armada's outcome was strategic, tactical, logistical, and meteorological—not simply a proof of national character.
4. National pride often preserves the victory and discards the suffering of the people who achieved it.
