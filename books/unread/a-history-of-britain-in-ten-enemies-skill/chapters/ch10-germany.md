# Chapter 10: Germany

## Core Idea

Germany is the enemy most modern Britons are likely to name, but the chapter begins by showing that Germanic people had also settled Britain and later supplied its ruling dynasty. The modern German state formed in 1871 through a common enemy, France, and immediately carried a militarized imperial ambition; technology that produced cars also powered airships, aircraft, and war. German hostility in the First and Second World Wars united Britain, yet the chapter repeatedly questions the national character those wars are said to prove. “Stiff upper lip,” “not cricket,” Dunkirk, the Battle of Britain, and Blitz Spirit all preserve real courage while potentially hiding error, unequal shelter, fear, crime, and civilian suffering. The final image, German goalkeeper Bert Trautmann accepted by Manchester City supporters, shows how an enemy category can dissolve through ordinary contact and shared excellence.

## Historical Sections

### Unification and the German state

Before 1871, German-speaking principalities and cities were loosely joined in a German Confederation, but a common culture and especially hostility toward France created a stronger flock. Wilhelm I was proclaimed German Emperor at Versailles in January 1871 after the Franco-Prussian War, giving the new state an imperial tone from birth. The chapter balances militarism with German invention: Karl Benz produced a practical automobile, but Bertha Benz supplied the courage and technical improvements that made the machine usable, while German law prevented her from receiving full credit. The same technical capacity was absorbed by a state organized around expansion and armed force. Zeppelin bombers and fighter aircraft turned innovation into a new threat to Britain.

### Germans already ruling Britain

Britain's ruling families had long come from outside England: Welsh Tudors, Scottish Stuarts, and finally the Hanoverian Georges after Queen Anne died without a surviving heir. The Union Jack joined English and Scottish symbols, the Act of Union of 1707 joined Scotland to England and Wales, and the 1800 act added Ireland's red saltire to the United Kingdom. Queen Anne's pregnancies and the death of her last surviving child opened the succession to George I of Hanover, returning a German royal line to the British throne. The Georgian period still contained Jacobite rebellion, slavery, smuggling, poverty, and violent class divisions, so dynastic continuity did not equal social unity. Queen Victoria's Saxe-Coburg-Gotha family later made the First World War an especially awkward conflict with her grandson Wilhelm II.

### Wilhelm II and the road to war

Wilhelm II was ambitious, insecure about his withered arm, willing to order troops to fire on striking workers, and jealous of British global power. Germany's authoritarian constitution gave the emperor control over the chancellor, armed forces, and foreign policy, making an unstable leader unusually consequential. Wilhelm I's son Frederick, married to Queen Victoria's liberal daughter, might have opposed military expansion and persecution of Jews, but cancer ended his reign after 99 days. Wilhelm II then strengthened the militaristic path, while France and Britain answered with the Entente Cordiale of 1904. By 1914, Central Powers and Allies had formed rival armed blocs; the assassination of Archduke Ferdinand by Gavrilo Princip triggered a chain of declarations in which Austria, Germany, Russia, France, and Britain entered the war.

### War as school sport and the Tandey counterfactual

Newbolt's poem Vitai Lampada made cricket a metaphor for courage, telling soldiers to “play up” and “play the game” even as the battlefield became a desert of bodies and machine-gun fire. Newbolt later called the poem a Frankenstein's monster, and trench poets such as Wilfred Owen replaced the sporting ideal with a view of war as disillusionment. Private Henry Tandey's documented Victoria Cross action shows genuine bravery, but the chapter adds a famous counterfactual: Tandey allegedly spared a wounded German soldier in 1918, and the soldier was Adolf Hitler. The source's causal claim is presented as a dramatic possibility rather than a proven explanation of later history; Hitler's bullet was not fired because Tandey followed a sporting ethic. The episode is useful precisely because it exposes the danger of turning a moral rule into an absolute when future consequences are unknowable.

### Appeasement, Blitzkrieg, and the RAF

Germany's defeat in 1918 was followed by a myth that internal traitors, not Allied victory, had caused the surrender. Hitler's hatred of Jews was explicit by 1922, but Britain and other powers were slow to treat him as the threat his own words described. Hitler's alliance with Italy and Spain and demand for the Sudetenland met Neville Chamberlain's 1938 appeasement and promise of “peace for our time”; Churchill warned that Britain had chosen shame and would get war. Blitzkrieg combined bombing and tanks to overwhelm Poland, then France, but Operation Sea Lion required control of the air and Channel, which Germany failed to secure. Churchill's appointment in May 1940, the RAF's victory on Battle of Britain Day, and Hitler's postponement of invasion created the central national story of standing alone.

### Blitz Spirit, inequality, and reconciliation

The Blitz generated real endurance, as in the Cardiff child who sang the national anthem while trapped under a staircase, but the chapter asks whether the spirit was universal. Poor families sheltered in Anderson shelters while wealthy people used the Dorchester's cellars and were served whisky; bomb-chasers looted, black markets flourished, and reports from Coventry recorded hysteria, terror, and helplessness. Evacuated children could encounter kindness, but some were abused, hungry, infested, or pressured to write falsely positive letters home. Around 43,000 civilians were killed by the end of the Blitz in May 1941, and wartime crime included the serial murders of Evelyn Hamilton and Evelyn Oatley. Bert Trautmann's postwar career as a German Manchester City goalkeeper, including playing through a broken neck in the 1956 FA Cup Final, helped British audiences see a German as a respected fellow citizen; memory of wartime suffering remains, but contact can reclassify the former enemy.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Common-enemy state formation**: Use when a new state turns external rivalry into internal political consolidation.
- **Technology-to-threat chain**: Trace how a civilian invention is redirected by institutions and incentives toward military power.
- **Patriotic virtue audit**: Separate courage and endurance from the policies, class relations, and command errors surrounding them.
- **Counterfactual humility**: Treat “what if” stories as prompts to examine contingency, not as proof of a single causal line.
- **Enemy reclassification**: Look for ordinary institutions—sport, work, neighbourhoods—that allow former enemies to become peers.

## Key Concepts

- **German Empire**: State proclaimed in 1871 after Prussian victory and German unification.
- **Saxe-Coburg-Gotha / Hanoverians**: German dynastic lines that ruled Britain.
- **Entente Cordiale**: 1904 British-French agreement against the background of German expansion.
- **Central Powers / Allies**: Rival coalitions in the First World War.
- **Vitai Lampada**: Newbolt poem that turns cricket into a model for military courage.
- **Appeasement**: British policy of conceding the Sudetenland in an effort to avoid war.
- **Blitzkrieg**: German “Lightning War” combining air attack and armour for rapid defeat.
- **Operation Sea Lion**: Planned German invasion of Britain requiring air and naval superiority.
- **Blitz Spirit**: Postwar national memory of civilian endurance under bombing.
- **Bert Trautmann**: German former paratrooper and Manchester City goalkeeper whose career softened anti-German feeling.

## Mental Models

- Use **family-versus-state separation**: kinship can influence diplomacy, but public policy also responds to domestic legitimacy and security.
- Think of **national character as a retrospective collage** assembled from poems, speeches, and selected survivors.
- Use **counterfactual guardrails**: mark the evidence for an anecdote separately from the larger causal story.

## Anti-patterns

- **Single-enemy genealogy**: assuming Germany was always Britain's enemy when German dynasties ruled Britain.
- **Stiff-upper-lip absolutism**: treating emotional control as universal and ignoring panic, grief, exploitation, and inequality.
- **Heroic causality**: turning one person's choice into proof that a whole later catastrophe had one cause.

## Worked Example

The Blitz Spirit test compares three kinds of evidence. A trapped Cardiff child singing the national anthem shows a moving instance of endurance; Dorchester shelters and black-market profiteering show that class and opportunism persisted; Mass-Observation reports from Coventry record terror and helplessness rather than calm defiance. Together these cases do not disprove courage. They show that a national label can be partly true while becoming misleading when it is treated as the complete experience.

## Key Takeaways

1. Germany's common enemy, France, helped create a militarized nation just as Germanic enemies helped create earlier British identities.
2. Technology becomes a threat through political institutions, not through invention alone.
3. Poems and myths can turn blunders, inequality, and fear into a single national virtue.
4. Trautmann's career demonstrates that shared activity can dissolve an enemy category faster than official reconciliation.
