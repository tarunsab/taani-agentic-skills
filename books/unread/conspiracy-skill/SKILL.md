## Core frameworks

### Conspiracy as agency under constraint

Holiday defines conspiracy broadly as determined people coordinating in secret to disrupt an established order or accomplish a difficult aim. The method is neutral: it can serve justice, revenge, reform, or abuse. The narrative moves through planning, doing, and aftermath, and repeatedly asks the reader to distinguish a tactic from the purpose it serves. The first practical move is therefore to name the actual objective, the existing power that blocks it, and the cost of leaving the situation unchanged.

### Decide to act, then choose the terrain

An apparently impossible fight can become thinkable when the principal stops treating the opponent's preferred contest as the only contest. Thiel considers financial and reputational exposure, possible expected value, fear, honor, and self-interest before committing resources. The book's “back door” is the line of least resistance and least expectation: work backward from the desired end state and look for a lawful route the opponent has neglected. This is not permission to hunt loopholes without merit; the route must be defensible, and a clever entry point cannot repair a weak underlying claim.

### High agency with moral friction

High agency means refusing to accept “nothing can be done” as a final fact. It requires testing constraints, finding allies, and acting through available institutions rather than waiting for permission from the dominant player. Yet the planning chapters also describe a psychological cost: effective action can require suppressing empathy, hardening against collateral discomfort, and turning a person into a means. Holiday uses Gawker's own indifference and the risk of becoming like one's enemy as warnings; agency without compassion can reproduce the cruelty it opposes.

### Build a small, capable team

The Berlin dinner illustrates the “briefcase” model: one principal gives a trusted operator enough authority, resources, and time to execute, while specialists handle distinct functions. Compartmentalization reduces leaks and coordination overhead, but it also creates blind spots and makes moral accountability harder to distribute. The useful selection rule is hunger plus competence, not prestige or the largest possible coalition. A small team can move quickly when roles, decision rights, communication rules, and the stopping condition are explicit.

### Work backward from the endgame

Holiday repeatedly invokes chess endgames and Eisenhower's hidden hand to emphasize patience. Instead of asking only how to win the next exchange, define the position that counts as success, the opponent's possible exits, and the actions that would make success self-defeating. The search for Hogan as a plaintiff exemplifies fit: the facts were unusually strong, the person had a direct claim, and the case could establish a broader boundary around privacy. Legal leverage is powerful precisely because it converts private resources into public process, but it must not be confused with guaranteed justice or with immunity from public criticism.

### Prepare for friction and setbacks

The doing phase includes denied injunctions, copyright complications, an FBI extortion sting, venue changes, delays, discovery, and the slow emotional “trough of sorrow.” Holiday's operating rule is that a setback should update the plan rather than define the result. Redundancy, reserves, alternate legal routes, and a willingness to advance two steps while losing one preserve optionality. Obstacles also reveal the opponent's assumptions and expose which parts of the plan were wishful thinking.

### Know the opponent better than the opponent knows you

Gawker's leaders treated the case as another easy media fight because they did not model Thiel's resources, patience, or motivation. The plaintiffs gained information from depositions, prior statements, corporate structure, and local jury research, while Gawker had little experience preparing its own key people for depositions. Opponent modeling means understanding incentives, identity, money, time horizon, relationships, and likely reactions—not merely collecting embarrassing facts. The limit is that intelligence can become dehumanization; a good model still needs uncertainty, evidence, and a refusal to invent motives.

### Secrecy, deception, and the boundary of legitimacy

Secrecy protects a plan while it is vulnerable; compartmentalization and a cutout can keep a principal from becoming the immediate target. Active confusion (“maskirovka”) can force an opponent to spend resources on several plausible fronts, while a clear main thrust keeps effort from dissipating. These tools create ethical and practical liabilities: a secret can leak, deception can injure bystanders, and a tactic that cannot survive exposure may be indefensible. The test is not whether secrecy is automatically good, but whether the action remains lawful, proportionate, truthfully explainable, and worth its foreseeable consequences.

### Use pressure without losing the main objective

The multi-front campaign against Gawker uses side claims and public confusion as pressure, but the Hogan action remains the decisive thrust. Pressure is useful when it changes the opponent's choices, exposes contradictions, or consumes attention; it is wasteful when it becomes revenge for its own sake. A campaign should therefore label probes, leverage, and the objective separately, then stop pinpricks once they no longer improve the endgame. The same discipline appears in negotiation: cornering an enemy can make peace harder, so preserve a paved retreat when the objective is secured.

### Culture and incentives determine cohesion

Strategy fails inside organizations when the local culture rewards behavior that defeats the plan. Gawker's creed—publish if true—combined with low pay, weak supervision, page-view incentives, and a status contest against critics; the result was bravado where caution was needed. A coalition can also hold together through shared hatred or a shared image of evil, but that bond may fracture when the target is no longer available or when an apology becomes strategically necessary. Audit incentives, not only stated values, and design dissent, accountability, and exit conditions before pressure begins.

### Win the audience, not merely the argument

The trial turns “kayfabe” into a “shoot”: routine legal posturing becomes an irreversible contest judged by people. Local counsel, Hogan's vulnerable testimony, the concrete fact of a secretly recorded intimate act, and A.J. Daulerio's callous remarks made the legal issue legible to a jury. A technically correct rule can lose if the messenger feels alien or the facts feel cruel. In any human decision, match the messenger and evidence to the audience, distinguish law from sympathy, and prepare for what the room will remember.

### Treat victory as a transition

After the verdict, enforcement, reputation, secrecy, retaliation, and settlement become a new game. A win can be undermined by a missing appeal bond, a leaking secret, a badly explained motive, or a failure to give a defeated opponent a dignified exit. Holiday's account of the settlement uses reciprocal violence and “paved retreat” to show why deterrence is different from annihilation. Decide in advance what is enough, what must be repaired, and which remaining value should be preserved.

### Audit second- and third-order effects

The book closes by resisting a single moral verdict. Gawker.com stops publishing and privacy norms change, but a billionaire's role in bankrupting a publisher raises chilling-effect concerns, the method is copied by movements with weaker ethical limits, and power can become addictive. A useful postmortem asks what was intended, what was foreseeable, what was genuinely surprising, who benefited, who paid, and what new capability was released. The final discipline is humility: learn the tactics, judge the ends, keep compassion, and name the stopping point.
