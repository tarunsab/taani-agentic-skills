# Introduction

## Core Idea

Holiday uses Peter Thiel’s copy of Machiavelli’s *Discourses on Livy*, especially its chapter on conspiracies, as the intellectual key to a real conflict between Thiel, Nick Denton’s Gawker Media, and Terry Bollea (Hulk Hogan). Machiavelli’s point, as Holiday presents it, is not that intrigue is admirable by definition but that some situations leave people with no ordinary route to change an entrenched order. The book follows a hidden campaign that began after Gawker publicized Thiel’s sexuality, found its decisive vehicle in Hogan’s lawsuit over a secretly recorded sex tape, and ended with a Florida verdict that helped destroy Gawker. The account is deliberately both factual and strategic: it asks how a conspiracy is organized, funded, staffed, kept secret, tested, and concluded, while also showing what it feels like to be the target who cannot see the campaign. Its three parts—planning, doing, and aftermath—map distinct requirements, from recruitment and legal positioning to setbacks, secrecy, public persuasion, settlement, and consequences. Holiday does not offer a clean moral endorsement; he keeps the reader inside the tension between privacy and free speech, justice and revenge, agency and abuse, and victory and blowback.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Machiavelli’s three-phase model**: Planning, doing, aftermath.
  - When to use: For any long campaign whose visible action is only one part of the work.
  - How: Build the people and plan first; execute through friction and changing conditions; prepare to hold, explain, and end the result.
- **Actual conspiracy versus theory**: An actual conspiracy is supported by coordinated acts and evidence, not imaginative claims about hidden actors.
  - When to use: When distinguishing a documented campaign from rumor or hindsight.
  - How: Track actors, resources, decisions, timing, and observable effects.

## Key Concepts

- **Planning**: Organization, recruitment, funding, selection, and preparation.
- **Doing**: Action under uncertainty, secrecy, opposition, and friction.
- **Aftermath**: The new strategic and moral situation created by success or failure.
- **Privacy**: The protected space Thiel says enables eccentricity and independent thought.
- **Hubris**: Confidence that turns past success into blindness about a new opponent.
- **Public narrative**: The story audiences use to assign legitimacy after an event.

## Mental Models

- Think of the conflict as **a chess game with more than one board**: winning the case does not automatically win the public story or the settlement.
- Use **the capability-and-cost lens**: ask what power, conviction, and organization look like in concrete behavior, and what they cost the people involved.
- Treat **the opponent’s self-description as evidence, not truth**; Gawker’s belief in its invulnerability becomes one of its liabilities.

## Anti-patterns

- **Chronology without mechanism**: Listing events misses the choices, incentives, and asymmetries that made the outcome possible.
- **Moral simplification**: Calling one side purely heroic or villainous erases the privacy, speech, ego, and unintended-consequence conflicts the case exposes.
- **Confusing money with agency**: Thiel’s resources matter, but Holiday repeatedly attributes the decisive edge to patience, ideas, people, and execution.

## Worked Example

The introduction juxtaposes two apartments: Thiel’s, where Machiavelli and a chess set sit near the planning of the campaign, and Denton’s, where Seneca offers consolation after Gawker’s collapse. The juxtaposition is not evidence that books caused the conflict; it shows two people interpreting power differently. Thiel sees a plan to reassert agency and protect privacy, while Denton experiences the consequences of an opponent he had underestimated. The verdict—$115 million initially, followed by punitive damages—makes the abstract argument visible: a small group can use a legal process to impose a systemic consequence on a powerful publisher.

## Key Takeaways

1. Read the book as a case study in intentional action, not a general license for covert behavior.
2. Keep the three phases distinct; each has different failure modes.
3. Evaluate both sides: the strategist’s patience and the target’s hubris.
4. Expect the aftermath to change the meaning of the original victory.
