# Chapter 3: Turning to Conspiracy

## Core Idea

Machiavelli’s “special means” become relevant when the weaker party cannot win through ordinary power, publicity, or negotiation. From 2007 to 2011, Thiel tried those ordinary routes: he asked peers for ideas, accepted advice to develop thicker skin or feed Gawker gossip, consulted the fixer Eddie Hayes, met journalists, donated more than $1 million to the Committee to Protect Journalists, and even considered buying Gawker with Sean Parker. These efforts exposed a structural mismatch: Gawker’s defenses were not simply legal but cultural, because it had made itself difficult to shame, bargain with, or intimidate. Its public self-mockery and radical honesty worked like an abatis, an intimidating line of defense that made critics hesitate while preserving Gawker’s underdog image. Thiel’s high-agency response was to reject “there is nothing we can do” as a final answer and ask whether the normal playbook itself was the problem. Holiday emphasizes that high agency does not make a person right; it makes the person willing to test impossibility rather than accept it. The transition to conspiracy occurs when Thiel concludes that a direct war of words would only give Gawker the terrain it already dominated, so he chooses a hidden, coordinated legal campaign designed to alter the power balance.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **High-agency person**: Someone for whom “you can’t” begins an investigation rather than ending the conversation.
  - When to use: When institutions, peers, or conventional wisdom declare a problem unchangeable.
  - How: Test the stated constraint, locate the actual bottleneck, and build a route that does not depend on the opponent’s preferred terrain.
- **The abatis of public shamelessness**: Preemptive self-snark and radical openness can make an opponent hard to embarrass.
  - When to use: To diagnose why criticism, exposure, or conventional reputational pressure is failing.
  - How: Identify which social sanctions the target has already neutralized and which forms of leverage remain.
- **Buy-or-bypass test**: Consider acquisition, negotiation, publicity, or a new mechanism, then reject routes that reward or strengthen the behavior being opposed.

## Key Concepts

- **Special means**: Unusual methods used by a weaker party to change an unfavorable balance.
- **High agency**: The refusal to treat impossibility claims as conclusive.
- **Abatis**: A defensive obstacle; here, Gawker’s shameless public posture.
- **Leverage**: A pressure point that can change an opponent’s incentives.
- **Streisand Effect**: Attention increased by an attempted suppression of information.
- **Power differential**: The asymmetry that makes normal confrontation ineffective.
- **Mimetic desire**: Girard’s idea that people want what others want, creating conflict through imitation.

## Mental Models

- Use **the terrain test**: if the opponent wins at speech, reputation, or speed, do not assume more of the same will work.
- Think of **“nothing can be done” as a prompt to locate the bottleneck**—money, legal doctrine, access, information, or will.
- Use **the reward test** before buying or negotiating: would success pay the adversary for the conduct you want to stop?

## Anti-patterns

- **Calling an entrenched target shameless and stopping there**: Diagnosis without a remaining point of leverage produces helplessness.
- **Mistaking visibility for influence**: A public argument may give the opponent more content and attention without changing behavior.
- **Confusing high agency with omnipotence**: Thiel’s initiative succeeds only because he pairs it with patient research, capable operators, and a legal opening.

## Worked Example

After conversations with Hayes, Choire Sicha, Ryan Tate, Sean Parker, and others, Thiel finds that no one can offer a normal solution. Buying Gawker would reward Denton and would not guarantee a change in the underlying culture. A smear campaign would fail because Denton had made public shamelessness a shield. The young operative later called Mr. A therefore helps Thiel formulate a different plan: assemble legal and investigative capacity, search Gawker’s enormous archive for a meritorious cause of action, and proceed without announcing the financier. The plan is born not from certainty about the case but from the conclusion that ordinary channels cannot supply leverage.

## Key Takeaways

1. Identify the opponent’s preferred terrain and avoid fighting where its advantages are strongest.
2. Treat high agency as a question-making habit, not proof of moral or strategic correctness.
3. Reject options that reward the behavior you want to end.
4. A conspiracy begins when a problem, a power mismatch, and a hidden route converge.
