# Chapter 4: Assembling the Team

## Core Idea

Machiavelli’s distinction is simple: a lone act may be a crime, but a planned act with collaborators is a conspiracy. The first strategic move is therefore combining with the right people—people who can execute, who share enough of the problem, and who can be trusted with only the part of the plan they need. At a Berlin dinner in April 2011, the young man Holiday calls Mr. A uses the “briefcase technique”: he arrives with a researched proposal, timeline, budget, and a concrete route for attacking Gawker through legal causes of action. Thiel is attracted not only to the plan but to Mr. A’s ambition, confidence, and ability to frame action as a challenge to the assumption that nothing could be done. The team then adds Charles Harder, a hungry but experienced lawyer, through a recruitment process that tests motives without disclosing the financier. Holiday presents the resulting arrangement as a small venture: Thiel supplies capital and vision, Mr. A operates as president-like coordinator, and Harder executes the legal work. Its power comes from the plenipotentiary model—trusted representatives empowered to act on a principal’s behalf—but its risk is equally clear: every added ally expands capability and increases the chance of exposure.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Briefcase technique**: Bring a complete, concrete proposal to a powerful decision-maker instead of vague ideas.
  - When to use: When trying to convert someone’s latent frustration into a committed project.
  - How: Arrive with the problem, proposed mechanism, people, timeline, budget, and first steps already researched.
- **Professional son**: An ambitious younger operator makes himself useful to an older power-holder by offering energy, loyalty, and a vehicle for legacy.
  - When to use: To understand mentorship alliances where personal need and strategic utility overlap.
  - Limit: The relationship can become instrumental on both sides; ambition is not the same as shared ethics.
- **Plenipotentiary model**: A principal creates the vision and delegates execution to trusted agents whose judgment is close enough to his own.
  - When to use: When the principal cannot personally handle every specialized decision or location.
  - How: Match responsibility to competence, preserve a clear chain of trust, and reserve only high-consequence decisions for the principal.
- **Minimum viable conspiracy**: Add enough specialized capability to execute, but no more people than the plan requires.

## Key Concepts

- **Coconspirator**: A collaborator who turns an individual intention into coordinated action.
- **Briefcase technique**: A prepared, full-stack pitch that demonstrates usefulness.
- **Compartmentalization**: Restricting knowledge and decisions to role-appropriate slices.
- **Cutout**: An intermediary who separates the principal from the visible operation.
- **Hunger**: The willingness to bet on oneself and pursue an uncertain result.
- **Competence**: The practical ability needed to turn hunger into execution.
- **Plenipotentiary**: A trusted representative empowered to act for a principal.
- **Shared loneliness**: A bond formed when people feel unseen or powerless and recognize the same condition in one another.

## Mental Models

- Use **the capability-versus-exposure trade-off**: every new person adds skills and another possible leak.
- Think of **a team as a small company**: capital, operating leadership, specialized execution, incentives, and governance still matter.
- Use **role clarity** when collaborators have different motives; shared purpose need not mean identical reasons.

## Anti-patterns

- **Recruiting only for loyalty**: Loyalty without competence cannot execute a complex campaign.
- **Recruiting only for hunger**: Ambitious people may treat the operation as a vehicle for their own status.
- **Over-sharing the plan**: Giving every participant the whole picture increases challenge, confusion, and exposure.
- **Assuming paid work resolves ethical conflict**: Compartmentalization limits knowledge; it does not erase responsibility.

## Worked Example

Mr. A’s Berlin pitch proposes a shell company that would hire investigators and lawyers to search Gawker’s archive for viable claims, with a three-to-five-year horizon and a $10 million budget. Thiel initially answers with the learned helplessness Mr. A has heard from everyone else, but Mr. A asks what the world would look like if everyone accepted that conclusion. Thiel commits, then Mr. A tests law firms until he finds Charles Harder, presenting the backers as “wealthy individuals” rather than naming Thiel. Harder sees both a difficult mission and billable work, while Thiel sees a representative who can go places he cannot. The alliance works because each man’s immediate motive is different but compatible with the shared objective.

## Key Takeaways

1. Convert a vague grievance into a prepared proposal with a first executable step.
2. Select for the combination of hunger, competence, and judgment.
3. Keep the team small enough that trust and secrecy remain manageable.
4. Delegate execution while retaining clarity about the principal’s objective and limits.
