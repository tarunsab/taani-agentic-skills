# Chapter 13: The Testing of Faith

## Core Idea

By 2015 the Hogan case has become a test of endurance as much as law: more than twenty-five depositions, roughly 25,000 pages of filings, countless hearings, investigations, travel, and a special magistrate costing nearly $1 million. The case survives motions to dismiss, summary judgment attempts, the federal-to-state shift, and years of tit-for-tat discovery over medical records, finances, marriage, expert testimony, and the FBI sting. When the trial is postponed four days before it begins, the conspirators experience the most dangerous kind of delay: they had prepared psychologically for victory and now must carry the same uncertainty for nine more months. Gawker’s attrition strategy is designed to create precisely this doubt, telling Hogan that he will receive no justice because he will run out of money. Thiel counters by keeping the team focused on a few decisive steps rather than multiplying every procedural risk into a probability that appears impossible. The delayed trial is followed by another crisis when Gawker publishes the outing of a married Condé Nast executive and then Hogan’s racist comments from the sting become public, destroying Hogan’s reputation just as he needs to remain a sympathetic plaintiff. Instead of settling or quitting, Hogan becomes more locked in because his career, allies, and remaining public identity have already been lost; he now has no route out except through the case. The chapter’s lesson is that faith is not optimism: it is the ability to continue while the evidence repeatedly challenges the original timeline, without confusing persistence with proof that the objective is right.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Hofstadter’s Law**: A project takes longer than expected, even when the estimate includes that tendency.
  - When to use: In planning long legal, technical, or organizational campaigns.
  - How: Build time and morale reserves, expect late-stage delay, and avoid treating the original date as part of the objective.
- **Trough between buckets**: A campaign is vulnerable during the pause between one push and the next, when momentum and bonds evaporate.
  - When to use: After a near-victory is delayed or a major action stops.
  - How: Reconfirm purpose, reassess the case, and deliberately restore the next operational rhythm.
- **Lick ’em tomorrow**: Grant’s posture of reassembling after a disastrous day and returning with renewed effort.
- **No way out but through**: Once every alternative has been exhausted or destroyed, commitment can become stronger because retreat no longer offers relief.

## Key Concepts

- **Attrition**: Wearing down the opponent through delay, cost, and repeated procedural demands.
- **Second-guessing**: Doubt that follows a plan’s failure to meet its expected timeline.
- **Morale**: The emotional capacity of a coalition to continue after setbacks.
- **Discovery burden**: The time and cost of producing, reviewing, and contesting evidence.
- **Late-stage crisis**: A setback that arrives after the team believes victory is near.
- **Locked-in commitment**: Persistence intensified because the cost of retreat has become too high.
- **Rational self-interest**: Gawker’s assumption that a ruined plaintiff will eventually settle.

## Mental Models

- Use **the next-bucket test**: after a delay, specify the next concrete action that restores motion.
- Think of **commitment as path-dependent**; exposure and sunk costs can strengthen resolve while also narrowing judgment.
- Use **the faith-versus-evidence distinction**: endurance keeps a plan alive but does not validate it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating a scheduled date as a guarantee**: Legal and organizational friction can move the finish line at the last moment.
- **Assuming humiliation makes an opponent rationally compliant**: Exposure can remove the very exit the opponent is counting on.
- **Equating persistence with correctness**: A team can continue because it has no acceptable alternative, not because the strategy is sound.

## Worked Example

The trial is set for July 6, 2015, after years of work, and Mr. A prepares a New York base near bankruptcy counsel. On July 2, an appellate ruling delays the trial for nine months. The team must choose whether to keep funding an expensive case whose schedule has already defeated its forecasts. Thiel is asked directly whether to continue and says yes. Days later, Gawker’s publication of the Condé Nast executive’s private life and the leak of Hogan’s racist comments create public disasters for both sides, but Hogan cannot accept Gawker’s settlement logic after losing his endorsements and status. The delay and scandal do not end the campaign; they change the incentives that make retreat possible.

## Key Takeaways

1. Design morale and time reserves for late, demoralizing delays.
2. After a pause, restore a specific next action rather than relying on enthusiasm.
3. Check whether commitment is evidence-based or simply the result of having no exit.
4. Expect the target’s pressure tactics to change your own coalition’s incentives.
