# A Word: Conspiracy as a Method

## Core Idea

Holiday defines “conspiracy” as a neutral method rather than a synonym for a fantasy or a crime: determined, coordinated action carried out secretly to disrupt the status quo or accomplish an aim. The point of the definition is practical—if people assume that complicated plans cannot be executed, they mistake designed outcomes for inevitable events and become easier to manipulate. The 1919 World Series illustrates the blindness: spectators experienced the result as something that merely happened, while Arnold Rothstein had engineered it, and Dwight Eisenhower later treated the revelation as a lesson to distrust first appearances. Holiday’s larger claim is that agency, planning, patience, and coordination are underused because modern culture is more comfortable with public complaint than with intentional action. He also warns that the same machinery can serve good, terrible, or mixed purposes; the word does not settle the moral question. The book therefore asks the reader to study how power is organized while deciding independently what ends are worth pursuing.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Neutral method, non-neutral application**: Separate the mechanics of coordinated action from the moral evaluation of its goal and consequences.
  - When to use: When a proposed strategy is being dismissed merely because it is secret, unusual, or associated with a bad example.
  - How: Define the aim, the coordination, the secrecy, the disruption sought, and then judge the purpose and likely effects separately.
- **Three phases of conspiracy**: Planning, doing, and aftermath require different skills.
  - When to use: When a long campaign appears to be one problem; divide it into design, execution, and post-action control.
  - How: Plan organization and recruitment; execute with strategy, secrecy, and resilience; then manage public meaning, settlement, and consequences.

## Key Concepts

- **Conspiracy**: Secret, coordinated, determined action directed at a goal.
- **Intrigue**: Strategic coordination to alter an existing order.
- **Agency**: The belief that people can intentionally shape events.
- **Status quo**: The arrangement a plot seeks to preserve, disrupt, or replace.
- **First appearances**: The surface explanation that can hide deliberate design.
- **Complacency**: The passive acceptance of “that is just how things are.”

## Mental Models

- Use **the Rothstein/Eisenhower lens** when an outcome seems inevitable; ask who benefited from it and what hidden coordination could explain it.
- Think of **the three phases** as three different games: an excellent plan can still fail in execution or be damaged after victory.
- Use **neutral-mechanics analysis** before moral judgment, but never let analytical neutrality become moral indifference.

## Anti-patterns

- **Conflating conspiracy with conspiracy theory**: Speculation without evidence hides the real discipline of planning and makes actual agency easier to overlook.
- **Telegraphing every intention**: Publicly announcing an ambition gives opponents time to organize against it and forfeits the informational advantage secrecy can provide.
- **Treating the status quo as inevitable**: This turns frustration into helplessness instead of a testable question about what action is possible.

## Worked Example

The book opens with Gatsby’s fictional Meyer Wolfsheim and then corrects the history: the 1919 World Series was not fixed by the fictional character but by Arnold Rothstein. Eisenhower, following the scores by telegram, assumed the result was simply the end of an inevitable chain. Learning that one person had coordinated the outcome changed his view of the world. Holiday uses the episode as a compact model for the whole book: an event can look public, distributed, and spontaneous while its decisive causes are private, coordinated, and patient.

## Key Takeaways

1. Define conspiracy as a method of coordinated action, not as a moral verdict.
2. Treat apparent inevitability as a hypothesis about agency, not a fact.
3. Analyze planning, execution, and aftermath separately.
4. Study the mechanics without forgetting that ends and consequences determine whether a method is defensible.
