# Chapter 5: Finding the Back Door

## Core Idea

Holiday argues that successful strategy is as much patience and due diligence as visible boldness. Thiel’s team spends years studying Gawker, rejecting illegal retaliation, hacking, bribery, sabotage, and meritless lawsuits in favor of a legal claim strong enough to survive appeal and do real damage. They deliberately avoid libel and defamation, where the First Amendment and the public-figure “actual malice” standard would give Gawker its strongest terrain. Instead they search the company’s vast archive and complicated corporate structure for the equivalent of Al Capone’s tax violation: a concrete legal weakness outside the publisher’s most protected position. The guiding strategic distinctions are Liddell Hart’s line of least resistance and least expectation and John Boyd’s instruction not to go through the front door. Gawker’s scale helps the search: millions of posts, loose editorial controls, aggressive treatment of privacy, and a history of crossing lines make it likely that the company has left exploitable gaps. The chapter also makes clear that a legal strategy is not automatically clean merely because it is legal; the conspirators want meritorious cases, a defensible record, and a result a jury can understand. The “back door” is therefore an informational edge plus patient execution, not a license to abuse the system.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Line of least resistance and least expectation**: Seek the route an opponent has not fortified because it seems too difficult or too obvious to attempt.
  - When to use: When the opponent’s strongest defense is entrenched and direct attack is predictably costly.
  - How: Map the protected front, inspect adjacent obligations and weaknesses, and test only claims with genuine merit.
- **Study the endgame**: Capablanca’s rule—begin by understanding the position you ultimately need to reach.
  - When to use: Before committing to a long legal, business, or strategic campaign.
  - How: Define what counts as a durable win, including appeal, finances, and public legitimacy.
- **Principled constraint as forcing function**: Limiting the team to legal, strong, non-defamation cases forces better search and selection.

## Key Concepts

- **Back door**: An indirect route around an opponent’s strongest defense.
- **Informational asymmetry**: An edge created by knowing something the opponent does not.
- **Actual malice**: The demanding standard for public figures in defamation cases described in the book.
- **First Amendment terrain**: The area where Gawker’s publisher defenses were strongest.
- **Maginot Line**: A formidable-looking defense that can be bypassed rather than attacked directly.
- **Gating resource**: The scarce input that actually limits progress; here, ideas, people, and execution rather than capital.
- **Meritorious claim**: A legally supportable claim worth pursuing for its own strength, not merely for harassment.
- **Clean victory**: A result that remains defensible when the strategy becomes public.

## Mental Models

- Use **the front-door audit**: write down the argument your opponent wants to have, then look for a different legal or operational domain.
- Think of **volume as search space**: a large, fast-moving system can create more errors, but only disciplined review can find a decisive one.
- Use **the exposure test**: ask whether the proposed tactic would still look defensible if every decision were later examined.

## Anti-patterns

- **Brute-force litigation**: Filing meritless cases may impose cost but weakens legitimacy and can become self-defeating.
- **Illegal retaliation**: Hacking, bribery, surveillance, or sabotage turns the campaign into the thing it claims to oppose.
- **Confusing a technical violation with a decisive case**: A small regulatory issue may not create meaningful leverage.
- **Attacking the strongest defense head-on**: A direct First Amendment fight was precisely where Gawker expected to win.

## Worked Example

Harder’s team reviews Gawker’s ownership structure, affiliate relationships, employment practices, patents, and years of stories. They discover a pattern of privacy-invasive material, including sex tapes, naked images, and stories published despite requests for removal. The search is not simply “find anything embarrassing”; it is “find a claim outside defamation that is lawful, strong, jury-comprehensible, and capable of creating a large judgment.” When the Hogan tape arrives, the team recognizes the rare fit: Gawker has published stolen footage after being told it was recorded without consent. The opportunity is powerful because Gawker’s own choice of material creates the case, while Thiel’s legal framing avoids litigating whether ordinary political or cultural speech may be suppressed.

## Key Takeaways

1. Patience is productive when it is attached to systematic search and clear constraints.
2. Define the endgame before choosing the opening move.
3. Look for the opponent’s unfortified legal or organizational edge, not just an embarrassing fact.
4. Keep the claim strong enough to survive both legal scrutiny and later public exposure.
