# Chapter 9: Know Thy Enemy

## Core Idea

Sun Tzu’s command to know both the enemy and oneself becomes a study of asymmetrical awareness. Gawker cannot study Thiel because it does not know he exists as an opponent, while Thiel’s team spends years learning Gawker’s incentives, people, legal history, and self-image. The first depositions reveal that Gawker’s confidence is not only ideological but experiential: none of the key employees had previously been deposed in a Gawker matter, and past cases had ended before discovery. A.J. Daulerio treats his deposition as a formality and jokes about the age at which a celebrity sex tape would become newsworthy; Nick Denton is more composed but explains the company’s absolutist commitment to transparency and admits he has not watched the tape at issue. Their answers create unforced evidence because they are speaking under oath while assuming the old script will continue. The conspirators learn that the target’s strongest defense—its belief in the First Amendment and its history of winning—has also produced arrogance, weak preparation, and an inability to understand a local jury. Holiday stresses that knowing an opponent means modeling what the opponent believes about itself and about the other side, not merely collecting facts. Gawker’s failure is not simply that it is wrong about the case; it is that it is unaware of the campaign, the plaintiff’s resolve, and the consequences of its own posture.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Know thy enemy and thyself**: Study motives, capabilities, self-conception, blind spots, and likely reactions on both sides.
  - When to use: Before choosing a confrontation or interpreting an opponent’s public behavior.
  - How: Gather direct statements, past decisions, incentives, training, and the assumptions that make the opponent feel safe.
- **Unforced-error strategy**: Give an overconfident opponent time and room to create evidence against its own position.
  - When to use: When the opponent is likely to speak or act carelessly under conditions it considers routine.
  - Limit: Patience must be paired with a case strong enough to use the evidence.
- **Sample-size-of-one edge**: Being the first opponent to reach a previously untested procedural stage can reveal that “impossible” was merely “unattempted.”

## Key Concepts

- **Deposition**: An oath-bound discovery interview whose answers can be preserved and shown to a jury.
- **Discovery**: The process of obtaining evidence before trial.
- **Unforced error**: A damaging statement or decision made without being forced by the opponent.
- **Self-image**: The target’s internal story about its strength and legitimacy.
- **First Amendment defense**: Gawker’s belief that its publication was protected speech.
- **Terra incognita**: Untested territory where the target lacks experience and confidence should be discounted.
- **Opponent modeling**: Predicting behavior from incentives, history, and assumptions.

## Mental Models

- Use **the blind-spot question**: What does the opponent not even consider because it believes the issue is settled?
- Think of **past success as training data with a small sample**; a long streak of early dismissals may not predict the first case that reaches trial.
- Use **the record-is-the-battlefield lens**: in a deposition, tone, timing, and apparent indifference can matter as much as literal words.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming a familiar procedure is harmless**: A deposition that feels routine can become the evidence a jury remembers.
- **Studying only the opponent’s claims**: Ignoring its culture and self-image misses why it will make predictable errors.
- **Interpreting confidence as capability**: Gawker’s “Superman complex” rested on prior opponents quitting, not on proof that it could survive a trial.

## Worked Example

Daulerio answers that he did not consider Hogan’s distress and would have published even knowing the tape was private; later he makes a joke that a jury can read as indifference to child pornography. Denton is less reckless in tone but describes the article as sympathetic without having watched the video and insists that total informational transparency is an extreme principle. Because neither man expects a real trial, they answer from the company’s culture rather than from a prepared witness strategy. The conspirators now know not only what Gawker did, but how Gawker’s confidence sounds when placed in a courtroom record.

## Key Takeaways

1. Study the opponent’s assumptions and habits, not just its stated legal position.
2. Prior victories may teach the target the wrong lesson about future threats.
3. Let confidence create opportunities for the opponent to reveal its own character.
4. Treat the first successful step into untested territory as strategic information.
