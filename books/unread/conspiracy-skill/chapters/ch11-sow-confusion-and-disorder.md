# Chapter 11: Sow Confusion and Disorder

## Core Idea

The Russian concept of *maskirovka* extends secrecy into active deception: a plan needs feints, multiple branches, and distractions so the counterstrategy cannot be read directly. After filing Hogan’s case, Harder and Thiel use related and independent disputes to create pressure around Gawker—Lena Dunham’s copyright claim, an unpaid-intern lawsuit, outreach to former employees, other potential plaintiffs, and the largely autonomous hostility of Gamergate. These fronts do not need to win individually; they force Gawker to spend money, attention, credibility, and executive time while its main trial approaches. The campaign separates Gawker from sympathetic media organizations by associating it with exploited interns, stolen intimate material, advertiser boycotts, and its own harsh rhetoric. The mechanism is cumulative pressure: one attack can be parried, but several simultaneous obligations create a dilemma in which defending one position opens another. Holiday compares it to persistence hunting and to British deception before D-Day—harassment and false possibilities keep the stronger enemy defensive until the decisive stroke. He also insists on a boundary: feints are useful only while the central object remains visible, because strategy can become procrastination and pinpricks cannot replace the main battle. The Hogan case remains the largest investment and the decisive route; the auxiliary fronts are scaffolding that make the target weaker and less able to prepare.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Maskirovka**: Active deception and confusion that make an opponent misread the location, timing, or scale of the real effort.
  - When to use: When a direct response would be easy to anticipate and block.
  - How: Maintain the objective, create alternative explanations, use independent pressure points, and keep the opponent reacting.
- **Multiple-branch plan**: A healthy plan resembles a tree with several branches rather than a single brittle pole.
  - When to use: When the first case or route may fail.
  - Limit: Branches must serve the objective; otherwise they consume resources without improving the position.
- **Long-term strategic pressure**: Repeated non-decisive stress can produce mistakes, fatigue, and internal conflict.
- **Main thrust versus pinpricks**: Once the opponent is weakened, concentrate on the route most likely to finish the campaign.

## Key Concepts

- **Maskirovka**: Deception designed to confuse an opponent.
- **Feint**: A move that attracts defensive attention away from the primary objective.
- **Reconnaissance in force**: A limited action used to learn how an opponent responds.
- **Second front**: An additional conflict that divides the target’s attention.
- **Persistence hunting**: Wearing an opponent down through sustained pursuit.
- **War of attrition**: Making the opponent spend scarce resources until its position fails.
- **Cumulative pressure**: The combined effect of individually manageable attacks.
- **Culminating point**: The moment when continuing beyond victory begins to endanger it.

## Mental Models

- Use **the attention-splitting test**: What must the opponent stop doing to answer this new problem?
- Think of **auxiliary actions as probes**; their value may be intelligence or pressure, not a final win.
- Use **the branch-pruning rule**: once a decisive route is available, stop investing in distractions that no longer improve it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Set-and-forget strategy**: A good initial lawsuit still needs adaptation and surrounding pressure.
- **Unlimited fronts**: Too many campaigns can exhaust the conspirators or create uncontrolled allies.
- **Mistaking autonomous allies for controlled assets**: Gamergate’s attacks helped Thiel while also carrying nihilistic, unpredictable energy.
- **Letting strategy become procrastination**: A pile of clever plans that never reaches trial is failure disguised as sophistication.

## Worked Example

Harder’s work for Lena Dunham produces a small copyright victory and shows that Gawker will fight over a trivial matter even when its position is weak. An unpaid-intern lawsuit then costs Gawker time, money, and public credibility while revealing a hypocrisy between its reporting and its employment practices. Gamergate adds an advertiser-pressure front after Gawker writers respond to online anger with jokes about bullying. None of these actions alone destroys Gawker, and some are not directly controlled by Thiel. Together they create a distracted, financially strained organization that is not preparing effectively for Hogan’s trial. When the time comes, Thiel and Harder narrow back to Hogan rather than allowing the pressure campaign to become the objective.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use deception to make the opponent’s response less direct and less efficient.
2. Auxiliary actions can create information and pressure without carrying the main burden of victory.
3. Preserve flexible branches, but concentrate on the strongest decisive route.
4. Expect uncontrolled second-order effects when mobilizing hostile allies or public outrage.
