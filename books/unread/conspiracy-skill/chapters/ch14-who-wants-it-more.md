# Chapter 14: Who Wants It More?

## Core Idea

Holiday reduces the final contest to a battle of wills: planning, resources, and righteousness matter, but the side willing to keep working and bear the shame often wins. Gawker believes the law and public consensus are on its side, offers increasingly large settlements, and assumes that Hogan or his lawyers must eventually accept a rational exit. Hogan, however, interprets each offer as a reward for quitting and refuses at least $10 million; Thiel’s funding means Gawker’s financial attrition strategy cannot force the expected surrender. The nine-month delay becomes preparation time for the conspirators, who run two mock trials, gather market and media research, consult bankruptcy lawyers, and design a courtroom presentation for an actual Florida jury. They discover that the case’s strongest audience is not the Manhattan media world but local jurors who can empathize with online humiliation and regard Gawker’s style with suspicion. Holiday treats the targeted jury insight as strategically effective but ethically uncomfortable: selecting people for body-image vulnerability means turning private experience into a litigation advantage. The plaintiffs also choose local counsel, invest in large displays and audiovisual support, and build a factual story around the hidden camera, lack of consent, view counts, and Gawker’s own contradictions. Gawker continues to argue law, rely on its propaganda, and prepare incompletely; its chief strategy officer barely knows the case and its lawyers underestimate the jury. The chapter’s distinction is between feeling entitled to win and doing what is necessary to finish: the will to win is shown through preparation, audience understanding, and the willingness to reject a comfortable settlement.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Who-wants-it-more test**: Measure resolve by work, preparation, sacrifice, and willingness to bear uncertainty—not by public confidence.
  - When to use: When two sides appear evenly matched in resources or formal arguments.
  - How: Examine what each side has prepared, what it will give up, and whether it has a credible exit.
- **Mock-trial loop**: Test a case with representative decision-makers, observe reactions, and revise the presentation before the real event.
  - When to use: When a judgment depends on human interpretation rather than only technical doctrine.
  - How: Test strengths, weaknesses, audience fit, presentation, and likely emotional response.
- **Local-audience fit**: Match the messenger, examples, and medium to the actual decision-makers rather than to elite peers.
- **Enemy-making-mistake rule**: Do not interrupt an opponent who is using confidence and propaganda to underprepare.

## Key Concepts

- **Will to win**: The practical resolve to do what is necessary to finish.
- **Settlement leverage**: The ability to make the other side prefer agreement to continued risk.
- **Mock trial**: A rehearsal before prospective jurors used to gather evidence about persuasion.
- **Jury fit**: The relation between the audience’s experience and the facts that need empathy.
- **Local counsel**: Lawyers whose identity and knowledge fit the venue and jury.
- **Facts versus law**: The plaintiffs’ human narrative contrasted with Gawker’s doctrinal lecture.
- **Propaganda line**: A repeated public claim that can become internal belief.
- **Presentation debt**: The disadvantage created when a party underinvests in how evidence will be understood.

## Mental Models

- Use **the entitlement-versus-effort test**: “We should win” is not the same as “we prepared to win.”
- Think of **the jury as an audience with local priors**, not as a neutral abstraction.
- Use **mock evidence to replace self-serving confidence** with observed reactions.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming every client settles**: The opponent’s willingness to endure can invalidate an attrition strategy.
- **Preparing for the doctrine but not the audience**: Correct law can lose when the facts and human stakes are not made intelligible.
- **Treating public confidence as preparation**: Repeating that the case is weak can prevent the work needed if it reaches trial.
- **Using demographic empathy without ethical reflection**: Strategic audience selection can become exploitative or demeaning.

## Worked Example

Thiel spends nearly $100,000 on two mock trials; the reconstructed verdicts are approximately $120 million and $149 million. The team then prepares for Pinellas County: local lawyers, accessible visuals, the hidden camera, Gawker’s internal chats, view counts, and the contrast between its privacy rhetoric and the Hogan publication. Gawker makes settlement offers up to more than $10 million but continues to believe that Hogan will rationally accept. Its lawyers enter the courtroom without a comparable understanding of the jury or a persuasive factual story. The result is not simply a stronger legal argument; it is a side that has studied how its case will land and another that has mistaken formal strength for practical victory.

## Key Takeaways

1. Measure resolve by preparation and sacrifice, not rhetoric.
2. Test the case with the people who will actually decide it.
3. Use local credibility and concrete evidence to translate law into human judgment.
4. Leave room for a dignified exit before the opponent reaches a no-exit state.
