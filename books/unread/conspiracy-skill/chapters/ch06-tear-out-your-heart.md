# Chapter 6: Tear Out Your Heart

## Core Idea

Once a plan moves from thought to action, its participants confront the human cost that abstraction had hidden. Holiday uses Machiavelli, the failed German plots against Hitler, and Thiel’s first payment to show the “and then” moment when a person must decide whether to do the deed rather than merely endorse it in theory. A conspiracy treats people as pieces in a plan, so participants must suppress ordinary empathy to keep moving; the chapter presents this hardening as a dangerous capacity, not a virtue. Thiel’s own history of “disruption” had allowed him to benefit from consequences that other people handled, but the Gawker campaign makes the destruction of a company—and the livelihoods of uninvolved employees—his responsibility. His team also needs plaintiffs to expose humiliating experiences publicly, even though Thiel had refused that exposure for himself. To sustain the campaign, Thiel reframes a personal grievance as a broader prosecution of a repeat offender hurting many people; Holiday calls this “self-serving selflessness,” a moral shield that makes destructive action bearable. The chapter’s limiting lesson comes from Ross Ulbricht: a cause can feel liberating while each step into secrecy, violence, and rationalization makes the actor more brutal. Choosing a cause does not remove the danger of becoming like the enemy, and ethical language cannot substitute for examining who will be harmed.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The “and then” test**: Distinguish intellectual endorsement from the moment an action becomes real and consequences attach.
  - When to use: Before moving from a plan or argument to an irreversible act.
  - How: Identify the actual human action, the people affected, the alternatives, and what you will do when empathy and theory collide.
- **Self-serving selflessness**: Expand a personal grievance into a cause involving others to supply moral energy for persistence.
  - When to use: As a diagnostic of motivation, not as proof that the broader cause is valid.
  - Limit: The broader cause can become a rationalization that prevents reconsideration.
- **Choice-of-cause test**: James’s distinction is that everyone may be capable of savagery; the moral difference lies partly in the cause chosen.

## Key Concepts

- **Moral hardening**: Temporarily suppressing empathy to carry out a chosen action.
- **Human chess pieces**: People reduced to roles or instruments inside a plan.
- **The ends justify the means**: The self-protective story used to excuse harmful methods.
- **Ethical line**: A boundary the conspirators claim they will not cross.
- **Self-serving selflessness**: A personal motive recast as defense of a larger group.
- **Savagery**: The capacity for cruelty activated by a cause.
- **Cautionary tale**: A narrative such as Ulbricht’s showing how escalation changes the actor.

## Mental Models

- Use **the human-cost ledger**: name the people who bear the cost even when they did not cause the original harm.
- Think of **moral certainty as accelerant**: it can provide resolve while reducing the ability to notice new evidence.
- Use **the enemy-resemblance check**: ask which traits of the opponent the campaign is requiring you to adopt.

## Anti-patterns

- **Objectifying the target or bystanders**: Treating people as pieces makes collateral harm easy to ignore.
- **Moral laundering through a larger cause**: “I am helping everyone” can hide revenge, status, or fascination with power.
- **Assuming legality settles morality**: A lawful act may still be disproportionate or corrosive.
- **Believing commitment proves righteousness**: Persistence can show resolve, but it can also show entrapment by sunk costs.

## Worked Example

The case research gives Thiel many possible plaintiffs, but each would have to expose an embarrassing episode and face an uncertain fight. The practical question is therefore not only whether a claim is strong; it is whether Thiel can ask another person to bear the exposure he would not bear himself. He resolves the tension by seeing Gawker as a pattern of harm, not a single insult, and by treating the campaign as prosecution on behalf of other victims. Holiday contrasts this with Ulbricht, whose libertarian project gradually forces decisions about drugs, violence, secrecy, and murder. The comparison makes the mechanism clear: a cause can supply the courage to act and the permission to cross lines.

## Key Takeaways

1. Make the “and then” consequences concrete before action.
2. Keep a separate record of personal motive and public justification.
3. Name collateral victims even when the opponent’s conduct is genuinely harmful.
4. Reassess whether the campaign is making you resemble the thing you oppose.
