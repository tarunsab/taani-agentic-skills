# Chapter 18: There Are Always Unintended Consequences

## Core Idea

E. P. Thompson’s definition of history as a record of unintended consequences supplies the final analytical test: the visible outcome is never the whole result. Thiel achieves his stated objectives—Gawker.com stops publishing, the sex tape disappears, and media norms around nonconsensual intimacy shift—but the victory also makes him famous, exposes him to public scrutiny, and aligns him with political forces he cannot fully control. Holiday contrasts the bad economist, who sees only the visible effect, with the good economist, who considers effects that should have been foreseen. Critics reasonably worry about a chilling effect on journalism and the power of a billionaire to bankrupt a publication, while Thiel sees the legal system as a way to fund legitimate claims that poorer plaintiffs could not otherwise pursue. The later turn toward Donald Trump shows the risk of confusing a successful contrarian bet with a generally reliable method: Thiel’s patient, concealed, legally bounded campaign differs sharply from his rushed, public, coalition-heavy political support. Gamergate and the alt-right carry some of the campaign’s tools—social media, trolling, covert coordination, and money—but not its claimed ethical limits, revealing how a useful method can be inherited by nihilists. The consequences also reshape the participants: Denton and Daulerio become more introspective, Hogan is publicly restored but carries the memory of his own racist disclosures, and Thiel acquires power that may become addictive or corrupting. The book refuses both “everything was justified” and “nothing changed”; it records fewer gratuitous privacy violations alongside the possibility of less aggressive journalism. The durable lesson is to forecast second- and third-order effects, decide what risks are acceptable, and remember that forces released to defeat one enemy may return as the next problem.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Visible-and-foreseeable analysis**: Evaluate what happened, what should have been anticipated, and what new system the action creates.
  - When to use: Before and after a high-leverage intervention.
  - How: List first-, second-, and third-order effects on opponents, allies, institutions, norms, and the actor.
- **Blowback**: A covert intervention produces later effects that damage or constrain the initiator.
  - When to use: When empowering forces whose future behavior cannot be controlled.
  - Limit: The possibility of blowback does not prove the original intervention was wrong; it changes the risk calculation.
- **Weapons-of-one-war principle**: Old methods can become the dominant tools of the next conflict when recombined in a new context.
- **Power addiction check**: After a surprising success, test whether resistance and criticism are being reinterpreted as invitations to act again.

## Key Concepts

- **Unintended consequence**: An effect not included in the actor’s original objective.
- **Second-order effect**: A consequence produced through the reaction to the first result.
- **Chilling effect**: Reduced willingness to speak or investigate because of perceived legal risk.
- **Champerty**: Funding a lawsuit by a party without a direct claim, described historically in the book.
- **Blowback**: Harmful return effects from an intervention.
- **Nihilism**: Destructive energy without a stable positive ideology.
- **Amor fati**: Accepting and finding meaning in what has happened.
- **Effective truth**: A belief that becomes powerful because people act on it.
- **Parabiosis**: The youthful-blood treatment mentioned as part of Thiel’s post-victory notoriety.

## Mental Models

- Use **the mongoose-and-rats test**: Could the chosen solution be active at the wrong time or in the wrong domain, worsening the original problem?
- Think of **power as a changed identity**: after winning once, the actor may no longer accept the constraints that shaped the initial decision.
- Use **the method-transfer test**: Who else can copy the playbook, with what motives and safeguards?
- Keep **normative gain and institutional risk** in the same frame; fewer privacy abuses can coexist with more cautious journalism.

## Anti-patterns

- **Counting only the intended win**: Gawker’s closure does not settle media legitimacy, public narrative, or political consequences.
- **Assuming allies remain controllable**: The forces that help defeat a target may later pursue their own ends.
- **Generalizing from one successful contrarian bet**: Success in a patient legal campaign does not validate rushed open political choices.
- **Using “chilling effect” as a complete answer**: It identifies a risk but does not erase the harm of gratuitous privacy invasion.

## Worked Example

Thiel’s campaign changes the media environment: a former Gawker writer later admits that earlier privacy-invasive material was wrong, and the Hogan tape becomes effectively unavailable online. At the same time, the victory and Thiel’s public support for Trump make him a celebrity and associate him with people whose tactics resemble Gawker’s nihilism. The book compares this to introducing diurnal mongooses to control nocturnal rats: a solution that ignores the system’s timing can make the situation worse. The example captures the central limit of strategic agency—actors can shape events, but they cannot dictate every consequence once new power and incentives have been released.

## Key Takeaways

1. Forecast effects beyond the immediate objective before declaring success.
2. Treat the transfer of a successful method to new actors as a central risk.
3. Reassess identity and appetite for power after an unexpected win.
4. Hold privacy protection, press freedom, accountability, and institutional abuse in tension rather than reducing them to one slogan.
