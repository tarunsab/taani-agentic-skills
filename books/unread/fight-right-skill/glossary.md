# Glossary

**5:1 magic ratio** — During conflict, aim for at least five positive interactions for every negative one; the authors describe 20:1 outside conflict (Ch 2, Ch 6).

**Accepting influence** — Letting a partner’s needs, ideas, and experience change one’s position without collapsing or surrendering (Ch 8).

**Attachment injury** — A violation of a partner’s expectation of support that can block apology and repair until trust is addressed (Ch 9).

**Avoidant conflict style** — A style that tends to minimize or skip conflict and persuasion; it can be stable but risks silent distance (Ch 3).

**Bagel Method** — A map with nonnegotiable core needs in the inner circle and flexible details in the outer ring, used to find compromise (Ch 8).

**Bid for connection** — A small verbal or nonverbal attempt to receive attention, interest, comfort, or contact from a partner (Ch 4).

**Characterological violence** — A pattern of domination, control, jealousy, isolation, or unpredictable attack; the authors distinguish it from situational violence and advise leaving (Ch 4).

**Conflict culture** — The often-unspoken rules about how emotion and disagreement should be expressed and handled, learned through family, culture, and experience (Ch 1, Ch 3).

**Conflict task** — The Love Lab exercise in which partners choose an ongoing disagreement and discuss it while their interaction is observed and coded (Ch 2).

**Constructive planning** — The fifth stage of processing a regrettable incident: each partner names one change they can make next time (Ch 9).

**Cooperative gain** — A conflict outcome in which both partners’ gains are considered together rather than one person’s gain cancelling the other’s (Ch 8).

**Dream Catcher’s questions** — Six questions about beliefs, history, importance, feelings, ideal dream, and underlying purpose that uncover meaning beneath a position (Ch 7).

**Dreams within conflict** — A speaker/listener exercise for exploring the personal dream, value, history, or purpose beneath a repetitive conflict (Ch 7).

**Emotion bank account** — The reservoir of goodwill built by repeated turning toward, affection, appreciation, and connection; it buffers conflict (Ch 4).

**Emotional flooding** — A fight-or-flight/freeze state in which physiological arousal narrows attention and removes access to empathy and conflict skills (Ch 6).

**Four Horsemen** — Criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling; the authors associate their repeated presence with destructive conflict (Ch 1, Ch 5, Ch 6).

**Gridlock** — Repetitive, painful conflict around a perpetual problem with entrenched positions, no progress, rejection, and lost warmth (Ch 4, Ch 7).

**Harsh start-up** — Beginning with criticism, partner-description, blame, or kitchen sinking, making defensiveness the easiest reply (Ch 5).

**Kitchen sinking** — Adding a backlog of unrelated complaints to the immediate issue instead of discussing one incident or problem (Ch 5, Ch 9).

**Meta-emotion** — A person’s feelings and beliefs about feelings, especially beliefs about whether emotion should be expressed, avoided, or debated (Ch 3).

**Meta-emotion mismatch** — A couple’s divergent emotional rules or conflict styles, which can make each partner misread the other’s behavior (Ch 3).

**Nash equilibrium** — A stable outcome in which neither player can do better given the other’s strategy; in love, it is translated into thinking for two (Ch 8).

**Negative perspective** — A lens produced by repeated unresolved conflict in which neutral or ambiguous partner behavior is interpreted negatively (Ch 7).

**Perpetual problem** — A recurring conflict rooted in enduring personality, lifestyle, value, or belief differences; it is managed rather than finally solved (Ch 1, Ch 4).

**Positive need** — A specific request for what a partner can do to help, stated as a constructive action rather than a list of failures (Ch 5).

**Repair attempt** — Any apology, validation, empathy, affectionate gesture, humor, or other move that interrupts negativity and reconnects partners (Ch 6).

**Regrettable incident** — A conflict that leaves emotional injury and requires later processing rather than a quick “sorry” or silent resumption of life (Ch 6, Ch 9).

**Self-soothing** — Deliberately lowering one’s physiological arousal during a break through an absorbing, calming activity rather than rumination (Ch 6).

**Situational violence** — Mild violence arising when a particular conflict escalates out of control; the authors contrast it with characterological domination and recommend professional support (Ch 4).

**Softened start-up** — “I feel [emotion] about [situation/problem] and I need [positive need],” with no criticism, blame, or kitchen sinking (Ch 5).

**SPAFF** — John Gottman’s Specific Affect Coding System for coding facial expression, language, tone, physical cues, culture, and interactional emotion (Ch 2).

**Stop Action** — The repair-checklist category for interrupting an escalating exchange and requesting a break or restart (Ch 6).

**Turning against** — Responding negatively or harshly to a partner’s bid for connection (Ch 4).

**Turning away** — Ignoring, postponing, or failing to respond to a partner’s bid for connection (Ch 4).

**Turning toward** — Responding positively to a partner’s bid; repeated turning toward builds trust and goodwill (Ch 4).

**Validation** — Showing that a partner’s experience makes sense or is understandable; it is not agreement with the partner’s position (Ch 3, Ch 5).

**Volatile conflict style** — A style that engages quickly with high emotion and debate; it can thrive when humor, positivity, and repair remain available (Ch 3).

**Yield to win** — The aikido-inspired principle that accepting influence and moving with a partner creates more relational power than resisting them (Ch 8).

**Zero-sum dynamic** — Treating a relationship decision as one partner’s win and the other’s equal loss, a frame the authors say harms both (Ch 8).
