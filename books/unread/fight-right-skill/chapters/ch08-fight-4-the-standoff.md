# Chapter 8: Part 2 — Fight #4: The Standoff

## Core Idea

The Standoff is the mistake of treating a relationship decision as a zero-sum contest in which one partner’s gain must be the other’s loss. The authors argue that many nuanced choices are not poker: influence, timing, sequence, location, duration, and creative design can create cooperative gain. The first requirement is accepting influence, which means allowing a partner’s experience and needs to change one’s view without surrendering the core of one’s own dream. The Bagel Method makes this visible by placing nonnegotiable needs in an inner circle and flexible routes in an outer ring. A good compromise honors both cores and is evaluated as “what is best for us,” not by who was proven right. The authors connect this to trust, commitment, and a relationship version of Nash equilibrium: both partners can be satisfied within the actual constraints, even if neither receives the original all-or-nothing plan. If the core dreams remain irreconcilable after genuine exploration, separation may be more honest than forcing a loss of self.

## Mistake: Competing to Win

In a zero-sum frame, the dishes, children’s summer camp, a move toward family, or a retirement plan becomes a scoreboard. The partner who yields feels defeated, so both double down on the belief that their own position is the only rational one. Vince and Jenny embody this pattern: he wants to sell their Washington house and sail around the world; she wants to return to her family’s farm in Iowa. Their dreams seem geographically impossible to combine, and their long marriage has accumulated unspoken sacrifice and resentment, so any concession feels like erasing a life.

## The Big Problem with a Win-Lose Dynamic

The authors report a twenty-year study in which some couples’ interaction ratings moved in opposite directions: when one partner experienced a gain, the other’s experience dropped. They called these zero-sum couples and say that 58% of the husbands in that group died during the study, compared with 20% in more collaborative couples; they associate the difference with chronic physiological stress and note a possible oxytocin-related protection for women. Whatever the limitations of applying this observational finding to every couple, the chapter’s behavioral conclusion is direct: a win-lose pattern harms the relationship even when one partner gets the immediate decision. A cooperative interaction keeps both partners’ ratings and physiology more aligned, with more positivity and less arousal. The authors also report chronic difficulty finding compromise in 84% of heterosexual, 81% of gay, and 78% of lesbian couples entering therapy, showing how common the trap is in their sample.

## “Yielding to Win”

The authors borrow from aikido, the “art of peace,” where a person uses a partner’s movement rather than blocking it with force. Emotional aikido means moving with a partner’s information, staying centered, and changing one’s perspective as one learns why the request matters. It is not collapsing into whatever the partner demands; it is being open enough that influence can flow both ways. Love Lab findings are presented as showing that heterosexual men who accepted their wives’ influence had happier marriages and lower divorce risk, while men who resisted power sharing had an 81% probability of splitting. The practical question is not “How do I keep control?” but “What do I lose by giving influence here, and what might I gain?”

## Gender, Sexuality, and Accepting Influence

The authors place influence inside cultural and historical power rather than treating it as a purely individual preference. They argue that men may have been socialized to experience yielding as weakness and that women may have learned vigilance, fear, or reduced authority in ways that affect conflict and intimacy. They report that same-sex couples in their 12 Year Study tended to use more humor, positivity, and openness to influence, while also noting possibilities rather than claiming one cause: shared marginalization, empathy from rejection, and a desire not to reproduce domination. These observations are contextual explanations, not rules about every gender or orientation. The authors’ concrete reframe for men is that protection means protecting a partner’s humanity and pathway toward her dreams, including by taking on childcare or other work coded as feminine. Their examples include John learning to braid his daughter’s hair while Julie pursued Everest and a son-in-law taking night duty so his breastfeeding partner could sleep.

## Don’t Be the Rock!

A partner who says no to every proposal becomes an obstacle that the other partner learns to go around. Like a rock in a trail, the immovable partner may think they have preserved power, but they have lost influence because no collaborative information can move through them. Real relational power comes from a give-and-take in which each partner’s emotions, needs, and dreams are treated with respect. A useful self-assessment is whether each partner seeks the other’s opinion, asks curious questions, believes the other has common sense, learns during disagreement, tries the other’s way, and wants the other to feel influential. Resistance can be practiced safely in the low-stakes Island Survival Game, where each partner ranks ten items and then creates a consensus list while watching for domination, sulking, withdrawal, and whether both feel included.

## Collaborative Gain: Toward True Compromise

The authors use a tandem kayak stuck on Arctic ice as a physical model of a fight: either paddler’s plan might have worked, but criticism and refusal to credit each other kept the boat stuck until an outside group intervened. The Bagel Method is introduced after the dreams conversation has made the meanings visible. Draw an inner circle for the part of the dream necessary for identity and wholeness, then an outer ring for flexible logistics. Ask journalist-like questions—when, where, how much, how often, how long, what comes first and last—to expose options that the all-or-nothing frame hid. Vince and Jenny discover that the core is, for him, freedom and world travel, and for her, living the family legacy on the farm; the flexible fields include whether to sell or rent the house, whose dream goes first, part-time versus full-time, timing, money, and duration. They imagine a year on the farm, sailing courses, and later shorter or longer trips, so both can be excited rather than defeated.

## Examples of Flexible Compromise

For Andrés and Moira, the core is respectively giving their son access to the innovative school Andrés helped build and staying connected to public education and local community. They consider the child’s preference and preserve neighborhood ties through activities even though the school choice is not a 50/50 split. For the Geneva–Nigeria couple, the cores are supporting a child’s special-needs education and honoring a cultural duty to parents; they design a nontraditional arrangement in which mother and son spend part of the year in Lagos with educational support while the father travels to Geneva. These plans are not universal templates; they work because the partners identify their actual cores, have enough autonomy and trust, and accept unusual logistics. The method is for generating a path, not for pretending every circumstance has equal flexibility.

## When This Fight Really Does Mean the End

The Bagel Method cannot make room where the inner circles directly negate each other. A person who needs parenthood and a person who needs a child-free life cannot split the difference without violating a core life dream. The authors distinguish this from rigidity: some sacrifice is part of love, but a compromise should not require a person to abandon the self they need to live. If the couple has explored the dreams, listened, validated, and separated core from flexible details and the sacrifices remain intolerable, ending the relationship can honor both people rather than assigning blame. “Success” is not always forever; it can include a compassionate recognition that two lives cannot be built in the same shape.

## From Zero Sum to the Nash Equilibrium

The authors translate John Nash’s model into relationship language: a stable outcome is one in which neither partner can do better given the other’s constraints and strategy. Couples reach this not by treating one another as opponents but by thinking for two and trusting that each person has the other’s best interests at heart. Trust makes flexibility possible because a partner can risk yielding without expecting exploitation. Commitment is visible when partners bring important concerns to each other rather than outsiders and when they notice what they have rather than constantly maximizing what is missing. The techniques in the book form a positive feedback loop: turning toward, softened starts, listening, empathy, dreams, and repair build trust, and trust makes those techniques easier.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Zero-sum versus cooperative gain**: Replace a winner/loser scoreboard with shared outcomes.
- **Yield to win**: Accept influence to preserve reciprocal influence.
- **Protector of humanity**: Support a partner’s ability to pursue a core dream, including through role flexibility.
- **Gottman Island Survival Game**: Practice power sharing in a low-stakes decision.
- **Bagel Method**: Separate core nonnegotiables from flexible routes to fulfillment.
- **Nash equilibrium for couples**: Think for two until neither partner can do better within the constraints.

## Key Concepts

- **Zero-sum outcome**: One partner’s gain is treated as the other’s loss.
- **Cooperative gain**: Both partners’ needs are advanced through joint design.
- **Accepting influence**: Openness to changing one’s position in response to the partner.
- **Inner circle**: Nonnegotiable core of a dream or need.
- **Outer ring**: Flexible details surrounding the core.
- **Nash equilibrium**: A stable mutually workable outcome under the actual constraints.
- **Thinking for two**: Choosing for the relationship rather than only for oneself.

## Mental Models

- **Use the rock test**: a refusal that makes the partner go around you is not power.
- **Use the bagel test**: if every detail feels nonnegotiable, the dream has not been reduced to its core.
- **Use the shared-score test**: ask whether a “win” leaves both partners safer, respected, and connected.

## Anti-patterns

- **Arguing until the partner admits you are right**: it confuses correctness with a workable relationship.
- **Calling influence weakness**: resistance often destroys the influence one is trying to preserve.
- **Offering a fake compromise**: a midpoint that abandons a core dream is not cooperative gain.
- **Assuming one standard arrangement fits every couple**: the book’s examples depend on the partners’ actual needs, culture, and resources.

## Worked Example

Vince and Jenny first hear the dream beneath their retirement standoff. He wants the freedom and adventure of sailing around the world with her; she wants to fulfill a family legacy by living on the Iowa farm with him. Their Bagel diagrams reveal that the location and sequence are flexible even though those cores are not. They consider living at the farm for a year, training for sailing, taking shorter trips first, and deciding later whether to sell or rent their Washington house. The authors present the plan as emerging from mutual influence: each partner keeps the essential dream while giving on timing, duration, and logistics.

## Key Takeaways

1. Diagnose a win/lose frame before arguing harder.
2. Let influence flow both ways; yielding can increase rather than reduce power.
3. Separate identity-level needs from the details chosen to express them.
4. Use a temporary compromise when the details need testing.
5. Accept that some core dreams are incompatible and that safety outranks compromise.
