# Chapter 11: Fight Right Quick Guide

## Core Idea

The Quick Guide is the book’s portable map from the moment a conflict starts through repair. It keeps the goal visible: understand the partner more deeply, not win, erase disagreement, or force an immediate solution. The sequence is deliberately practical—start gently, listen, regulate flooding, uncover dreams, separate core from flexible details, and process old injuries. It is a lookup section rather than a new theory, so its lists and scripts should remain compact. The authors explicitly encourage using the guide in the middle of a fight or reading a repair phrase directly from the page.

## Going into a Conflict Conversation

- Conflict can be upsetting, but it can also teach partners how to love and understand each other.
- Anger, sadness, and fear are not automatically bad; contempt, criticism, defensiveness, stonewalling, and unregulated escalation are the danger.
- The goal is not to win. It is to understand, stay collaborative, and create a better next interaction.

## 1. Start Right

### If You Need to Raise an Issue

- **Mistake**: A harsh start with criticism, blame, partner-description, or kitchen sinking.
- **Move**: “I feel [emotion] about [situation/problem], and I need [positive need].”
- **Why**: The authors’ body text says that 96% of the time the first three minutes predict the trajectory; the Quick Guide rounds this to 97%.
- **Rules**: Describe yourself and the situation; request a positive action; keep to one issue.

### If Your Partner Raises an Issue

- **Mistake**: Defending, explaining, or rebutting immediately.
- **Move**: Listen; ask clarifying questions; summarize; validate.
- **Validation**: Understanding the partner’s experience is not agreement with their position.

## 2. Stay Collaborative

### If You Are Getting Flooded

- **Tells**: Racing heart, short breath, heat, tight body, spinning thoughts, attacking, or shutting down.
- **Move**: Say you are overwhelmed and ask for a break; do not silently walk out or diagnose the partner.
- **Break**: Leave visual range, do something soothing that occupies attention, take at least 20 minutes, and return within 24 hours or clearly renegotiate.
- **Do not**: rehearse rebuttals, ruminate, seek the last word, or leave the partner hanging.

### If You Are Getting Polarized

- **Mistake**: Trying to win, persuade, or solve the entire problem.
- **Move**: Solve the moment by self-description, clear needs, self-soothing, and repair.
- **Positives**: Appreciation, affection, empathy, validation, open questions, a nod, a smile, humor without sarcasm, and apology.
- **Ratio**: Keep about five positives for every negative in conflict; the authors describe 20:1 outside conflict.
- **Assumption of similarity**: If a quality is visible in the partner or oneself, look for the corresponding quality on both sides before polarizing.

## 3. Get Down to the Dreams

### When You Are Stuck

- **Tells**: Repeating topic, failed compromise, trivial trigger with a large reaction, gridlock, or avoidance.
- **Move**: Use speaker/listener roles to explore the dream, belief, value, history, feelings, and purpose beneath the position.
- **Listener’s six questions**:
  1. What do you believe or value about this issue?
  2. Does it connect to your history or childhood?
  3. Why is it important to you?
  4. What feelings do you have about it?
  5. What is your ideal dream here?
  6. What underlying purpose or goal is present?
- **Rule**: Do not persuade or solve yet. If an answer is not available, say that more reflection is needed.

## 4. Find Areas of Flexibility

### When You Need to Compromise

- **Mistake**: Competing to win or treating the decision as zero-sum.
- **Move**: Draw a Bagel: inner circle = nonnegotiable core; outer ring = flexible details.
- **Ask**: Why is the core essential? What would flexibility look like? What do we agree about? What feelings and goals are shared?
- **Design variables**: When, where, how much, how often, sequence, duration, and whether a plan is temporary.
- **Principle**: Yield to win; partners with influence are able to be influenced.
- **Limit**: A compromise cannot split an irreconcilable core such as parenthood versus a child-free life.

## 5. Process Past Fights

### When a Fight Has Not Been Addressed

- **Mistake**: Sweeping the incident under the rug or fighting about the fight.
- **Prep**: Wait until both are calm enough to sit in the balcony; release the demand for one factual transcript; use “I” language.
- **Five steps**:
  1. **Feelings** — Say what you felt, not why and not what your partner felt.
  2. **Realities** — Describe what you saw, heard, and imagined; summarize and validate both realities.
  3. **Triggers** — Connect the escalation to an older bruise and share the story behind it.
  4. **Responsibility** — Name your contribution, regrets, and specific apology.
  5. **Constructive Plans** — Name one change your partner can make and one you can make.
- **Rules**: One incident at a time; no blame, intent assignment, patterns, or kitchen sinking. It is never too late, but major betrayal, attachment injury, addiction, or violence may need professional support.

## Worked Example

In the book’s own example, Julie and John use the five-step guide after a fight about their daughter’s health and independence. They name different feelings, describe two valid realities, trace their reactions to childhood illness and bullying, apologize for specific words, and agree on different next actions. They do not decide who was objectively right or solve parenting forever. The structured sequence changes the interaction from a fresh fight into mutual understanding and accepted repair.

## Key Takeaways

1. Start with self + situation + positive need.
2. Listen and validate before persuasion.
3. Stop action when the body floods.
4. Explore dreams before compromise.
5. Put core needs inside the Bagel and flex the route.
6. Process old incidents from the balcony, one at a time.
