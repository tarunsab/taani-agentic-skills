# Chapter 6: Part 2 — Fight #2: The Flood

## Core Idea

Flooding is the physiological point at which a partner’s nervous system has taken over the conflict: heart rate and stress hormones rise, attention narrows, and the capacity to process words, empathy, or newly learned skills collapses. Attack, defensiveness, and withdrawal are different outward responses to the same underlying state, so continuing the argument is not evidence of commitment or courage—it is a route to damage. The authors report flooding as a major challenge for 97% of couples in their international study and connect it to the Four Horsemen and missed repairs. The intervention is to notice each person’s bodily tells, stop action, communicate a timed break, leave visual range, self-soothe without rehearsing the case, and return. Once calm, the goal is not to solve the whole perpetual issue but to “solve the moment”: restore a positive, collaborative exchange, express needs clearly, and make or accept repairs. The chapter’s examples show that a difficult betrayal and a minor morning logistics conflict can both become catastrophic when neither partner recognizes the nervous system’s flood.

## Enemies and Strangers

Stan and Susan’s crisis begins with an affair, a genuine betrayal that neither can resolve in one conversation. Susan feels that the life she knew is collapsing, while Stan is ashamed but defends himself by arguing that loneliness and lack of sex explain his behavior. Both become flooded, recruit criticism and contempt, and throw the relationship into a cycle in which even discussing childcare ends in yelling or objects being thrown. Nora and Robbie’s conflict begins much smaller: she is sick and needs him to drive the children, while he is exhausted and feels he has been carrying extra work during her demanding deal. Each assumes the other sees their need and is choosing not to help, so a request becomes a ledger of old sacrifices, then defensiveness, then a slammed door. The difference in topic does not change the mechanism: once the nervous systems are in overdrive, the partners experience one another as enemy or stranger and cannot listen.

## What Went Wrong?

Starting softly helps, but it cannot make a flooded brain process information. In the two examples, there are moments that could have become repairs—an apology, a concession, or a request for understanding—but the other partner is too activated to receive them. Flooding therefore creates a decision point that overrides the original topic: stop the interaction before the next regrettable incident. The authors emphasize that a person may outwardly lash out and attack or inwardly shut down and stonewall; neither is a reliable sign that the person has calmly chosen their position. The first diagnostic question is bodily and immediate: “Am I flooded, and is my partner flooded?”

## How to Stop the Flood

Learn personal early-warning signs such as shortness of breath, gritted teeth, a tight jaw, heat, reddening, muscle tension, a racing heart, queasiness, or a spinning mind. Tell the partner that you are overwhelmed and need a break, say where you are going, and give a return time; do not silently walk out or tell the partner that you have diagnosed their flood. Leave visual range and do something absorbing and soothing—walk with music or a podcast, read, cook, garden, exercise, meditate, breathe, or do a manageable task—rather than replaying the injustice or planning the next rebuttal. The authors give a minimum break of about 20 minutes, the time they associate with stress hormones beginning to metabolize, and a maximum of 24 hours before reconvening or clearly renegotiating. If the agreed time arrives and arousal remains high, return to communicate that more time is needed and name the new return point. The break is protective, not selfish, because staying in a flood makes contempt, verbal injury, or thrown objects more likely.

## Solving the Moment

After calming, reframe the objective. The couple is not trying to win, force persuasion, produce a final solution, or even necessarily reach compromise during this interaction; the immediate objective is a positive conversation about the issue. The authors call this “solving the moment,” and tie it to the 5:1 ratio rather than to a tally sheet. Three behaviors naturally add the needed positivity: self-soothe, speak about one’s own needs rather than the partner’s defects, and make, recognize, or accept repairs. A serious issue such as an affair may require a long process of rebuilding trust, but one calm, empathic exchange is still a meaningful win because it restores teamwork. A positive moment does not deny the injury or settle the future; it creates the conditions for the next useful conversation.

## Expressing Needs: Just Do It!

Partners often assume their needs are obvious and interpret nonfulfillment as deliberate withholding. That assumption turns vulnerability into resentment, as with the musician who believed his wife should know his needs because they had known each other since childhood. When he used a needs card deck, he found precise requests—warm greetings, affectionate touch, and romantic time away from the children—that his wife could understand and even wanted too. The authors’ “Truth Exercise,” adapted from Eugene Gendlin’s focusing, helps people who cannot name feelings: repeat a lie about something loved, then the truth, and notice the body’s different proprioceptive signals. Tightness, constriction, or a pit can signal “not my truth,” while ease can point toward a more accurate emotional word. Greater precision about feeling—lonely rather than merely bad, for example—often reveals the need that can be communicated.

## Small Repairs Prevent Major Damage

A repair attempt is any move that counteracts negativity: apology, validation, empathy, appreciation, a nod, affectionate touch when welcome, or a genuinely goofy reset. Its effectiveness depends less on elegant wording than on whether the receiving partner can recognize and accept it, which is why friendship and daily turning toward matter. In a depleted relationship, one partner may open the connecting door while the other sees only a closed door because flooding blocks reception. The authors therefore treat repair as a two-person event: make the attempt, watch for it, and give it a chance to land. A couple of lawyers use “Let the record show” as shared repair shorthand; the phrase works because both know the pattern and can laugh without humiliating one another. Small repairs are not a substitute for accountability, but they prevent a temporary mistake from becoming a larger injury.

## Gottman Repair Checklist

Use the checklist when the conversation is slipping. Its six categories are:

- **I Feel**: name fear, hurt, defensiveness, criticism, or not feeling understood.
- **I Need to Calm Down**: request safety, quiet, support, listening, gentleness, or a break.
- **Sorry**: own an overreaction, ask to start again, or ask what would make it better.
- **Stop Action!**: pause, say the exchange is off track, and agree to resume.
- **Get to Yes**: identify common ground, acknowledge a point, or invite compromise.
- **I Appreciate**: name a good point, the partner’s effort, love, or shared problem.

The checklist is allowed to be read directly; the authors report that a workshop couple pinned it to the refrigerator and used the location as a cue to cool down. A signal or hand gesture can also communicate “I need to pause but will return” when words are inaccessible, but both partners must respect it.

## A Do-Over, with Repair

With Stan and Susan, intervention begins by making conversations possible without automatic flooding, then by asking Susan to describe hurt and fear without character attack and Stan to validate instead of defending. Their rewritten exchange does not resolve the affair; Stan asks about Susan’s nightmare, validates the fear of losing the relationship, and offers reassurance, while Susan states that talking has not yet made her feel better without turning that pain into contempt. A hand offered and accepted closes the moment with connection rather than a verdict. With Nora and Robbie, learning to notice the flood makes the rest easier because they can discuss the need for support without unloading years of perceived unfairness. The chapter’s measure of progress is not a solved topic but a changed interaction.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Flooding signs and Stop Action**: Notice physiology and interrupt before skill access disappears.
- **Timed self-soothing break**: Communicate, leave visual range, soothe, return in 20 minutes to 24 hours.
- **Solve the moment**: Optimize this interaction for positivity and collaboration, not final resolution.
- **Truth Exercise**: Use proprioceptive feedback to identify a more accurate feeling.
- **Gottman Repair Checklist**: Choose a phrase from six repair categories when language is hard.

## Key Concepts

- **Flooding**: Physiological fight/flight/freeze state that blocks productive processing.
- **Self-soothing**: Calming the body without ruminating on the dispute.
- **Stop Action**: A clear request to halt and resume later.
- **Repair**: A move that lowers negativity and reconnects partners.
- **Proprioception**: Awareness of bodily changes that can help identify emotion.
- **Regrettable incident**: A fight that leaves injury needing later processing.
- **Green zone**: A conflict exchange with more positivity than negativity.

## Mental Models

- **Treat flooding as an operating-state change**: once the nervous system is offline for dialogue, pause the task.
- **Use “solve the moment” as a smaller target**: one good interaction can be progress on a large trauma.
- **Think of repair as a door**: the attempt opens it; the partner’s reception determines whether you meet.

## Anti-patterns

- **Staying to get the last word**: it increases arousal and makes the next injury more likely.
- **Leaving without a return plan**: a break becomes abandonment or punishment.
- **Ruminating during the break**: the body stays activated even when the partner is absent.
- **Assuming an apology ends the issue**: the impact and needs may still require attention.
- **Using touch or humor without reading the partner**: a repair must be received, not merely performed.

## Worked Example

The authors show a post-affair exchange after Stan and Susan learn to stop and validate. Stan asks how Susan feels; she says she is still suffering and describes recurring nightmares of him disappearing. Instead of arguing that the dream is not his fault, Stan asks what she dreamed, acknowledges how terrible it sounds, and states that he is present and wants their relationship. Susan says it is hard to believe him, and he accepts that trust will take time; the interaction ends with her taking his hand. Nothing about the betrayal is solved, but the couple has replaced attack/defend with a positive, truthful moment.

## Key Takeaways

1. Learn the body’s first flood signals before relying on conflict technique.
2. Use a communicated break, not silent withdrawal or tactical delay.
3. Return to the smaller goal of a positive interaction.
4. Ask for needs explicitly; partners are not mind readers.
5. Make and receive small repairs before the injury compounds.
