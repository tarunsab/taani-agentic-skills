# Chapter 9: Part 2 — Fight #5: The Chasm in the Room

## Core Idea

The final fight mistake is stewing about a bad fight or pretending it did not happen. A regrettable incident leaves an emotional wound and an unfinished memory; if partners apologize too quickly, sweep it under the rug, or relitigate it as a fact trial, the wound can keep shaping later conflicts. The authors’ repair blueprint processes one incident in five ordered steps: Feelings, Realities, Triggers, Responsibility, and Constructive Plans. Both partners’ subjective realities are treated as valid, even when their memories and interpretations differ, and first-person description replaces blame or assumed intent. The process must happen after flooding has subsided, from the “balcony” rather than the stage, and it repairs the communication pattern rather than promising to solve the original perpetual problem. The authors report that the process can produce breakthroughs even years later, but set limits around ongoing betrayal, attachment injury, addiction refusal, and unsafe or characterological violence. Properly used, processing turns an old fight from a chasm into shared knowledge about vulnerability, impact, accountability, and the next attempt.

## Mistake: Stewing About the Fight

Molly and Selena’s pandemic fight begins with work and remote schooling. Molly, who has carried more of the children’s school support, asks for a morning to catch up; Selena cannot cancel an important meeting and minimizes the urgency of graphic work. Molly then says Selena might care more about school if she had given birth, a remark that strikes Selena’s grief about being the nonbiological parent. They each apologize and create a schedule, but the emotional incident remains unresolved, so they keep arguing about who began it, what was said, and who was the more injured partner. The surface schedule is solved while the chasm grows through repeated relitigation, avoidance, and reduced connection. The authors use the case to show why a quick “sorry” is not the same as repair.

## Some Fights Are Forever If You Do Not Process Them

The authors use Bluma Zeigarnik’s interrupted-task studies to explain why unfinished events remain vivid: participants recalled interrupted tasks more accurately than completed ones. A fight that was never emotionally completed can therefore stay present as if it happened yesterday, especially when its memory carries physiological fear or betrayal. They add a neuroscience caveat: memory is malleable, and every retrieval can edit the event toward the person’s current feelings and self-protective interpretation. The result is not one objective transcript but two changing realities of a shared moment. Processing does not decide whose facts win; it reduces the need to keep carrying the unresolved plate in memory.

## Signs You Need to Address a Past Incident

Look for fighting about the fight, inability to discuss the incident without re-escalating, total avoidance of the topic, less general connection or sex, increased harshness about unrelated issues, or an outsized response to an ordinary request. These signs suggest that the past incident is still active in the nervous system and is leaking into present interpretation. A couple may remember different tones, motives, and sequences, and neither can be corrected by appealing to an imaginary “God camera.” The intervention is not to deny that one person may have acted harmfully; it is to give both people a safe, structured way to describe impact, context, and responsibility without forcing agreement about every detail. If the conversation cannot stay out of the fight, pause and seek additional support.

## The Key to Successful Processing: Both Realities Are Valid

Each partner filters an interaction through assumptions, emotions, perceptions, and extrapolations. The authors recommend “I” descriptions such as “I saw,” “I heard,” “I imagined,” “I assumed,” and “It seemed to me,” rather than “you were angry” or “you meant to hurt me.” The listener summarizes and validates the partner’s reality, not because it is the only truth but because it is useful information about how the event was experienced. Validation means understanding part of the experience and can coexist with a different memory. The couple’s shared task is to learn what the partner saw, heard, felt, and needed, then connect the present trigger to older vulnerabilities without making the current partner responsible for the original wound.

## The Five Steps

### Step 1: Feelings

Take turns naming what was felt during the incident without explaining why or describing the partner’s feelings. The authors’ list includes defensive, flooded, angry, sad, unloved, misunderstood, criticized, unsafe, lonely, abandoned, powerless, taken for granted, exhausted, remorseful, or wanting either to leave or stay. A long list is acceptable because the purpose is accurate emotional inventory, not a single neat label. Keep the language in the form “I felt…” so this opening does not become a new accusation.

### Step 2: Realities

Each partner describes their own moment-by-moment perception like a reporter, then the listener summarizes and validates at least part of it. The listener asks whether the summary is right and what else is needed to understand; if the partner does not feel understood, the couple stays with this step. Do not assign intent, say what the partner felt, or argue over the factual version. The authors’ own processing of a fight about their daughter shows how hearing the other’s story can transform “you criticized me” into an understanding of why the comment landed as a parenthood and trust injury.

### Step 3: Triggers

Identify what in the incident produced the large reaction, then trace the similar feeling to a time before the current relationship. A trigger is an old bruise pressed on by the present, not simply another event from this relationship that needs to be added to the same conversation. Julie’s vigilance about illness was connected to contracting polio after a missed childhood illness; John’s alarm about his daughter’s college experience connected to bullying, isolation, and finding community later. These histories make a current remark intelligible without making it inevitable or excusing harmful behavior. The listener validates the logic of the trigger’s emotional origin.

### Step 4: Take Responsibility

After each partner has been heard and the triggers are visible, name the state or contribution that set up the miscommunication: stress, irritability, preoccupation, low confidence, turning away, lack of appreciation, martyrdom, not asking for a need, or running on empty. Then state the specific regret and apology, such as overreacting, attacking, failing to listen, being disrespectful, or assuming the worst. Apology comes late on purpose; an immediate “sorry” may only seek to end the discomfort before impact is understood. If an apology does not land, ask what the partner still needs to hear rather than defending the adequacy of the apology.

### Step 5: Constructive Plans

Each partner names one thing the other can do differently next time and one thing they themselves can do. The plan should change the way the issue is communicated, not pretend the underlying perpetual disagreement has disappeared. In the Gottmans’ example, John agrees to ask questions when Julie becomes emotional, while Julie agrees to avoid the awful statement that escaped in the heat of the moment. The pair ends with a concrete, mutual action and a better chance of recognizing the next trigger before it becomes an incident.

## Beta-Testing the Five Steps on Themselves

Julie and John use the process after a fight about their daughter’s health and independence. Julie experiences John’s warning not to control their daughter as criticism of her careful self-restraint; John experiences Julie’s remark about needing a mature co-parent as an attack on the role that matters most to him. Their realities are both coherent once explained. Their triggers reveal old wounds around missed illness and social exclusion, and understanding these makes responsibility and apology easier. The process does not erase either history, but it lets them stop using the history as an invisible weapon in the present.

## Closing the Chasm

The authors’ workshop experience suggests that people can follow the five steps without returning to the fight, even when the incident is intense or decades old. Molly and Selena eventually process their pandemic argument after nearly a year: Molly explains the fear that parenthood is crowding out her career, while Selena explains the pain and grief attached to not giving birth and the fear of being asked to prove love by surrendering too much. Their apologies become specific and meaningful, and shared humor returns without denying the injury. The authors call this putting the stones out of the shoes; the memory is not deleted, but it no longer has to dictate every step.

## Doing Repair Right: Limits and Pitfalls

Do not start while either partner is flooded; wait until both can observe the event from the balcony. Follow the order, process one incident, and do not use the conversation to prove patterns or add every past injury. A trigger is from before the relationship; a similar event inside the relationship should be processed separately. If there is an attachment injury, a history of betrayal or secrecy, or an apology that cannot restore trust, professional support may be needed. The process can handle a single regrettable incident but is not treatment for long-term affairs, financial betrayal, addiction, or coercive violence. The goal is to repair how the couple communicates about the issue and prevent another regrettable incident, not to force resolution of every underlying difference.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Five-step processing**: Feelings → Realities → Triggers → Responsibility → Constructive Plans.
- **Balcony perspective**: Gain enough distance to describe the incident without re-entering it.
- **Two valid realities**: Hold different perceptions without litigating an inaccessible objective transcript.
- **Trigger mapping**: Connect present intensity to an older, pre-relationship wound.
- **Specific apology**: Name one’s own contribution after impact and context are understood.

## Key Concepts

- **Regrettable incident**: A fight that leaves emotional injury and unfinished business.
- **Zeigarnik effect**: Unfinished tasks or events remain more vivid in memory.
- **Subjective reality**: What one partner perceived, heard, felt, and inferred.
- **Trigger**: An old wound activated by a present resemblance.
- **Enduring vulnerability**: A tender historical spot that can be pressed again.
- **Attachment injury**: A betrayal of expected support that can block repair.
- **Balcony**: The observational stance required before processing.

## Mental Models

- **Use “two drawings of the same plant”**: vantage point changes the picture without making either view fake.
- **Use “one incident at a time”**: a pattern cannot be repaired by burying several incidents in one conversation.
- **Use “apology after understanding”**: impact must be known before responsibility can be specific.

## Anti-patterns

- **Just moving on**: the unprocessed wound continues to shape memory and later fights.
- **Processing while flooded**: the couple returns to the stage instead of observing from the balcony.
- **Arguing over the God-camera facts**: this makes incompatible memories a new contest.
- **Apologizing to end discomfort**: a fast apology may leave impact and triggers untouched.
- **Kitchen sinking the repair**: adding every pattern or incident overwhelms the single process.

## Worked Example

In the authors’ own fight, Julie says she felt criticized and unsupported after John told her to stop controlling their daughter; John says he felt attacked when Julie called for a mature co-parent. They summarize and validate each other’s realities before identifying triggers: Julie’s childhood illness and fear of missed danger, and John’s bullying and longing to protect his daughter’s new community. Each then takes responsibility for stress, poor listening, and the exact hurtful words used. Their constructive plans are concrete—John asks questions when Julie is emotional, and Julie signals the need to pause rather than launch a character attack. The result is laughter, accepted apologies, and a restored connection without needing to decide who was factually right.

## Key Takeaways

1. Do not confuse silence or a quick apology with emotional completion.
2. Wait until both partners can observe the incident calmly.
3. Validate the partner’s reality without surrendering one’s own.
4. Trace present intensity to older triggers, then take specific responsibility.
5. Use one constructive plan each to change the next communication, not to erase the original issue.
