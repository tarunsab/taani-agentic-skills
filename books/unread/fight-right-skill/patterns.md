# Patterns and Interventions

## Turn Toward the Bid
**When to use**: Daily connection is thin, or a partner’s complaint seems to arrive sideways as sarcasm, withdrawal, or a sigh.
**How**: Notice the bid; respond with attention, warmth, curiosity, or comfort; treat even a negative-sounding remark as possible longing for connection.
**Trade-offs**: Turning toward does not erase a real problem, and constant positivity without addressing issues can become avoidant drift.

The authors’ Love Lab observations make small moments consequential: masters turned toward bids far more often than troubled couples. Those responses fill the emotion bank account with goodwill that makes later conflict less brittle. A missed bid increases the chance that partners will assume bad intent and recruit the Four Horsemen. Use the pattern to strengthen the relationship before the next conflict, not as a way to dismiss the content of a bid.

## Softened Start-Up and Listening Pair
**When to use**: Raising a complaint or receiving one before persuasion begins.
**How**: Speaker names emotion, situation, and positive need; listener postpones their own case, asks clarifying questions, summarizes, and validates; switch roles only after understanding is confirmed.
**Trade-offs**: It can feel slower and less cathartic than attack/defend, and intensity can still be high; the test is the absence of blame, criticism, contempt, and kitchen sinking.

The start-up changes the listener’s available response from defending character to understanding a situation. The authors connect successful couples and successful diplomats through the same sequence: no blame, no criticism, no contempt, followed by careful listening before persuasion. Validation does not require agreement, so it can hold two different positions without pretending they are identical. Keep the issue bounded to one situation; the backlog belongs in another conversation.

## Stop Action and Self-Soothe
**When to use**: Early bodily signs of flooding appear—racing heart, heat, tight muscles, breathlessness, spinning thoughts, or the urge to attack/stonewall.
**How**: Say you are overwhelmed, name where you are going and when you will return, leave visual range, do something absorbing and soothing, then reconvene.
**Trade-offs**: A break protects the relationship but is not permission to disappear, punish, ruminate, or postpone indefinitely; return after at least 20 minutes and no later than 24 hours unless you renegotiate.

Flooding is a physiological state, not merely a bad attitude, so better reasoning is unavailable until arousal drops. The authors advise self-soothing rather than replaying the argument or rehearsing rebuttals. A communicated return time preserves safety and trust; an unannounced exit can feel like abandonment. Use “we need a break” rather than diagnosing the partner as flooded.

## Solve the Moment with Repair
**When to use**: The couple is polarized, but the whole problem cannot be solved in this interaction.
**How**: Reframe the goal as a positive, collaborative exchange; talk about self and needs, self-soothe, and make, notice, or accept small repairs.
**Trade-offs**: A positive moment is not a final solution, and repair can fail when the receiving partner is flooded or the friendship account is empty.

The authors’ “solve the moment” rule prevents perpetual problems from being judged by an impossible standard of one-conversation resolution. Repairs include apology, empathy, validation, appreciation, humor, a nod, or a hand offered when touch is welcome. Success depends less on elegant wording than on how the partner receives the attempt. Build daily knowledge of each other so that repairs are recognizable and likely to land.

## Dreams Within Conflict
**When to use**: A conflict repeats, a compromise will not stick, a small issue triggers a large reaction, or the couple is in gridlock.
**How**: Choose one speaker and one listener; ask the six Dream Catcher questions; suspend persuasion and switch roles after a full turn.
**Trade-offs**: The exercise delays the solution and can uncover genuine incompatibility; it is not a guarantee that both dreams can be honored.

Surface topics often carry beliefs about love, security, family, identity, or purpose. The speaker explains the meaning and history of a position rather than arguing it; the listener makes the exchange safe and curious. The authors’ examples—gifts as love versus gifts as manipulation, or a cabin as safety versus property as danger—show why identical childhood conditions can produce different meanings. Understanding must precede resolution; only then can a couple see whether flexibility exists.

## Accept Influence / Yield to Win
**When to use**: A decision has become a contest, one partner says no by default, or any gain feels like the other’s loss.
**How**: Ask what would be lost by giving influence and what might be gained; listen for the partner’s valid information; change one’s position where possible without abandoning core needs.
**Trade-offs**: Influence is not surrender, and compromise is not always equal in every moment; refusing all influence turns a partner into an obstacle.

The authors use emotional aikido to describe moving with a partner’s energy instead of strong-arming it. Their zero-sum research links chronic winner/loser interaction with relational and physiological cost, while accepting influence supports cooperative gain. Cultural and gendered power histories can make this harder, so the exercise requires conscious attention to who is being heard and whose humanity is protected. The relevant question is not “Who won?” but “How did both partners’ needs gain a path forward?”

## Bagel Method
**When to use**: Dreams are known but appear incompatible, and persuasion has turned into a standoff.
**How**: Draw an inner circle for nonnegotiable core needs and an outer ring for flexible details; compare timing, place, sequence, amount, duration, and other options; build a temporary or lasting plan.
**Trade-offs**: The method cannot manufacture flexibility around a truly core, irreconcilable dream, such as one partner needing parenthood and the other needing a child-free life.

The inner circle protects identity and the outer ring creates design space. Vince and Jenny’s farm-versus-sailboat conflict loosened when each saw that the core was different from the assumed logistics: they could vary where, when, how long, and in what order. A compromise is successful when both dreams are honored, not when both partners receive identical objects or time. Revisit a temporary compromise and use trust as the condition for saying yes.

## Five-Step Regrettable-Incident Processing
**When to use**: A past fight remains vivid, keeps resurfacing, produces fighting about the fight, or leaks into unrelated conflict.
**How**: From a calm “balcony” perspective, move in order through Feelings, Realities, Triggers, Responsibility, and Constructive Plans; process one incident only.
**Trade-offs**: It is never too late, but it is not for immediate post-fight flooding, patterns of ongoing betrayal, or unsafe/characterological violence that needs professional support.

The process treats both subjective realities as valid rather than asking partners to agree on a God-camera transcript. First-person descriptions reduce blame; trigger stories explain old bruises without making the current partner responsible for the past. Responsibility and apology come after impact and triggers are understood, so “sorry” can address a real injury rather than end the conversation prematurely. The final plan repairs the communication pattern, not necessarily the underlying perpetual issue.

## Safety Triage Before Couple Exercises
**When to use**: Conflict includes violence, coercive control, addiction-related harm, major betrayal, or an attachment injury that prevents trust.
**How**: Check whether both people can participate freely and safely; distinguish a single situational escalation from a pattern of domination; obtain qualified professional or domestic-violence support when needed.
**Trade-offs**: Safety can require leaving or specialized help rather than joint dialogue; a relationship exercise is not a treatment for coercion.

The authors explicitly limit their blueprint to conditions with enough safety, agency, and willingness for both partners. They describe characterological violence as a pattern involving control, jealousy, isolation, or unpredictable attack, and advise getting out rather than trying to process it as an ordinary fight. They also flag refusal to address addiction and irreconcilable parenthood dreams as possible relationship endpoints. Use the conflict tools only inside those limits.
