# Reusable Patterns

## 1. Find the blind side

**When to use:** A visible outcome depends on an unseen, time-sensitive exposure.

**How:** Work backward from the injury, failure, or loss. Identify what the decision-maker cannot see, which role absorbs the downside, and whether success removes the evidence of the threat.

**Trade-offs:** Attention to hidden risk can underweight visible production; test the causal chain rather than rewarding obscurity for its own sake.

The left tackle is valuable because a right-handed quarterback turns away from the approaching rusher. The same logic applies to operations, security, and care: a quiet protector may be more important than the person receiving the credit. The model fails if the “hidden” role is merely low-impact or if the threat has not been demonstrated.

## 2. Protect the real constraint

**When to use:** An outlier threat makes an old allocation unsafe.

**How:** Name the asset being protected, locate the shortest route to failure, and redesign the neighboring roles around that exposure.

**Trade-offs:** Extra help solves one weakness while opening another; protection is an ecological change, not a free add-on.

Lawrence Taylor made the quarterback's blind side a strategic constraint. Offenses added tight ends, backs, faster decisions, and eventually a specialized tackle. Any analogous intervention should be tested for the new gap it creates.

## 3. Match the system to the bottleneck

**When to use:** A person or team has useful ability but cannot succeed inside the current process.

**How:** Reduce unnecessary choices, decide the sequence in advance, rehearse the critical timing, and let the system amplify the available strength.

**Trade-offs:** Simplification can become brittle when conditions change; a system that protects a weak arm or a weak vocabulary still needs practice and adaptation.

Bill Walsh's short, timed passing game shows that strategy can manufacture reliability without pretending the quarterback has different gifts. Use the same distinction in teaching: reduce noise, not standards.

## 4. Diagnose access before ability

**When to use:** Low scores or poor production coexist with effort, contextual competence, or unstable living conditions.

**How:** Check food, sleep, transport, documents, prior instruction, vocabulary, safety, and trusted relationships before interpreting the score as a fixed trait.

**Trade-offs:** Context explains a performance but does not guarantee future success; keep standards while changing the conditions that make them observable.

Michael's early transcript measured years of interrupted schooling. Oral biology, visual football instruction, and later testing revealed capacities that written labels had obscured.

## 5. Make the assignment visible

**When to use:** Verbal labels or symbolic diagrams do not survive into action.

**How:** Use people, chairs, bottles, tape, or a familiar scene; name each moving part; rehearse; then test recall in the real setting.

**Trade-offs:** A concrete model can oversimplify a changing defense. Add complexity only after the base movement is reliable.

The Gap play and DeLeone's kitchen-chair lesson work because they turn coordination into spatial memory. The teaching channel should match the eventual performance channel without reducing the learner to a single “style.”

## 6. Build support as infrastructure

**When to use:** Potential exists but the surrounding routines repeatedly fail.

**How:** Pair admission with meals, a safe bed, clothes, transport, healthcare, tutoring, identity documents, monitoring, and a trusted advocate.

**Trade-offs:** Support can become dependency or be read as influence when institutional incentives overlap. Define boundaries and build the person's agency alongside the resource.

The Tuohys' intervention is effective partly because it is mundane and repeated. A compliment about talent would not have solved the missing bed, card, ride, or homework supervision.

## 7. Run a motive audit

**When to use:** Care, money, recruitment, and institutional advantage appear in the same relationship.

**How:** Record what was given, when, by whom, for what stated reason, what the recipient needed, and whether a commitment was expected in return.

**Trade-offs:** Compliance rules protect against exploitation but can misclassify necessary care; genuine care does not make conflicts of interest disappear.

The NCAA's suspicion of the Tuohys is not proof that the family acted improperly. It is a prompt to distinguish ordinary support from inducement without assuming that a rule's categories capture the whole lived situation.

## 8. Use a crisis bridge

**When to use:** A high-cost event has occurred, but the person's long-term path is still recoverable.

**How:** Secure immediate safety, get competent advice, preserve accountability, and reconnect the person to a stable routine.

**Trade-offs:** A network can prevent one bad event from becoming a permanent identity while also insulating someone from consequences. The bridge must include repair and learning.

After Michael's fight, Sean's legal and community network creates a lawful next step; Coach O's lecture supplies the behavioral boundary. The combination is stronger than either rescue or punishment alone.

## 9. Audit replication

**When to use:** One exceptional success is being proposed as a model for many people.

**How:** List every enabling input, mark which depends on rare wealth or relationships, locate near-misses, and design the scalable version around the missing links.

**Trade-offs:** Structural learning can become so cautious that it loses the original person's agency and distinctiveness. Preserve both individual choice and system conditions.

Big Zach and Arthur Sallis prevent Michael's story from being treated as inevitable. The portable lesson is a chain of access and support, not the assumption that every child will receive a wealthy family's attention.

## 10. Read price as delayed evidence

**When to use:** A market suddenly reprices a role previously treated as interchangeable.

**How:** Ask what asset is being protected, how costly failure is, how scarce credible protection is, and what new information let buyers compare alternatives.

**Trade-offs:** Price is a signal of perceived scarcity and risk, not a complete measure of causal contribution or social value.

Free agency exposes the value of left tackles after quarterbacks and blind-side rushers become expensive. The market can discover hidden work, but it can also encode bias and use an imperfect prototype as a gate.
