# Chapter 2: The Market for Football Players

## Core Idea

Tom Lemming's grainy tape of Michael Oher reveals a market that can miss a spectacular prospect when the prospect has no publicity, production record, stable address, or social network. Lemming built an information business by visiting thousands of high-school players, filtering a national pool, and selling colleges judgments that NCAA recruiting rules prevented coaches from making early themselves. His work also shows how prototypes travel down the football pipeline: what the NFL prizes on Sunday is supplied by colleges on Saturday and high schools on Friday, with a lag of several years. The left tackle becomes a rare form of quarterback insurance because the passer is expensive, the blind-side rush is dangerous, and the ideal blocker combines mass, long arms, large hands, quick feet, agility, and body control. The market therefore values a scarce combination rather than a generic large body, while also treating grades, character, and college eligibility as part of the asset's usable value. Oher is an exception to Lemming's normal production rule: his film suggested a future star even though the surrounding information system had not yet learned to see him.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The scouting sifting machine**: reduce a huge population through trusted information, film, interviews, and repeated judgment.
  - When to use: when the candidate pool is too large for direct evaluation.
  - How: narrow by reliable signals, inspect the survivors in person, and separate potential from demonstrated production.
- **Sunday-to-Saturday-to-Friday pipeline**: professional prototypes travel backward through college and high-school football.
  - When to use: when demand at the top of a system shapes supply below it.
  - How: identify the lag between a new role's emergence and the next generation's training.
- **Insurance pricing of protection**: a protective role rises in value with the asset protected, the threat, and the scarcity of credible protectors.
  - When to use: when prevention is dismissed as overhead.
  - How: compare the cost of the protected asset, the frequency and severity of failure, and the supply of people who can prevent it.

## Key Concepts

- **Production**: honors and achievements already recorded, rather than raw potential.
- **Prototype**: a recurring physical and functional profile that scouts seek for a role.
- **Sifting**: progressively filtering a large candidate pool into a small trusted list.
- **Information gap**: the missing knowledge between a prospect and a college able to use him.
- **Left-tackle type**: a very large but agile blocker with long arms, large hands, quick feet, and lower-body strength.
- **Quarterback insurance**: the value of preventing injury or disruption to an expensive passer.
- **Character risk**: the possibility that conduct, grades, crime, drugs, or instability will prevent talent from reaching the next stage.

## Mental Models

- Use **production versus potential** as two separate axes; an unusual candidate may justify an exception, but the exception needs close inspection.
- Think of a market as a **delayed learning system**: incentives change first, then institutions build the language and prototypes to describe the change.
- Treat eligibility as part of talent's **realized value**; ability that cannot survive the path to college is not yet usable in the market.

## Anti-patterns

- **Rely on reputation or local visibility**: Oher was nearly invisible in Memphis despite exceptional physical evidence.
- **Confuse a physical stereotype with a complete evaluation**: the market also needs grades, conduct, learning capacity, and role fit.
- **Assume information is neutral**: NCAA restrictions, publicity, and established networks shape who gets seen and when.

## Worked Example

Lemming's normal process moved from millions of high-school players to about 1,500 personal interviews, then roughly 400 names, a national Top 100, and a small All-American group. Oher entered through a different door: a tape showed a wall-sized player moving fast enough to chase linebackers, but he had no press clippings or production honors. In person he was enormous and silent, leaving the questionnaire blank. Lemming treated the silence as a character warning while still recognizing a possible first-round left tackle; the book later makes clear that the evaluator had mistaken missing context for a settled judgment.

## Key Takeaways

1. Build an information pipeline that reaches beyond the usual local network.
2. Price protection by the downside it prevents, not by how dramatic the work looks.
3. Separate rare physical fit from the institutional conditions needed to realize it.
4. Treat exceptions as invitations to investigate more deeply, not as reasons to discard the process.
