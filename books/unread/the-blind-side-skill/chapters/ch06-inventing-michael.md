# Chapter 6: Inventing Michael

## Core Idea

By the 2004 season Michael has been told often enough that he is a star to begin acting like one, but his coaches still need to convert raw ability into football behavior. Tim Long teaches him to "get fit": fire off the line, place the hands narrowly inside the defender's shoulder pads, and use his mass and grip without drawing an illegal broad hold. Hugh Freeze's shorthand, "fire to fit," gives Michael a concrete sequence rather than an abstract lecture. A scrimmage against Munford reveals the latent aggression: after half a game of insults, Michael lifts a 220-pound defender and carries him toward and through the opposing bench, then calmly explains that he was taking the talker to the bus. Briarcrest then turns this force into the Gap play, a deliberately simple assignment in which the running back follows Michael's right side while Michael drives straight ahead and destroys the path in front of him. Repetition lets the entire team move with conviction, while opponents respond by tackling Michael, cut-blocking him, or stacking extra defenders, forcing the coaches to adapt again. The football transformation runs alongside a social one: stable family life, a driver's license, and growing confidence make it possible for Michael to arrive at the field as someone who expects to belong there. The chapter's title is apt because coaches, family, and institutions are not creating ability from nothing; they are teaching the person and the system how to recognize and use it.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Fire to fit**: explode from the snap and establish legal, close hand position before the defender can react.
  - When to use: when a strong blocker can control a defender once contact is secured.
  - How: accelerate first, fit the hands narrowly, keep the feet moving, and continue until the whistle.
- **Gap play**: assign each lineman a gap while the back follows the left tackle's right side through the opening.
  - When to use: when one player can create a reliable point of force and the team needs simplicity.
  - How: repeat the play until timing, confidence, and the downfield path become automatic.
- **Channel the fire**: convert anger and physical force into a bounded role with rules.
  - When to use: when a person has intensity but lacks a safe, useful outlet.
  - How: give a clear target, a stopping condition, and consequences for attention-seeking excess.

## Key Concepts

- **Getting fit**: legal hand placement and body position that let an offensive lineman control a defender.
- **Gap**: the space between a lineman's inside eye and the head of the defender inside him.
- **Board drill**: a one-on-one drill in which players try to drive each other off a narrow board.
- **Force**: in Freeze's shorthand, mass multiplied by acceleration.
- **Physical intelligence**: learning a movement from demonstration and adjusting through the body.
- **Block until the whistle**: a literal rule that Michael applies with extraordinary persistence.
- **Opponent adaptation**: changes such as low tackles or extra blockers designed to neutralize a dominant force.

## Mental Models

- Use **simple assignment plus repeated execution** when a rare capability is being wasted by too many choices.
- Distinguish **controlled aggression** from indiscriminate violence; the first serves a task, the second creates new risk.
- Think of confidence as an **input to execution**: stable care can change how a person uses an unchanged body.

## Anti-patterns

- **Teach only through charts and words**: Michael learns faster from film, demonstration, and a visible target.
- **Use a powerful player without redesigning the offense**: running Michael's side only occasionally hides the very advantage the team has.
- **Reward revenge without boundaries**: the Munford block worked, but it also drew a penalty and could have started a fight.

## Worked Example

Against Treadwell, Hugh announces Gap repeatedly before the game, then calls it on the first seven plays. Michael fires through the right side, moves a 365-pound defender, and creates a path for the small running back; Briarcrest leads 14–0 before the opponent can adjust and wins 59–20. The team later uses the same principle against stronger opponents, while Michael's assignment remains concrete enough for him to remember. When a defender finally tackles him to stop the escort, Hugh adds blockers rather than abandoning the underlying advantage.

## Key Takeaways

1. Translate raw capacity into a small number of observable moves.
2. Use repetition to turn a newly understood role into team-level confidence.
3. Give intensity a target, a boundary, and a reason.
4. Expect a dominant strategy to provoke counter-strategies; keep adapting the surrounding system.
