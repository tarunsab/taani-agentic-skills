# Chapter 7: The Pasta Coach

## Core Idea

The chapter turns college recruitment into a social and political market in which every coach, parent, tutor, and sibling has a stake in Michael's decision. The Tuohys' Christmas card announces that the heavily recruited prospect is not an unattached asset but part of a wealthy, well-connected family, and that changes who can reach him and what they must promise. Leigh Anne and Miss Sue care about distance, education, supervision, food, and whether a campus can protect the support system Michael still needs; Sean manages appearances while quietly using his Ole Miss network. Coaches compete through private visits, flights, locker-room access, family promises, and offers to accommodate friends such as Justin Sparks, while NCAA rules make direct contact unusually formal and indirect. Michael tells different coaches that he likes their schools, partly because the visits and flights are enjoyable and partly because he has learned to give people the answer that ends a conversation. The adults read this as indecision, but he has already formed a deeper judgment about character, stability, and whether a coach will honor commitments to other players. The choice of Ole Miss is therefore less a free-market auction than a choice of home: the place is familiar because his family, tutor, coach, and routines can travel with him. The process also raises a limit that the book does not resolve: when a child depends on adults who control money, transport, education, and access, protection and influence are difficult to separate.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Support-system portability**: evaluate an opportunity by whether the relationships and routines that make performance possible can travel with it.
  - When to use: when a transition removes a person from the environment that stabilized them.
  - How: list the non-negotiable supports, identify who can provide them, and test the new institution's willingness to cooperate.
- **The indirect recruiting market**: when formal rules restrict direct persuasion, influence moves through families, friends, gifts, visits, and proxies.
  - When to use: when a regulated choice is surrounded by informal channels.
  - How: map each participant's interest, promise, resource, and ability to shape the decision.
- **Character through behavior**: judge a would-be leader by how he treats commitments and vulnerable people, not only by his pitch.
  - When to use: when sales language is polished and motives are mixed.
  - How: examine what happens to existing players, promised scholarships, family concerns, and inconvenient questions.

## Key Concepts

- **Official visit**: a school-sponsored recruiting trip with formal rules and travel constraints.
- **Unofficial visit**: a trip arranged through other connections, often including private aircraft in the story.
- **Legal guardianship**: the formal relationship the Tuohys later use to clarify their responsibility for Michael.
- **Support system**: tutors, family, transport, supervision, and trusted relationships that accompany the recruit.
- **Proxy influence**: shaping a choice through a friend, sibling, tutor, or access promise rather than a direct order.
- **All-access pass**: Sean Junior's bargaining symbol for the access promised by competing programs.
- **Home**: Michael's practical and emotional criterion for choosing Ole Miss.

## Mental Models

- Use the **transition test**: an opportunity is viable only if the person can still access the supports that produced current performance.
- Treat a recruitment process as a **multi-sided negotiation**, not a conversation between institution and individual.
- Look for **revealing commitments**: a coach's response to a small question can tell more than the central sales pitch.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treat the recruit as unattached property**: the coaches initially assume they are selling to an isolated prospect, not negotiating with a family system.
- **Equate many options with autonomy**: fifteen schools and endless flights do not remove the power adults hold over a dependent teenager.
- **Ignore the cost of distance**: an institution may be attractive on paper but unusable if the learner loses the support that makes attendance possible.

## Worked Example

During the visits, Michael's adults use different tactics. Miss Sue invents an absurd Knoxville warning to make the Tennessee trip less attractive; Sean Junior asks each coach what access he will receive; Leigh Anne sends Ole Miss a precise list of food, entertainment, and safety preferences. Nick Saban answers the family questions with detail, Phil Fulmer offers homespun promises, and Ed Orgeron earns Michael's respect by saying commitments to current players will remain in place and by refusing to promise an immediate starting job. Michael ultimately chooses Ole Miss because it feels like home, though he had known the answer since September.

## Key Takeaways

1. Evaluate transitions by support portability, not prestige alone.
2. Map the informal actors and incentives around a regulated decision.
3. Test character with inconvenient, concrete questions about commitments.
4. Protect the dependent person's agency when adults control the choice architecture.
