# Patterns & Techniques

These are study patterns extracted from the book, not current clinical protocols or instructions for unsupervised care.

## 1. ATLS Primary Survey
**When to use**: A major trauma presents with several simultaneous threats.
**How**: Assess Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure in that order; fix the immediately lethal problem before moving on.
**Trade-offs**: A theoretically ideal action may be too slow or unsafe in the actual scene.

ATLS gives people a shared language when the environment is chaotic and information is incomplete. Keira’s extraction was dangerous because of her possible C-spine injury, but absent breathing and circulation made immediate action more urgent. The method is valuable because it ranks threats by physiology rather than by visual drama. It still depends on training, judgment, and later definitive care.

## 2. Damage-Control Surgery
**When to use**: A patient is exsanguinating or physiologically too unstable for a complete repair.
**How**: Stop bleeding and contamination quickly, stabilize, image and reassess, then plan definitive reconstruction if the patient survives.
**Trade-offs**: The first operation is incomplete by design and leaves later work and risk.

The chapter’s emergency surgeon clamps, cuts, and resects enough bowel to control hemorrhage rather than reconnecting everything immediately. This pattern accepts imperfection to preserve the possibility of survival. It is distinct from careless or incomplete work: the limited goal is chosen consciously because physiology cannot tolerate more. It illustrates the wider book principle that “best” depends on the patient’s current state.

## 3. Support as a Bridge
**When to use**: An organ is failing, but recovery, transfer, or a definitive treatment remains possible.
**How**: Replace the essential function temporarily, monitor whether the support is working, and use the time to reach the next decision.
**Trade-offs**: Support may create complications, dependence, and a prolonged state without guaranteeing the endpoint.

Ventilators, inotropes, cardiopulmonary bypass, and Max’s LVAD all buy time rather than erase disease. The support must be understood by what it makes possible next: transfer, assessment, healing, or transplant. Max’s LVAD restored circulation but introduced driveline, clotting, stroke, and psychological risks. The bridge can become a difficult landscape in its own right.

## 4. Family-Centred Pediatric Care
**When to use**: A child faces prolonged admission, invasive treatment, or possible death.
**How**: Include parents and siblings when safe, use age-appropriate honesty, provide routine and play, and treat relationships as part of care.
**Trade-offs**: Family presence needs preparation, privacy, infection control, and respect for different capacities.

The history of Laura’s isolation shows the harm created when hospitals optimize staff convenience or infection anxiety while ignoring attachment. Max’s music, play specialists, Christmas visit, and family accommodation make the present environment more humane without pretending he is well. Keira’s sisters demonstrate that children may understand the truth and need participation rather than protective silence. The evidence base for every form of sibling involvement is not complete, so the pattern remains contextual rather than automatic.

## 5. SNOD Conversation and Donor Management
**When to use**: A patient meets potential-donor criteria and the family has begun to understand that death is unavoidable.
**How**: Let a trained specialist build rapport, start with the person’s values and wishes, explain donation alongside other end-of-life choices, and coordinate support with clinical and legal requirements.
**Trade-offs**: Too early can overwhelm; too late can lose time for testing and organ preservation.

The book presents donation as an optional part of end-of-life care, not a demand made at the instant of crisis. SNODs support grief while also managing laboratory tests, coroner contact, matching, and retrieval. The role requires emotional containment but not emotional indifference. It succeeds when the family’s consent and the organ’s safe journey are treated as parallel responsibilities.

## 6. Fair Matching Under Scarcity
**When to use**: A scarce organ must be allocated among multiple medically suitable recipients.
**How**: Code donor identity, independently check inputs, rank by defined compatibility, urgency, geography, and predicted benefit, and keep backup offers ready.
**Trade-offs**: Abstraction can reduce bias, but it cannot make loss, grief, or the algorithm’s value choices disappear.

The NHS list is described as a matching system rather than a queue. The algorithm makes the distribution criteria explicit and prevents sentiment or status from deciding the offer, while outcome data refine future runs. Tissue compatibility improves prospects but cannot guarantee survival or prevent rejection. Human staff remain responsible for explanation, family care, and the integrity of the data entering the calculation.

## 7. Moment of Honor and Last Offices
**When to use**: A deceased donor is being moved into retrieval and later returned to the family.
**How**: Pause to acknowledge the donor and gift; after retrieval, wash, dress, and present the person with deliberate care.
**Trade-offs**: Ritual takes time and may intensify emotion, but it can also steady the team and protect dignity.

The thirty-second pause before Keira’s retrieval lets staff gather themselves without denying the tragedy. The last offices then reverse the visual transformation produced by surgery: the child is cleaned, her lines are removed, her hair is combed, and her chosen dress is put on. These acts do not pretend she is alive; they affirm that death has not made her worthless. The pattern is both symbolic and practical because it shapes how families experience the final handover.

## 8. Readiness Before Irreversibility
**When to use**: Donor and recipient procedures are synchronized under a hard time limit.
**How**: Define the receiving team’s readiness condition, prepare teams and transport in parallel, verify the handoff, and delay the irreversible step until the condition is met.
**Trade-offs**: Waiting preserves recipient readiness but consumes organizational time; acting early risks wasting the donor organ.

Alison Davidson’s repeated warning to remember Max’s LVAD prevents Bristol from cross-clamping Keira’s aorta while Newcastle is still trapped in scar tissue. The donor heart’s cold clock matters, but the recipient’s open-chest dissection is the variable that cannot be rushed safely. The transplant succeeds only when both clocks are coordinated. This pattern generalizes to any system in which an irreversible action starts a perishable handoff.

## 9. Post-Transplant Balance
**When to use**: A new organ is functioning but remains vulnerable to immune attack and treatment toxicity.
**How**: Pair adherence and monitoring with education about rejection, infection, and medication risks; judge recovery by function and ordinary life as well as immediate survival.
**Trade-offs**: More immune suppression may reduce rejection while increasing infection, renal, and cancer risks.

Cyclosporine’s selective effect improved the balance but did not make immunosuppression harmless. Max’s return to food, school, music, and home therefore sits alongside exact twice-daily medication and surveillance for hyperacute rejection. The pattern treats aftercare as part of the transplant itself. It also leaves room for psychological recovery, because discharge does not erase trauma.
