# Chapter 7: Matching

## Core Idea
Matching begins with a decision that Keira’s family has already made: they are certain she would have wanted to donate, so paperwork can proceed before neurological death testing is complete. The family’s willingness does not loosen clinical safeguards; two doctors must perform repeated brain-stem tests in a specified order, after sedatives, hypothermia, biochemical abnormalities, and other confounders have been corrected, and any residual reflex or spontaneous breathing means the criteria are not met. Specialist nurses in organ donation combine communication, bereavement support, and logistical coordination, while keeping the family’s room protected from the system’s feverish activity. Once Keira is registered, the NHS algorithm treats her identity as coded data and ranks potential recipients using size, age, blood group, tissue type, urgency, geography, and expected organ survival; this is a matching system, not a simple queue. The science behind tissue compatibility is traced through Peter Medawar’s wartime work on skin grafts, which showed adaptive immune rejection and later demonstrated acquired immunologic tolerance in mice. The resulting allocation is emotionally stark: a computer run produces a ranked list for thirteen children, with nine-year-old Max at the top, while the families whose lives are not selected remain in danger.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Brain-death protocol**: Confirm irreversible loss of brain-stem function with repeated, standardized tests and conservative treatment of doubt.
- **Matching, not queuing**: Allocate a scarce organ by biological compatibility, urgency, geography, and predicted benefit rather than arrival time alone.
- **Acquired immunologic tolerance**: The immune system can learn to accept a particular foreign tissue under specific developmental conditions, a discovery that made transplant biology more hopeful.
- **Dual-track donation care**: Keep family support and organ logistics running in parallel without letting the logistics turn the donor into an object.

## Key Concepts
- **Neurological death**: Death defined by irreversible cessation of brain and brain-stem function despite maintained circulation.
- **Brain-stem reflex**: A primitive response used in the formal assessment of brain-stem function.
- **Specialist nurse in organ donation (SNOD)**: A trained professional supporting families and coordinating donation from consent through retrieval.
- **Tissue typing**: Testing donor and recipient characteristics to estimate immunological compatibility.
- **Rejection**: Immune attack against a transplanted organ recognized as foreign.
- **Allocation algorithm**: A computer process that ranks potential recipients according to defined criteria.
- **Anonymity**: Coding donor identity to protect families and reduce emotional or commercial pressure.

## Mental Models
- **Fairness requires abstraction**: Remove names and sentiment from the matching calculation, while preserving the person’s dignity everywhere else.
- **Safeguards should err toward life**: If a required test is ambiguous, do not declare brain death.
- **Compatibility is probabilistic**: A close match improves prospects but cannot eliminate rejection or lifelong treatment.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating consent as a shortcut**: A family’s yes cannot replace neurological criteria, coroner approval, laboratory testing, or safe logistics.
- **Calling the list a queue**: It creates the expectation that waiting time alone determines fairness.
- **Mistaking a good match for a cure**: Tissue typing changes risk; it does not end immune surveillance.

## Worked Example
After consent, the clinicians withdraw sedation and confirm that Keira’s unresponsiveness is not explained by drugs or physiology. A doctor tests pupil, corneal, vestibulo-ocular, gag, and breathing responses; a second doctor repeats the process, and the absence of every required response records her death at 4:02 p.m. Separately, blood samples provide infection screening and tissue typing. At the hub, coordinators enter Keira’s coded data, independently cross-check it, and run the pediatric heart algorithm against super-urgent and urgent cases. Thirteen children match her size and blood group, but Max ranks first because the combined criteria and his urgency make him the strongest match.

## Practical Application
When designing a fair allocation process, define criteria before the emotionally salient case appears, separate data entry from checking, and make the reason for each criterion legible. Pair algorithmic abstraction with human roles that explain decisions and support those who lose out. For any irreversible diagnosis, publish the safeguards, confounder checks, repetition requirements, and route for resolving doubt. The chapter’s arrangement works because the same system that strips names from the matching run leaves room for named nurses and families to carry the moral meaning of the decision.

## Limits & Caveats
The allocation model optimizes predicted outcomes under scarcity; it cannot measure love, deservingness, or the moral worth of one child against another. Brain-death tests and organ-allocation rules vary by jurisdiction and change over time. The book presents Medawar’s work as a foundational scientific advance, not as proof that all immune rejection can be prevented. Even a carefully checked algorithm inherits the criteria and value judgments built into its design, so transparency remains necessary.

## Key Takeaways
1. Consent, diagnosis, matching, and retrieval are separate safeguards in one chain.
2. Fair allocation needs formulaic criteria, independent checking, and transparent boundaries.
3. A matching algorithm can be emotionally cold without being morally indifferent if the surrounding care remains humane.
4. Compatibility reduces risk; it does not remove the need for lifelong immunosuppression.
