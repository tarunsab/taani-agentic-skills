# Chapter 25: Harry Houdini

## Core Idea

Mitnick becomes a fugitive and chooses a new identity partly as an homage to Harry Houdini, his childhood hero. He distinguishes a temporary persona from a more permanent identity and tries to make the cover coherent through records, identification, employment, and ordinary social artifacts. The narrative illustrates how identity fraud is not one document but a web of mutually supporting claims, and how every added artifact increases both believability and future exposure. Mitnick also uses telephones and intermediaries to locate a person for a private-investigation assignment, showing that his skills remain active even while he is trying to disappear. The chapter is historical and cautionary: modern identity controls should assume that plausible documents and friendly explanations can be assembled across institutions.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Identity coherence**: A false persona appears credible only when documents, biography, address, employer, and behavior agree.
  - When to use: Defensively in onboarding, identity proofing, and fraud detection.
  - How: Verify claims through independent authoritative sources and look for impossible overlaps.
- **Temporary-versus-permanent cover**: A short-term story may support movement while a long-term identity seeks durability.
  - When to use: As a threat model for identity fraud, not as a procedure.
  - How: Detect rapid identity changes, inconsistent histories, and newly assembled records.

## Key Concepts

- **Harry Houdini**: The escape artist whose name inspires Mitnick’s alias.
- **Identity proofing**: Establishing that a person is who they claim to be.
- **Credential bundle**: Multiple documents and accounts used to support one identity.
- **Believability artifact**: A business card, reference, or ordinary account that reinforces a story.

## Mental Models

Use “identity as a graph”: one document is weak, but linked documents can create false confidence. Treat rapid consistency as suspicious when all supporting records originate from the same short window.

## Anti-patterns

- **Trusting a document because several other documents repeat it**.
- **Failing to check for duplicate or deceased identities**.
- **Allowing a new applicant to choose the verification path**.

## Worked Example

Mitnick selects an identity associated with a deceased infant, obtains supporting documents, and uses a professional persona to make the request seem ordinary. The story becomes believable enough to support a job and a move. A defender would break the chain by checking authoritative records, cross-jurisdiction consistency, and whether the applicant’s history is proportionate to the claim.

## Key Takeaways

1. Verify identity against independent, authoritative sources.
2. Look for impossible overlaps and rapid document assembly.
3. Treat ordinary artifacts as part of the identity-proofing attack surface.
