# Chapter 32: Sleepless in Seattle

## Core Idea

In Seattle, Mitnick turns his attention to Mark Lottor, whose cellular experimentation kit represents another technical “trophy.” He reaches Lottor indirectly through Lile Elam, her employer, and a chain of accounts and systems. The chapter then follows a separate identity-maintenance episode: after using a Portland State record to build the Michael Stanfill identity, he encounters an old driving instructor and improvises a cover explanation well enough to pass a test. His investigation of Lottor produces a map of technical relationships, while the driving encounter shows that the same identity is also a social performance. Defensively, the chapter joins adversarial curiosity to identity lifecycle management: access paths, cover stories, records, and human recognition all interact.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Adversarial curiosity**: A difficult target becomes more attractive because it offers a challenge and a prize.
  - When to use: In threat modeling and insider-risk assessment.
  - How: Identify assets whose prestige, novelty, or symbolic value may motivate disproportionate effort.
- **Identity lifecycle**: A cover is not a single credential; it needs records, history, references, behavior, and continuity.
  - When to use: In identity proofing and fraud prevention.
  - How: Verify independent attributes at issuance, renewal, and high-consequence use.
- **Multi-source confirmation**: Strong conclusions emerge when independent records and observations agree.

## Key Concepts

- **Access-path graph**: The people, systems, employers, and accounts connecting an objective to an asset.
- **Recognition hazard**: A familiar person or place can expose a false identity without a formal investigation.
- **Trophy asset**: An asset valued for prestige or challenge beyond its ordinary utility.

## Mental Models

Model a person’s identity as a graph, not a card. Every new record adds both legitimacy and another place where inconsistencies can surface.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a strong password or a single credential as a complete identity check.
- Assuming a technical target is isolated from its people and suppliers.
- Rewarding curiosity-driven access because the target appears interesting rather than necessary.

## Worked Example

An investigator reviewing a high-risk account would map the person’s employer, device, recovery channels, and historical records, then test whether the edges agree. A driving examiner’s recognition is a low-tech example of the same control: real identity is supported by continuity across contexts.

## Key Takeaways

1. Valuable technical assets attract motivated, patient investigators.
2. Identity proofing must cover the whole lifecycle and multiple contexts.
3. Human recognition is an unpredictable but important control surface.
