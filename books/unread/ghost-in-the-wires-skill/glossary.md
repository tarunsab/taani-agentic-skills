# Glossary

Definitions below are study aids grounded in the memoir’s incidents and framed for defensive use.

## Authorization

Permission from the asset owner, with scope and accountability. Capability alone does not create authorization. (Ch 00, ch 01, ch 39)

## Call-detail record

Metadata about a call—such as time, direction, duration, and endpoints—that can reveal patterns without recording content. (Ch 19)

## Cover identity

A constructed identity whose records, biography, behavior, and relationships are intended to appear consistent. (Ch 10, ch 26, ch 35)

## Correction lure

A prompt that invites someone to fix an apparent mistake, causing them to disclose information or reveal institutional details. (Ch 04, ch 06)

## Default control

A preset credential, route, or configuration that remains usable because nobody changed or independently verified it. (Ch 15, ch 21)

## Early-warning tripwire

A change that suggests an account, system, or environment is being observed, altered, or investigated. (Ch 24, ch 30, ch 37)

## Evidence calibration

Matching the strength of a claim to the quality and independence of its evidence. A demonstrated possibility is not proof of a historical occurrence. (Ch 34, ch 39)

## Identity graph

The connected set of records, institutions, devices, references, and relationships that support or contradict a claimed identity. (Ch 20, ch 33)

## Identity triangulation

Comparing independent records and observations to determine whether an identity claim holds across contexts. (Ch 20, ch 23, ch 33)

## Information-chain attack

A route to a protected asset that crosses several people, systems, or institutions, exploiting the handoff between them. (Ch 18, ch 35)

## Layered weakness chain

A sequence in which physical, social, account, host, network, and institutional weaknesses reinforce one another. (Ch 01, ch 23)

## Metadata inference

Deriving behavior, relationships, or location from patterns around communications or records rather than their content. (Ch 19, ch 32, ch 36)

## Pretext

A claimed role, story, or circumstance used to make an unusual request seem ordinary. (Ch 03, ch 14, ch 17, ch 21)

## Rapport

The feeling of familiarity or cooperation that lowers social friction. Rapport can support service, but it is not identity proof. (Ch 03, ch 27)

## Residual trace

An overlooked artifact—paper, record, device, account, or reference—that links a person or identity to earlier activity. (Ch 25, ch 37)

## Reverse social engineering

A situation in which the target is induced to seek help from, or respond to, the person controlling the pretext or workflow. (Ch 21, ch 23)

## Scope-and-stop rule

A written boundary for an authorized test, including what is in scope, what is prohibited, and when activity must stop. (Ch 01, ch 34, ch 39)

## Social engineering

Manipulating human trust, process, or institutional knowledge to obtain an action or disclosure. (Ch 03, ch 06, ch 17)

## Source-code trophy

Technical material valued for prestige or challenge, even when it has no immediate financial use. (Ch 09, ch 29, ch 34)

## Tailgating

Physical entry by following an authorized person through a controlled boundary. (Ch 01, ch 03, ch 05)

## Traffic analysis

Studying communication patterns—frequency, timing, direction, duration, and relationships—to infer activity. (Ch 19, ch 36)

## Trust boundary

A point where one system, person, or organization accepts an assertion from another. It needs explicit authentication and least privilege. (Ch 01, ch 28, ch 34)

## Trophy motive

The desire to solve a difficult problem or possess a prestigious artifact, independent of ordinary utility. (Ch 02, ch 09, ch 35)

## Vulnerability

A condition that permits an unintended result. Testing that condition lawfully demonstrates risk; it does not establish who used it or when. (Ch 34, ch 39)
