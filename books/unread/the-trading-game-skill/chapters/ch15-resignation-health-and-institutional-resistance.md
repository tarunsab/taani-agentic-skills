# Chapter 15: Resignation, Health, and Institutional Resistance

## Source sections retained

Part Five §15. Parts One through Four have no §15. This source section is retained as a standalone note.

## Core Idea

After returning, Gary discovers that nobody is calling him to trade; instead, he is moved into HR and Business Management under Gerald Gunt. The new role supplies a huge spreadsheet, but Gary finishes it in minutes, exposing how far his capability exceeds the work being used to keep him present. Holidays, travel to Hiroshima and Miyajima, a confession to a priest, Japanese study, and drawing become a life assembled from small non-market activities. The institution still pays him, so the practical meaning of freedom is initially negative: no one asks anything, and he can reduce his work to one or two hours a day. Gary realizes that this may be the best job he has had because the bank's retention strategy has accidentally removed the work that made the money a trap. The mechanism is administrative exile, which can function as punishment, protection, or a decompression chamber depending on how the worker uses it. The distinction is between being sidelined and being released; the same low-demand state can feel humiliating or liberating. Practical application is to use reduced workload to test what remains of one's identity when status and urgency are removed. The limitation is that the experiment is financed by continued bank pay and therefore not broadly available to people without that safety net.

## Source evidence and mechanism

Gary receives no calls and is moved away from the trading floor. Gerald gives him a massive spreadsheet, which Gary completes in fifteen minutes; no further meaningful work arrives, and he is allowed long holidays. He visits Hiroshima and Miyajima, tells a priest the full story, studies Japanese, and draws Beatles figures. Gradually he works only an hour or two daily, eats ramen, and sees that a low-demand existence can be better than the supposedly prestigious job he is trying to leave. The institution has not intended to give him a life, but its attempt to isolate him removes the daily reinforcement of the old identity.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Administrative exile:** reassignment can reduce access and status while also creating the space needed for recovery.
- **Identity subtraction:** remove urgency, prestige, and PnL to see which activities remain intrinsically chosen.
- **Safety-net privilege:** freedom experiments depend on who can afford a period of institutional ambiguity.

## Mental Models

- **Empty-calendar test:** observe what fills time when no one else supplies a target.
- **Exile ambiguity:** ask whether a low-demand role is protecting health, punishing dissent, or preserving control.

## Anti-patterns

- Measuring worth only by whether the institution assigns difficult work.
- Assuming administrative isolation is harmless because it is paid.
- Treating a funded experiment as proof that anyone can leave the same way.

## Worked Example

Gary's reassignment gives him time but not an official release. He uses it to travel, learn, and reduce his hours, which produces evidence about the life he wants outside trading. A worker without his compensation should adapt the experiment to available resources—protected leave, part-time work, or a small weekly practice—rather than copying the financial conditions. The insight is to test identity before making the final leap.

## Key Takeaways

- A system can preserve control by removing meaningful work rather than increasing it.
- Reduced urgency makes intrinsic preference visible.
- The ability to wait is itself an economic privilege.
