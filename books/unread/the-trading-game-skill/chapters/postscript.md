# Postscript: Who Won?

## Source section retained

Postscript / ending scene.

## Core Idea

Years after Gary leaves, William Douglas Anthony Gary Thomas tells the story in a Harpenden pub, returning the reader to the trading floor through memory and performance. On the day Gary departed, Chuck asks who won—Gary or Citibank—and Caleb answers that Gary won. The room mutes its conference-call lines and laughs, turning the question into a shared joke rather than an official accounting. The mechanism is narrative ownership: the institution may keep records of PnL and deferred pay, but the people who lived the desk can still decide what the result means when they retell it. The scene distinguishes winning the bank's game from winning a life; the laughter recognizes both Gary's escape and the absurdity of asking a zero-sum question about it. The dedication to cold and hungry kids dreaming of becoming millionaires widens the ending from one trader to the aspiration that powers the whole system. Practical application is to ask who gets to narrate a career outcome and which costs disappear in the official version. The limitation is that the postscript is a crafted retrospective, not an independent verdict from every participant.

## Source evidence and mechanism

The later pub scene recounts a conference call on Gary's last day. Chuck asks the binary question of Gary versus Citibank, and Caleb says Gary won; the call is then muted while the remaining participants laugh. The laughter contains affection, recognition, and the knowledge that the desk's categories cannot fully contain what happened. The book closes by letting the old system acknowledge the exit in its own informal language.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Narrative ownership:** after an institutional contest, meaning is shaped by who can tell the story and who is allowed to laugh.
- **Win-condition audit:** define whether winning means PnL, autonomy, health, loyalty, or the right to choose a new game.

## Key Concepts

- Gary versus Citibank.
- Caleb's verdict.
- Muted conference call.
- Pub retelling.
- Dedication as social frame.

## Mental Models

- **Multiple scoreboards:** compare the institution's financial score with the person's autonomy, relationships, health, and future choices.
- **Retelling test:** imagine the story told by the employee, employer, colleague, and person who bore the external cost.

## Anti-patterns

- Accepting the official PnL number as the complete outcome.
- Treating a friendly retrospective verdict as proof that no harm occurred.
- Forgetting the hungry and excluded people whose aspiration feeds the game.

## Worked Example

The desk can call Gary the winner because he leaves with his story and a measure of autonomy, while the bank keeps its systems and records. Both can be true within different scoreboards. A post-project review should therefore ask what the organization gained, what the individual gained, what was lost, and who was absent from the meeting. This produces a more honest account than a single “win” label.

## Key Takeaways

- The meaning of a result depends on the scoreboard used.
- Informal memory can challenge institutional accounting without replacing factual review.
- The book's final audience is the next person who is hungry, excluded, or tempted by the towers.
