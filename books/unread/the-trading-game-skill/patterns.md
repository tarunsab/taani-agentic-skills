# Patterns

## Find the disagreement, not merely a forecast

**When to use:** When a decision depends on what the crowd already believes.

**How:** State the consensus price or narrative, then identify an observable fact it misweights. Turn that gap into a position or decision only after specifying the exit condition and the party on the other side.

**Trade-offs:** A contrarian view can be right and still lose money through timing, funding, size, or institutional limits. Gary's early card-game arbitrage and later inequality trades work because he acts on a mismatch between the quoted market and a concrete mechanism, not because he simply declares himself smarter (Ch 3, Ch 10).

## Read the economy from the ground up

**When to use:** When official models, forecasts, or market narratives feel detached from lived conditions.

**How:** Observe shops, housing, employment, debt, family spending, public services, and who is absent from the recovery. Use those observations to test whether a rate or growth forecast is plausible, then compare them with the market's priced expectation.

**Trade-offs:** Street-level evidence is vivid but incomplete; it can reveal distributional reality without giving precise timing. Bill's lesson is to add human observation to financial data, not to replace measurement with anecdote (Ch 9, Ch 10).

## Convert a risk story into a survival limit

**When to use:** Before placing a large, leveraged, or funding-dependent position.

**How:** Write the causal chain that breaks the trade, estimate the loss if it occurs, double the plausible bad case, and ask whether the person or institution can remain in the game. Separate “the thesis eventually wins” from “the position can be held until then.”

**Trade-offs:** Conservative sizing can reduce near-term PnL and status. Gary's Swiss-franc loss shows that a temporary price shock, a position that is too large, and a stop-out can destroy the benefit of being right later (Ch 6).

## Treat “easy and allowed” as a warning

**When to use:** When a trade or operating shortcut is profitable, popular, and explicitly tolerated.

**How:** Ask who authorized it, who owns the risk, whether everyone is correlated, what happens if liquidity disappears, and whether the institution is paying for tail risk. Demand an independent reason for the trade rather than borrowing comfort from participation.

**Trade-offs:** A popular trade may be genuinely attractive, and refusing it can cost opportunity. In the 2008–09 dollar-funding trade, however, scale and permission created concentration rather than safety; the same failure would have threatened the entire banking system (Ch 4, Ch 8).

## Use a benchmark to expose consensus

**When to use:** When a team needs to know whether an edge comes from skill, luck, or a shared position.

**How:** Keep one person, model, or simple strategy that represents the market's conventional view. Compare decisions and outcomes without assuming the benchmark is stupid; its disagreement is information about the consensus you are trying to beat.

**Trade-offs:** A benchmark can become a caricature if chosen unfairly. Gary's use of Titzy and later Arthur helps him see where academic or market confidence is acting as a proxy for truth (Ch 9, Ch 12).

## Build an exit route before you need it

**When to use:** When compensation, health, identity, or status makes leaving feel impossible.

**How:** Map vesting dates and contractual options, preserve lawful alternatives, maintain relationships outside the system, document decisions, and define a health boundary that is real rather than rhetorical. Assume the institution will optimize for retention, not for your freedom.

**Trade-offs:** An exit route may require giving up money, prestige, or a clean narrative. Gary's charity clause, sick leave, legal advice, and eventual administrative transfer make exit possible but slow and psychologically costly (Ch 12, Ch 15–18).

## Separate belonging from permission

**When to use:** When a team feels like family and its approval is tied to performance.

**How:** Name the relationships that provide sponsorship, learning, or care, then ask which of them would remain if PnL went to zero. Build at least one source of identity and support that the desk cannot grant or withdraw.

**Trade-offs:** Distance can reduce access to information and community. The book's desk family is genuinely formative, yet the same belonging helps Gary excuse cruelty and postpone leaving (Ch 4–11).
