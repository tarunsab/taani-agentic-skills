# Chapter 3: Prioritize Regularly

## Core Idea

Prioritization is high leverage because there are always more worthwhile tasks than available time, making every choice an opportunity-cost decision. Lau recommends one canonical, accessible backlog so the brain can reason about tasks instead of storing them across notes and memory. Rather than attempting a perfect global ranking, choose a small current set and compare the next task against alternatives as new information arrives. Favor work that directly advances a product, customer, team, or core metric, and protect important non-urgent work that prevents future fires. Maker time, limited work in progress, and implementation intentions reduce the attention tax of interruptions and starting. The exact tool matters less than a repeated rhythm of daily selection, weekly review, and occasional higher-level reassessment.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Canonical backlog**: One trusted list for commitments, ideas, and follow-up work.
  - When to use: Any time tasks are scattered across memory, notes, chat, or email.
  - How: Capture once, then select a small current queue from it.
- **Pairwise prioritization**: Compare the current candidate with the best alternative instead of pretending all estimates are precise.
  - When to use: When leverage estimates are uncertain or tasks create new tasks.
  - How: Re-run the comparison when evidence, constraints, or goals change.
- **Covey’s Quadrant 2**: Important but non-urgent work such as learning, prevention, relationships, and scalable systems.
  - When to use: Prevent recurring emergencies from consuming all attention.
  - How: Schedule and protect it before urgency makes the choice for you.
- **Work-in-progress limit**: Fewer simultaneous projects preserve context and flow.
  - When to use: Switching costs or partial work are slowing delivery.
  - How: Serialize projects where possible and make capacity visible to the team.
- **Implementation intention**: An if-then plan that connects a cue to an action.
  - When to use: Start a task that is valuable but easy to postpone.
  - How: Specify the cue, the first small action, and the time or place.

## Key Concepts

- **Maker’s schedule**: Long uninterrupted blocks needed for building, debugging, and design.
- **Manager’s schedule**: Meeting- and coordination-oriented time that fragments maker work.
- **Quadrant 1**: Important and urgent work, often made larger by neglected prevention.
- **Activation energy**: Friction between deciding to work and beginning the first concrete step.

## Mental Models

- Think of a to-do list as an external working memory, not a wish list.
- Treat interruptions as a latency tax: a brief event can destroy a much longer reasoning block.
- Ask “What important work would prevent this recurring urgent task?” after each fire.

## Anti-patterns

- **Fully sorting the universe**: Precision in a long ranking decays as tasks and information change.
- **Status as progress**: Hours, meetings, and activity can hide that no important outcome moved.
- **Unlimited WIP**: Starting more projects can make every project finish later.
- **Quadrant-3 capture**: Letting urgent but unimportant messages displace important work.

## Worked Example

Lau’s sample workflow keeps a backlog, a short Current Priorities list, and Today or Doing sections. He selects the day’s work in a five-to-ten-minute morning review, uses focused chunks such as a 25-minute timer, clusters meetings, blocks distracting sites, and records actual chunks to improve future estimates. A weekly review clears stale items and a monthly review checks whether the larger direction still makes sense. This is not presented as a universal productivity ritual; it is a mechanism for reducing context switching and making choices visible. If the team’s bottleneck is approval or unclear goals, a personal task system alone will not solve it.

## Limitations and Boundaries

Priorities are conditional on the current goal, so no static list can remain correct as product evidence, incidents, or dependencies change. Some urgent work is genuinely important, and a WIP limit must leave room for reliability and support rather than treating interruptions as moral failure. Focus blocks and if-then plans reduce friction but cannot resolve unclear ownership or a team-wide coordination problem. The book’s timing examples are sample mechanics, not requirements to use a particular timer or productivity application.

## Practical Application

Create one backlog, then select no more than a small set of current outcomes for the week. Each morning, choose the next concrete action and compare it with the best alternative; after an interruption, record the new constraint instead of silently adding more WIP. Reserve recurring blocks for learning, prevention, and relationship work before urgent demand consumes them. At the weekly review, remove stale commitments, inspect actual focus time, and update the next week’s estimate and priorities.

## Key Takeaways

1. Externalize commitments in one trusted backlog.
2. Prioritize by direct impact and opportunity cost.
3. Protect important, non-urgent prevention and learning.
4. Limit WIP and protect uninterrupted maker time.
5. Use small if-then commitments to lower starting friction.
6. Review priorities at daily, weekly, and monthly horizons.
