# Chapter 3: II. People Risk

## Core Idea

“People risk” is the risk created by who staffs public institutions, what they know, what they value, and which constituencies they can see. Ali Zaidi’s move from Pakistan to rural Pennsylvania lets Lewis show how government can form the ladder of opportunity while remaining invisible to the people climbing it. When Ali studies the USDA, he discovers an agency whose misleading name hides forests, food inspection, science, firefighting, a large rural bank, and the school lunch and infrastructure programs that supported his family. The transition story then supplies the failure mode: USDA staff prepare 2,300 pages of briefings, but the incoming team is late, tiny, focused on loyalty and symbolic targets, and unable to absorb the agency’s scale. The chapter’s key distinction is between the regulatory “stick” and the spending “carrot”: regulation is procedurally harder to change, while science grants, rural-development budgets, and organizational boxes can be redirected quickly. That asymmetry makes scientific capacity and the rural-development bank vulnerable even when headline farm subsidies remain protected by political power. Lillian Salerno’s experience shows both the value and the communication problem of public finance: a government-backed loan can create a rural company and jobs while the beneficiary believes he did it alone. Lewis presents this as a study of motives and institutional incentives, not a neutral forecast of every policy outcome; some claims about privatization and bankers are participants’ interpretations, and the chapter does not quantify the full distributional effects of USDA programs.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **People risk**: Institutional outcomes depend on the competence, motives, loyalties, and lived experience of the people placed in consequential roles.
  - **When to use**: When a new team is selected, an expertise pipeline is thinning, or a policy change appears administrative rather than substantive.
  - **How**: Examine who is appointed, what they understand, whose interests they represent, and which experienced people can still challenge them.
- **The seven little boxes**: A sprawling agency becomes more intelligible when its major organizational functions are mapped separately.
  - **When to use**: When the agency’s public label conceals many missions or when damage may be concentrated in one neglected unit.
  - **How**: Walk box by box through mission, budget, staff, beneficiaries, political exposure, and failure modes; do not assume equal vulnerability.
- **Stick and carrot**: Government regulates some behavior and pays for other work; the two levers have different political and operational fragilities.
  - **When to use**: When comparing a regulatory reform with a budget or grant decision.
  - **How**: Ask whether the government can compel the outcome, or must finance it, then protect the more easily redirected lever.
- **Invisible ladder of opportunity**: Public programs can create mobility and community capacity without their beneficiaries seeing the government behind the result.
  - **When to use**: When judging public support for a program whose value is diffuse or delivered through a local intermediary.
  - **How**: Trace the service, subsidy, loan, or infrastructure backward to its public source and make the contribution legible.

## Key Concepts

- **People risk**: Exposure created by the people selected to manage a system and by the people a system fails to understand.
- **U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA)**: A broad federal department whose work includes rural development, science, food safety, forests, and farm programs.
- **Rural Development**: The USDA function that channels low-interest loans and grants to small towns and underserved rural communities.
- **Farm and Foreign Agricultural Services**: The USDA box covering farm programs and, after reorganization, foreign agricultural affairs and trade.
- **Science box**: The USDA research function that depends on grants and can therefore be changed quickly through funding choices.
- **Food Safety**: A regulatory function whose rulemaking and legal process make it slower to alter than discretionary spending.
- **Political capital**: The support and attention needed to sustain a public program, especially one whose beneficiaries are dispersed.
- **Mission versus money**: The book’s recurring distinction between public purpose and private extraction.
- **Loyalty appointment**: A staffing choice in which allegiance to a leader substitutes for relevant expertise.

## Mental Models

- **Map the boxes before debating the label**: Understand each unit’s mission, money, people, and exposure before making an agency-wide judgment.
- **Follow the beneficiary backward**: Start with a hospital, fire station, school lunch, or business loan and identify the public infrastructure that made it possible.
- **Differentiate stick from carrot**: A regulation can be slow to repeal while a grant program can be redirected with a signature; protect them differently.
- **Motives are part of the system**: Ask what a decision-maker wants to gain, hide, or avoid knowing, not only whether the policy is technically possible.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming the agency name is its mission**: “Agriculture” hides forests, science, animal inspection, nutrition, and finance.
- **Hiring for loyalty while calling it representation**: Personal allegiance does not supply the technical knowledge needed to run a large public function.
- **Treating hospitality as a briefing**: A nice room, a mini-fridge, and a short meeting do not transfer a complex institution’s operating knowledge.
- **Making science the easiest budget cut**: Scientific work and rural capacity can be removed faster than formal regulations are changed, even when the underlying risk remains.
- **Confusing invisible public support with private self-sufficiency**: A local intermediary can hide the government contribution that made a personal or commercial success possible.

## Worked Example

Lillian Salerno builds Retractable Technologies after seeing AIDS patients go without safe care and working with an engineer on a retractable needle. A local bank lends her money through a program whose ultimate source is the Small Business Administration, but she does not realize it is government-backed until much later. The company receives patents and FDA approval, grows to 140 employees in Little Elm, and repays the loan, while a dominant incumbent later uses bundled products and litigation pressure to limit its growth. Salerno’s experience gives her a concrete view of both the opportunity created by public finance and the market power that can block a rural firm. When she later runs Rural Development, she sees the same invisibility in reverse: towns sometimes celebrate a project as self-made even though USDA supplied the loan or grant. The episode explains why rural-development work needs political capital even when it is economically consequential, and why the people who benefit may not organize to defend it. It also shows the limit of a simple “government versus market” story: public support can enable entrepreneurship, while private concentration can narrow the opportunity that follows.

## Key Takeaways

1. Treat staffing as a substantive policy decision: expertise, motives, and loyalty change what an institution can do.
2. Decompose broad agencies into functional boxes and identify where neglect can do the most damage.
3. Separate regulatory resilience from budgetary vulnerability; science and grants may disappear first.
4. Trace public value through local intermediaries so beneficiaries can see the ladder behind their success.
5. Ask where political capital comes from for dispersed or rural constituencies.
6. Check whether a reorganization protects a mission or simply makes assets easier to capture.
