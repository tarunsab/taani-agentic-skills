# Chapter 1: Prologue — Lost in Transition

## Core Idea

The prologue treats a presidential transition as a high-stakes operating handoff, not as an administrative formality. Chris Christie initially builds a serious transition for Trump: he assembles candidate lists for roughly five hundred jobs, gathers briefers for agencies, vets conflicts, and produces binders for the incoming team. Trump’s resistance to spending money or time on preparation, followed by the post-election removal of Christie and the wider transition staff, shows how quickly political suspicion and personal control can destroy institutional memory. Max Stier provides the counter-model: the federal government is a public-safety and opportunity system that manages risks too large, remote, or slow-moving for private actors to manage alone. The handoff is therefore a risk multiplier: if new leaders arrive ignorant, distrustful, or understaffed, bad outcomes become more likely and beneficial work becomes less likely. Lewis also stresses that most government failures are practical management failures rather than clean ideological disputes, such as hiring systems that require dozens of approvals and administrations that repeatedly discard predecessors’ work. The prologue’s limit is that it is a narrative diagnosis built from the 2016 transition and participant accounts, not a general evaluation of every transition or a complete design for reform.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Transition as a risk multiplier**: A bad handoff increases the probability of failure across the entire inherited portfolio.
  - **When to use**: Whenever authority changes over a complex, safety-critical institution.
  - **How**: Start before the result is known; map roles, vet candidates, prepare agency briefings, preserve access to experienced staff, and make the handoff operational on day one.
- **Government as a portfolio of risks**: Public institutions manage both visible hazards and invisible opportunities that no individual or company can carry.
  - **When to use**: When deciding what deserves institutional attention beyond the latest headline.
  - **How**: Include disasters, maintenance, public goods, and beneficial work that may never happen if capacity is lost.
- **Bright-spot visibility gap**: Government success is often quiet, while failure is highly legible and personalized.
  - **When to use**: When incentives seem to reward avoiding blame rather than producing durable public value.
  - **How**: Look for successful programs and the people sustaining them, then make their mechanism visible without pretending failure is irrelevant.

## Key Concepts

- **Presidential transition**: The legally supported period for preparing to assume federal executive power.
- **Landing team**: A small incoming group sent to an agency to learn its work and prepare the changeover.
- **Career civil servant**: A continuing government employee whose expertise can persist across administrations.
- **Institutional memory**: Knowledge of systems, risks, decisions, and operating details that is easily lost during turnover.
- **Risk portfolio**: The collection of catastrophic risks, routine obligations, and missed opportunities held by an institution.
- **Partnership for Public Service**: Max Stier’s organization for improving federal management and recruiting talent.
- **Sammies**: Stier’s awards recognizing extraordinary public-service accomplishments.
- **Political appointment**: A position whose occupant is selected by the incoming administration and may change with it.

## Mental Models

- **Handoff under a countdown**: Treat the period between election and inauguration as a compressed course in a complex system; every day without access to the right teacher is lost capacity.
- **Visible failure versus invisible success**: Ask what would stop working if competent people vanished, even if no headline currently names it.
- **Citizen, not consumer**: Evaluate government as a collective institution that supplies safety and opportunity, not only as a vendor whose services are individually purchased.

## Anti-patterns

- **“We can do the transition ourselves”**: Personal confidence cannot replace thousands of agency-specific decisions and technical briefings.
- **Treating preparation as bad optics**: Avoiding visible planning leaves the institution less ready exactly when uncertainty is highest.
- **Discarding inherited work on arrival**: Restarting every management effort creates a recurring learning deficit and wastes improvements made by predecessors.
- **Equating unfamiliarity with incompetence**: New leaders who assume career staff are lazy or stupid may destroy access to the expertise they need.

## Worked Example

Christie’s team begins by researching people for the top five hundred federal jobs and by creating agency lists of informed people who can brief the new administration. Trump’s first sustained attention to the effort comes through a newspaper story about the money it has raised, not through the substance of the work. When he orders the transition shut down, Bannon reframes the decision as a public signal that Trump expects to win, and Trump agrees to keep it alive for that reason. After the election, Christie is removed, the staff’s access to the new building is restricted, and their work is treated as disposable. The incoming team therefore loses the very people and binders designed to reduce the learning burden. The episode illustrates the prologue’s central mechanism: a transition can fail before policy begins because money, loyalty, and optics displace preparation. It also shows why institutional continuity is a safety issue rather than a courtesy to the outgoing administration.

## Key Takeaways

1. Make leadership transitions a formal knowledge-transfer process with owners, dates, and access.
2. Inventory the whole public-risk portfolio, including quiet successes and opportunities that can disappear.
3. Preserve career expertise long enough for incoming officials to understand what they are changing.
4. Separate practical management continuity from ideological disagreement.
5. Reward and communicate prevention and success, not only visible failure.
