# Patterns

## Build the transition as a knowledge handoff
**When to use**: Authority is changing over a complex public or safety-critical system.
**How**: Start before the result is known; map roles, vet candidates, prepare agency briefings, arrange access, and retain experienced staff through the handoff.
**Trade-offs**: It costs time and money before success is certain, and visible preparation can be misread as premature celebration.

Lewis’s prologue shows that the handoff itself changes the risk of every later decision. Christie’s team gathered lists and briefers, but the work became useless when the incoming leadership treated preparation as optional and expelled the people who held the context. The source’s practical warning is that roughly seventy-five days is not enough to learn a government from scratch. Continuity is therefore an operating control, not a courtesy to the outgoing team.

## Plot the risk portfolio, then look past the vivid events
**When to use**: A complex institution has more hazards than leadership can address equally.
**How**: Inventory risks, plot probability against consequence, protect the high/high quadrant, and ask what slow or systemic risk the list makes easy to forget.
**Trade-offs**: A simple grid clarifies attention but cannot capture every dependency, uncertainty, or risk of omission.

MacWilliams used a two-axis graph for about 150 DOE risks, while keeping severe low-probability events visible. The “fifth risk” is the follow-up question: what is not being imagined because it is slow, technical, or politically inconvenient? BAFU names large projects where billions can hide poor management. Use the grid as a prompt for inquiry, not as a substitute for judgment.

## Walk the agency’s little boxes
**When to use**: An agency name hides several functions with different beneficiaries and political exposures.
**How**: For each box, map mission, budget, expertise, delivery channel, constituency, and likely failure modes; compare which levers can be changed quickly.
**Trade-offs**: Decomposition improves focus but can obscure dependencies between boxes if the map is treated as a set of silos.

The USDA’s seven-box organization chart gives former officials a way to understand a sprawling department quickly. Lewis stresses that wildfire management, food regulation, science grants, farm subsidies, and rural development are not equally vulnerable to an incoming administration. This pattern also exposes the “stick and carrot” asymmetry: formal regulation is slow to rewrite, while money and staffing can move quickly. Use the map to find the quiet box where neglect or capture would matter most.

## Fund uncertain work as a portfolio
**When to use**: Important innovation has long payoffs, broad spillovers, or risks the private market will not carry.
**How**: Make small, competitive grants or loans, accept individual failures, inspect the portfolio, and preserve the scientific capability that lets later private capital scale success.
**Trade-offs**: Failure is visible and politically exploitable even when the overall portfolio creates public value.

ARPA-E and the DOE loan program embody this pattern. ARPA-E made roughly $300 million in small grants and accepted a very low approval rate for plausible but unconventional ideas; the loan program helped finance Tesla and utility-scale solar while still being attacked through Solyndra. The book’s argument is not that every public investment works, but that early-stage energy innovation often cannot be privatized without losing the reason for public support. Judge the portfolio’s mission and spillovers rather than the most marketable failure.

## Steward the long fuse
**When to use**: A project creates obligations that outlast administrations, budgets, or current staff.
**How**: Record the hazard, fund maintenance and successor capacity early, test assumptions, and surface the cost of delay before shortcuts become the default.
**Trade-offs**: Spending now is politically harder than postponing a cost that may fall on someone else.

Hanford shows the accumulated cost of wartime shortcuts, weak records, aging containment, and reluctance to study the full risk. The same principle applies to nuclear expertise, grid equipment, data archives, and satellites. Sullivan’s satellite planning demonstrates the positive version: budget for several successors instead of waiting for one asset to fail. A long-fuse risk is present even when the public cannot see an immediate incident.

## Use ensemble forecasting to expose uncertainty
**When to use**: A system is chaotic and a single prediction would hide how sensitive the result is to imperfect inputs.
**How**: Vary reasonable initial conditions, run the model repeatedly, measure agreement or spread, and communicate a range or cone that supports action.
**Trade-offs**: Uncertainty is more honest but can be harder for users and media to communicate than a single path.

DJ Patil’s weather example shows why the spread matters: a storm can be severe yet predictable, or unstable without being the most severe. Ensemble forecasts shift the odds rather than producing prophecy. The source suggests that forecast horizons should expand or contract with predictability, not follow a fixed five-day convention. Preserve the uncertainty signal when translating the model for decision-makers.

## Make the threat real through a trusted voice
**When to use**: Accurate information is not changing behavior.
**How**: Study the receiver, translate technical measurements into local consequences, use a trusted intermediary, and make the call to action clear without hype.
**Trade-offs**: Concrete language can save lives, but sensationalism can reduce trust in every later warning.

Klockow and Sullivan distinguish knowing what a tornado can do from imagining that it can happen here. In Elk City, a local emergency manager who trusted the NWS helped turn a prototype signal and unusual wording into earlier action. The source criticizes both jargon-heavy bulletins and private media hype: each can make warnings feel unreal. Human behavior is part of the forecasting system.

## Put the odd group in the room and make small bets
**When to use**: The dominant technical group cannot explain user behavior or cannot yet define the right mission.
**How**: Invite outsiders who understand the missing problem, give them influence, let conversation change the design, and test small interventions before forcing a top-down transformation.
**Trade-offs**: The process is slower and less controllable than transmitting a finished plan, and experts may resist sharing authority.

Sullivan used this method after Challenger by bringing teachers, museum professionals, curriculum designers, publishers, video specialists, and an architect into a new network; the resulting Challenger Centers reached millions of students. She later applied the same logic to NOAA by making social science part of its mission. The source’s boundary is important: the odd group must be able to shape the work, not merely attend a meeting. Small bets preserve learning when the correct intervention is uncertain.

## Turn public data into a decision product
**When to use**: Valuable records exist but are inaccessible, siloed, or disconnected from a user’s decision.
**How**: Obtain the historical data, move it into usable computing infrastructure, combine relevant sources, estimate probabilities, and present recommendations in the user’s context.
**Trade-offs**: Opening data creates public and commercial value but raises conflicts over access, privacy, credit, and who captures the benefit.

The Climate Corporation combined NOAA weather data with USDA field and Interior soil data, then showed farmers field-specific risks, timing, and financial consequences. The product began as insurance and became a knowledge business because users returned to learn from the data. Patil’s examples—IRS mobility research, policing patterns, and unanswered traffic-fatality questions—show the same pipeline at public scale. A pile of facts is not yet a public capability: access, analysis, and interpretation complete the chain.
