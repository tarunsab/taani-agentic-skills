# Cheatsheet

## Decision rules

| If you see… | Do this… | Because… |
|---|---|---|
| A leadership change over a complex system | Build the handoff before the result; map roles, briefings, access, and continuity | A bad transition multiplies every downstream risk |
| Incoming leaders say they can learn later | Put career experts and operating context in the room immediately | The learning window is short and expertise disperses |
| A risk list full of dramatic events | Ask which slow, systemic, or unimagined risk is missing | The fifth risk is often a long fuse, not a headline |
| Too many risks for equal attention | Plot probability × consequence, then inspect the high/high quadrant and severe omissions | The grid prioritizes without making rare catastrophes invisible |
| A sprawling agency with a misleading name | Walk its organizational “boxes” one by one | Mission, money, and vulnerability differ by unit |
| A proposed reform changes money or staffing | Check it before assuming regulation is the main lever | Spending and science capacity can move faster than formal rules |
| Markets reject uncertain, long-horizon research | Consider a competitive public seed portfolio and judge the whole portfolio | Early innovation creates spillovers markets may not finance |
| A warning is accurate but users do not act | Study the receiver; use concrete consequences and a trusted local voice | People respond to meaning and credibility, not raw data alone |
| Forecasts disagree | Show the ensemble spread or cone of uncertainty | A range reveals predictability and supports better decisions |
| A data asset is treated as a private product | Trace who funded collection and whether public access is being restricted | Public infrastructure can create both safety and private value |
| A project has a long life or dangerous residue | Fund successors, records, maintenance, and honest risk study now | Delay turns shortcuts into future liabilities |
| The technical team cannot explain behavior | Add the missing “odd group” and give it influence | Human, local, or behavioral knowledge may be the missing mechanism |

## Fast decision tree

1. **Name the system.** What does the institution actually do beyond its public label?
2. **Map the portfolio.** Which risks are visible, systemic, slow, or opportunities that can fail to happen?
3. **Locate the lever.** Is the outcome controlled by expertise, regulation (“stick”), spending (“carrot”), data, or trust?
4. **Check the people.** Who has the knowledge, who is being appointed, who benefits, and who bears the delayed cost?
5. **Protect continuity.** What must be retained, funded, or made intentional before the next deadline?

## Defaults and thresholds from the book

- Treat the post-election/pre-inauguration period as roughly **75 days** of compressed learning.
- A crude but useful risk inventory can contain **~150 risks**; use a two-axis probability/consequence grid to order attention.
- ARPA-E approved about **2 of every 100** proposals: high selectivity can coexist with portfolio-level public value.
- In chaotic forecasting, let the **spread of repeated runs** influence the forecast horizon; do not force every situation into the same five-day window.
- For public warnings, make the threat feel real without making unsupported worst-case claims; trust is an asset that hype can erode.

## Tells and smells

- No incoming team shows up, or a briefing is treated as a photo opportunity.
- The first question is “Who did the previous administration employ?” rather than “What does this system do?”
- A public program is judged by one visible failure while its portfolio wins are ignored.
- Scientific data, inspection records, or public links disappear after a commercial or political interest appears.
- Appointments share loyalty but not relevant knowledge.
- A large project has shortcuts, weak records, no successor plan, or incentives for stakeholders not to know the full risk.
