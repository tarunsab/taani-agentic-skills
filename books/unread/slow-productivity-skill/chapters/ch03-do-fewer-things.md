# Chapter 3: Do Fewer Things

## Core Idea

The first principle asks workers to reduce obligations until they can imagine completing them with time to spare, then use the margin to advance the small number of projects that matter most. Jane Austen’s most productive periods followed reductions in domestic and social responsibilities, which counters the popular myth that her achievement came from squeezing writing into ever smaller gaps. In modern knowledge work, every active commitment adds an overhead tax of coordination, memory, meetings, and switching; four simultaneous reports can consume so much overhead that one report takes twice as long as it would in focused sequence. Doing fewer things therefore increases both the quantity of useful hours and the quality of the thinking performed in them. Newport argues that overload is not inevitable: many semiautonomous workers regulate requests with stress, saying no only when the discomfort of refusal is finally lower than the discomfort of overload. The practical response is to limit the big at the scales of missions, projects, and daily goals, while containing the small tasks that otherwise erode attention. For work assigned by others, a pull system—team-wide if possible, simulated individually if necessary—prevents new work from becoming active before capacity exists. The principle is also humane: reducing the load can protect parents, caregivers, and people facing illness from a system that otherwise makes them negotiate every boundary alone.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Principle #1: Do fewer things**: Reduce obligations to create margin, then concentrate on the few projects that matter most.
  - **When to use**: When the list is full, attention is fragmented, and core output is being pushed into nights or weekends.
  - **How**: Apply limits at multiple scales and contain the small work that remains.
- **Limit the big**:
  - **Missions** — ongoing goals or services that direct professional attention. Two or three is presented as a tractable minimalist range; five or more commonly feels drowning, though the correct number depends on role and resources.
  - **Projects** — initiatives requiring more than one session. Estimate their real time, block that time on the calendar, and decline or replace work that has no honest space.
  - **Daily goals** — keep important project work to at most one major target per day; meetings, email, and administration can still occur.
- **Contain the small**: Put recurring tasks on an autopilot schedule; use rituals and locations to reduce the thinking needed to start them. Use office hours or team docket-clearing meetings to replace sprawling asynchronous conversations. Use reverse task lists or custom processes so requesters specify and complete more of the work around their requests. Choose projects by their task footprint, not only by duration, and spend money on tools or people when off-loading small work buys back meaningful attention.
- **Pull instead of push**: Admit new work only when the next stage or person has capacity. For an individual, keep unlimited incoming projects in a **holding tank** and no more than three in an **active list**; pull a new item only when a slot opens.
- **Transparent intake and cleaning**: Acknowledge a new project with missing-information requests, the number of existing commitments, and a completion estimate. Update estimates when they change, and review the holding tank and deadlines weekly; release or delete work that is languishing, obsolete, or redundant, while telling its source.

## Key Concepts

- **Administrative overhead / overhead tax**: The ongoing coordination and cognitive burden attached to a commitment.
- **Active list**: The small set of projects currently receiving focused effort.
- **Autopilot schedule**: Recurring time, place, and ritual assignments for regular task categories.
- **Holding tank**: A nonactive queue for projects that have been accepted but are not yet pulled into work.
- **Mission**: An ongoing goal or service that directs attention.
- **Office hours**: A recurring window for resolving many small questions in real time.
- **Project**: Work requiring multiple sessions.
- **Reverse task list**: A public list where requesters record complete task details themselves.
- **Task engine**: A project whose main burden is generating many urgent small obligations.
- **Pull workflow**: Capacity-limited admission of new work.

## Mental Models

- **Work-in-progress is a tax multiplier**: limit concurrent projects before optimizing the tools used to track them.
- **Protect the big by containing the small**: the goal is not to escape every task, but to stop each one from repeatedly claiming attention.
- **Evidence beats a vague “busy”**: a calendar estimate makes a refusal legible and defensible.
- **Relief beats apparent expediency**: people often accept slower work when they trust that it has been owned, estimated, and will not disappear.

## Anti-patterns

- **Using stress as the only boundary**: it keeps the worker at the maximum painful workload that still seems survivable.
- **Making yourself unpleasant to avoid projects**: Richard Feynman’s “irresponsible” persona eventually failed when NASA recruited him for the Challenger commission; unqualified refusal is not a stable system.
- **Choosing the short-looking task engine**: an event may finish on one date yet generate more coordination than a difficult report.
- **Free-only optimization**: refusing reasonable software, operations help, or professional services can preserve a low cash bill while destroying the attention needed for high-value work.

## Worked Example

The Broad Institute’s genetic-sequencing pipeline initially pushed samples from one stage to the next as soon as each technician finished. Slow stages accumulated backlogs, finding a requested sample could take two days, and turnaround reached 120 days. The institute switched to pull: each stage drew from a tray only when ready, and a full tray exposed whether upstream work was too fast or downstream work too slow. Machine utilization more than doubled and processing time fell by over 85 percent. Its technology group adapted the same idea with a wall of staged Post-it notes, weekly reviews, visible capacity, and a rule that a project advanced only when an engineer could pull it in; projects underway fell nearly 50 percent while completion improved. An individual can approximate this logic with a three-item active list, a holding tank, transparent estimates, and weekly cleaning.

## Key Takeaways

1. Limit missions, projects, and daily targets together; a leak at one scale recreates overload at the next.
2. Schedule the real time before accepting a project, and treat missing space as evidence that something must be declined or displaced.
3. Automate, synchronize, delegate, or add friction to small work so the important work gets protected.
4. Prefer a pull system; if the environment pushes, simulate pull with a holding tank and a three-project active limit.
5. Maintain trust through explicit estimates, updates, and transparent release of stale commitments.
