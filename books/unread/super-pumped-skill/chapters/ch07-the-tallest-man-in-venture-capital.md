# Chapter 7: The Tallest Man in Venture Capital

## Core Idea

Chapter 7 introduces Bill Gurley, the Benchmark partner who recognized Uber as a potentially enormous marketplace rather than merely a nicer black-car service. Gurley’s background as an analyst and contrarian investor made him attentive to two-sided network effects: more drivers improve availability, more riders make driving attractive, and the resulting activity can reinforce itself. He built a relationship with Kalanick, arranged an informal “jam” at the W Hotel, and reached a handshake agreement for Benchmark to invest $11 million at roughly a $50 million valuation for just under 20 percent and a board seat. The deal gave Uber capital and a sophisticated advocate, but it also created a future governance problem because the investor who admired Kalanick’s tenacity would eventually have to decide whether that tenacity had become dangerous. The chapter distinguishes founder-friendly support from permanent deference: a good investor can help a founder gain power without surrendering the ability to challenge him.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Two-sided network effect:** Growth on each side of a marketplace increases the value of participation on the other.
- **Founder-friendly investing:** Support a founder’s ambition while preserving enough information and governance to protect the company.
- **Conviction through relationship:** A high-risk investment is often won through repeated contact and trust, not a single spreadsheet.

## Key Concepts

- **Bill Gurley:** Benchmark investor and later Uber director who became Kalanick’s closest institutional ally and fiercest opponent.
- **Benchmark:** A venture firm whose concentrated partnership model emphasized large outcomes and close founder relationships.
- **$11 million round:** The early Benchmark investment that supplied credibility, cash, and board-level influence.

## Mental Models

- **Network effects amplify both value and damage:** A growing marketplace can become harder to displace, but a bad norm can spread just as efficiently.
- **Board access is an option:** Its value appears when the company’s direction, leadership, or controls need to change.

## Anti-patterns

- **Mistaking founder intensity for a complete strategy:** Tenacity can create adoption without resolving governance or economics.
- **Using friendship as oversight:** Personal trust makes hard conversations slower precisely when they matter most.

## Worked Example

Gurley was persuaded that Uber could grow far beyond a luxury car service and negotiated directly with Kalanick until the two shook hands on the investment. Benchmark’s money and reputation helped Uber recruit and expand. The same closeness later made Gurley responsible for confronting a founder he had helped empower.

## Key Takeaways

- Venture capital accelerates a marketplace when the investor understands the system, not just the product.
- A board seat can be both a support mechanism and a delayed accountability mechanism.
- Early alignment between investor and founder can become a source of conflict when the company outgrows the relationship.
