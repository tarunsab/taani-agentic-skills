# Postscript: The Public-Market Test

## Core Idea

The postscript tests Uber’s private-market mythology against its 2019 initial public offering. Bankers marketed a possible $120 billion valuation, while Khosrowshahi’s compensation depended on taking the company public above that threshold and holding the valuation for a sustained period. During the roadshow, investors questioned Uber’s losses, its path to profitability, and even the loyalty of SoftBank, which was funding competitors; the target fell before the listing. Uber priced its shares at $45, but the first trade settled around $42, producing the largest dollar loss on an American IPO since 1975 according to the account. Kalanick, Garrett Camp, and Ryan Graves still became billionaires on paper, yet their financial success coexisted with a public market unwilling to reward the old growth story at its private valuation. The distinction is between private capital’s option on future dominance and public investors’ demand for evidence, governance, and economics. The book closes with the possibility that Uber’s most lasting effect is not a single company but a generation of founders, investors, and employees deciding what kind of ambition the next platform should normalize.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Public-market reality check:** Subject private valuation, growth claims, and founder mythology to broader investor scrutiny.
- **Compensation–valuation alignment:** Executive incentives can make a headline valuation a personal objective as well as a company objective.
- **Unicorn normalization:** A once-unprecedented company becomes a benchmark that shapes the next cycle of funding and ambition.

## Key Concepts

- **IPO roadshow:** Process in which banks and management market the company to public investors.
- **$120 billion target:** Valuation aspiration used in Uber’s preparation and executive compensation terms.
- **$45 to $42 debut:** The offering price and first trade that signaled a weak public-market reception.

## Mental Models

- **Demand proof at the exit:** A private valuation is a hypothesis until diverse public investors accept the price.
- **Scale without profit has a deadline:** Capital can finance losses for years, but an IPO forces a clearer account of the path forward.

## Anti-patterns

- **Anchoring on the last private round:** A prior valuation is not a promise that public investors will underwrite the same story.
- **Treating a large user base as self-justifying:** Users, revenue, margins, safety, and governance remain separate tests.

## Worked Example

Uber arrived at the New York Stock Exchange with black merchandise, McDonald’s food, and a ceremony designed to celebrate the platform’s reach. Yet the stock fell below its $45 offer price almost immediately and the celebration became a warning about private-market excess. The founders’ wealth demonstrated the enormous value created, while the weak debut showed that value and public confidence were not the same thing.

## Key Takeaways

- An IPO converts a founder-led story into a public accountability event.
- A successful private fundraising history does not prove durable economics.
- The next generation can learn from Uber’s ambition without copying its tolerance for opacity and harm.
