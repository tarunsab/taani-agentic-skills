# Chapter 26: Fatal Errors

## Core Idea

Chapter 26 compresses Uber’s 2017 crises into a picture of controls failing in parallel. The Holder investigation was still pending; Levandowski was removed after invoking the Fifth Amendment in the Waymo case; a diversity effort and the “brilliant jerks” debate exposed how hard it was to change the talent system; and security groups such as Hell, COIN, and SSG used encrypted communications, impersonation, scraping, and attorney-client privilege to conduct covert work. The chapter also describes a possible Foreign Corrupt Practices Act risk in Indonesia, where local staff used cash to secure police cooperation, and Eric Alexander’s retention of an Indian rape victim’s medical file, which led to his firing and further executive departures. As complaints accumulated, senior leaders asked for an independent chairman, the removal of Emil Michael, and Kalanick’s leave. The sudden death of Kalanick’s mother and his father’s serious injuries added a personal catastrophe to the organizational one, while his written promise that Uber needed to “grow up” could not substitute for action. The distinction is between isolated errors and a control environment that makes secrecy, loyalty, and speed easier than review, disclosure, or accountability.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Control-stack failure:** Multiple weak controls interact, so legal, safety, diversity, and security issues reinforce one another.
- **Brilliant-jerk trade-off:** A company may tolerate abusive high performers because it believes their talent is scarce.
- **Crisis compression:** Personal tragedy and corporate accountability can collide, making governance both more urgent and more emotionally difficult.

## Key Concepts

- **Hell/COIN/SSG:** Internal names associated with Uber’s covert monitoring, competitive intelligence, and security activities.
- **Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA):** Legal framework implicated by cash payments to foreign officials or police.
- **Independent chair:** Governance reform demanded by executives and investors to counter founder control.

## Mental Models

- **Test interactions, not policies in isolation:** Ask how recruiting, secrecy, legal privilege, and growth incentives combine in practice.
- **Crisis response needs a decision owner:** A committee without authority can let a fast-moving risk survive until it becomes public.

## Anti-patterns

- **Using encrypted channels as a substitute for governance:** Privacy tools protect sensitive work but can also erase accountability.
- **Protecting the company’s image by withholding facts:** Concealment compounds the original failure and harms later investigators.

## Worked Example

Uber’s security and intelligence teams could use Wickr auto-deletion, impersonation, and access to sensitive information while leaders argued about whether the work was defensive or competitive. Separately, Alexander’s possession of a rape victim’s medical file showed that executive access had outrun ethical boundaries. The two episodes shared a deeper problem: powerful employees operated in gray zones until a reporter, lawyer, or board member forced a reckoning.

## Key Takeaways

- Repeated failures across functions indicate a governance design problem, not a public-relations streak of bad luck.
- Confidentiality must preserve evidence and rights, not merely protect the company from scrutiny.
- A founder’s personal crisis can deserve compassion while still requiring independent corporate decisions.
