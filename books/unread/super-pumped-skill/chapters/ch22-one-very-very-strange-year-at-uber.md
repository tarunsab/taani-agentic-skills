# Chapter 22: “One Very, Very Strange Year at Uber…”

## Core Idea

Chapter 22 follows Susan Fowler’s experience as an engineer and makes the company’s culture legible through a sequence of small, repeated failures. On her first day, a manager propositioned her; HR said it was a first offense and protected a high performer, so Fowler changed teams rather than seeing the manager disciplined. She later found that the same manager had repeated the behavior and that review labels and promotion rules were arbitrary, while an 85 percent male engineering workforce made the imbalance hard to ignore. Fowler also encountered a free-iPhone program that enabled theft and fraud, an Xchange lease program that left drivers with unsafe cars and losses, and a jacket decision in which six women were denied access because a bulk purchase would not be “equal.” The distinction is between isolated bad actors and a system whose incentives repeatedly minimize complaints, worker costs, and minority needs. Fowler’s blog became powerful because it did not depend on one dramatic incident; it documented how ordinary processes made misconduct rational to tolerate.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **High-performer exception:** Lower the consequence for misconduct when a person is seen as valuable to growth.
- **Process as culture:** Review systems, procurement, and HR rules encode whose needs count.
- **Whistleblower narrative:** A detailed account can connect seemingly separate incidents into a systemic pattern.

## Key Concepts

- **Susan Fowler:** Site reliability engineer whose 2017 blog post exposed Uber’s workplace and management failures.
- **T3/B3 reviews:** Arbitrary performance labels Fowler encountered in the review and promotion process.
- **Xchange:** Uber-linked vehicle lease program associated with driver financial and safety problems.

## Mental Models

- **Look for recurrence after reporting:** If a complaint changes the victim’s location but not the offender’s behavior, the system is preserving the problem.
- **Fairness is not sameness:** A formally equal rule can reproduce exclusion when starting conditions are unequal.

## Anti-patterns

- **Protecting a high performer from standards:** It teaches everyone that output buys permission.
- **Solving complaints by moving the complainant:** Reassignment can reduce immediate conflict while preserving structural harm.

## Worked Example

Fowler was told that a manager’s proposition was not serious enough for termination, then discovered the manager had behaved similarly toward other women. She also saw women denied a jacket because Uber would not buy enough for a bulk discount, even though men had access to the same benefit. Her blog connected the HR logic, gender imbalance, and arbitrary “equality” into one account of culture.

## Key Takeaways

- Organizational culture is often visible in mundane decisions, not only scandals.
- A policy that protects performance can make reporting irrational.
- Detailed first-person evidence can reveal a system that aggregate metrics obscure.
