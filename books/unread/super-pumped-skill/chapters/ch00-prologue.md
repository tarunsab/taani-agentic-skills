# Prologue: Super Pumped

## Core Idea

The prologue presents Uber’s growth method as a repeatable operating system: enter a city before permission is secured, create enough supply and demand to make the service feel inevitable, and force regulators to respond after the fact. In Portland in 2014, the company paired a local general manager, Craigslist recruiting, sign-up bonuses, long hours, and a willingness to evade enforcement with a founder slogan—“protect drivers, trick cops, unleash Uber.” Greyball later extended that adversarial logic into software by identifying likely regulators and showing them a harmless simulation rather than the real service. The narrative distinguishes deliberate confrontation from ordinary regulatory risk: Uber treated law, labor rules, and public officials as obstacles to route around, while investors and employees celebrated the speed that resulted. The prologue also sets the book’s limits and themes—tech versus regulation, founder worship, venture governance, sexism, data privacy, and the human cost of a company optimized for winning.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Launch-before-permission:** Build visible usage first, then negotiate from the political pressure created by users and drivers.
- **Principled Confrontation:** Reframe noncompliance as evidence of courage and moral clarity when leadership believes a rule is “bullshit.”
- **Adversarial scaling:** Treat regulators, competitors, and critics as opponents in a contest rather than as stakeholders in a shared system.

## Key Concepts

- **City-launch playbook:** A local operator recruits drivers, subsidizes early rides, and works long hours until the marketplace has momentum.
- **Greyball:** A code-supported system for withholding the real Uber service from selected accounts, including suspected regulators.
- **Founder ideology:** A set of slogans and stories that turns exceptional effort and rule-breaking into identity.

## Mental Models

- **Momentum can become leverage:** Early adoption is not only a product signal; it can become bargaining power against institutions.
- **A playbook carries its ethics with it:** Repeating a growth mechanism across cities also repeats its assumptions about consent, legality, and who bears risk.

## Anti-patterns

- **Confusing speed with legitimacy:** Launching quickly can expose a company to fines, safety failures, and public distrust that growth metrics do not capture.
- **Turning every constraint into an enemy:** An adversarial frame can suppress learning from regulators, workers, and communities.

## Worked Example

Uber entered Portland without authorization and used local recruiting, incentives, and a large driver base to make the service hard to remove. When enforcement pressure rose, the company did not simply pause; it used tactics intended to protect the launch and keep the confrontation asymmetric. Greyball shows the same logic in a different layer: instead of debating enforcement directly, the company used information and code to reduce an authority’s visibility into its operations.

## Key Takeaways

- Repeatable city launches can create powerful network effects, but they also scale legal and ethical exposure.
- Culture determines whether a tactic is seen internally as ingenuity or as misconduct.
- The book evaluates Uber’s success together with the systems and people it pressured along the way.
