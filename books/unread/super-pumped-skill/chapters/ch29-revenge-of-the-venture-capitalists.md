# Chapter 29: Revenge of the Venture Capitalists

## Core Idea

Chapter 29 narrates the June 2017 investor coup that removed Kalanick as Uber’s CEO. Benchmark sent Matt Cohler and Peter Fenton to confront Kalanick in Chicago, where he was interviewing a potential chief operating officer; their letter demanded immediate and permanent resignation, board reform, a CEO search, and a qualified CFO. Kalanick stalled, called allies, tried to flip shareholders, and threatened an ugly fight, while the syndicate prepared to make the dispute public through the New York Times. After the death of his mother and weeks of exhaustion, Kalanick finally agreed to a negotiated exit that kept him on the board and replaced Gurley with Cohler, but a leak exposed the confrontation as an investor coup rather than the graceful departure planned by both sides. The distinction is between legal control and practical legitimacy: Kalanick still possessed stock and allies, yet the company could no longer be credibly led by him. The episode also shows the moral ambiguity of investor intervention—Benchmark had helped create the founder-centric system and acted only after scandal threatened the company and its return.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Coup by coalition:** Combine contractual rights, shareholder alignment, deadlines, and media pressure to remove a founder.
- **Soft landing:** Preserve the departing leader’s dignity and continuing board role to reduce disruption.
- **Narrative control:** Manage public disclosure because the same governance event can be framed as succession or betrayal.

## Key Concepts

- **Matt Cohler/Peter Fenton:** Benchmark partners selected to deliver the resignation demand.
- **Moving Uber Forward:** Investor letter proposing Kalanick’s resignation and governance reforms.
- **Founder–investor betrayal:** Kalanick’s interpretation of the ouster as the VC betrayal he had always expected.

## Mental Models

- **A negotiated exit needs enforceable boundaries:** A title change is unstable if the former leader can still direct employees or control appointments.
- **Public process affects private outcome:** Leaks can alter trust, leverage, and the terms of the settlement even after agreement.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating a crisis as a personal loyalty test:** Kalanick’s calls to former allies made a governance decision more coercive and chaotic.
- **Using a soft landing to avoid the real story:** Euphemisms can protect dignity but leave stakeholders confused about accountability.

## Worked Example

The syndicate gave Kalanick until the end of the day to accept the demand, with a New York Times story ready if he stalled. He eventually signed, retaining a board seat while Gurley stepped off and Cohler replaced him. The planned graceful announcement failed when a source leaked the private confrontation, turning the exit into public evidence of the investor revolt.

## Key Takeaways

- Removing a founder is a governance action with financial, emotional, and narrative consequences.
- Coalition pressure can succeed even when the founder holds superior voting power, if the board and major shareholders align.
- The investors’ own role in building the system should be part of any accountability analysis.
