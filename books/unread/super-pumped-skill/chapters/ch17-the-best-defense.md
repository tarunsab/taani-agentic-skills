# Chapter 17: “The Best Defense…”

## Core Idea

Chapter 17 introduces Joe Sullivan, recruited from Facebook to build a world-class security function at Uber. Sullivan discovered Heaven, an internal system that tracked riders, and Hell, which monitored Lyft drivers through fake accounts, alongside scraping, receipt access, and categories for sexual misconduct; these capabilities made security inseparable from surveillance and competitive intelligence. A 2014 breach exposing roughly 50,000 driver names and license numbers had been kept secret, while attacks on drivers in Mexico and Brazil showed that physical safety and account identity were global operating problems. Sullivan wanted security to become a brand differentiator, but Kalanick’s condition was that Uber would not only play defense—the team should also support offensive competition. The distinction is between defensive security that protects users and an intelligence apparatus that turns access into leverage over rivals, employees, or regulators. The chapter’s practical lesson is that a security leader needs direct authority, transparent incident response, and boundaries that survive pressure from a growth-minded CEO.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Security as trust infrastructure:** Treat privacy, incident response, identity, and physical safety as part of the product.
- **Defense with offensive ambition:** Combine protection with competitive intelligence, creating a boundary problem.
- **Direct-accountability security:** Give the security leader access to the CEO and authority to stop unsafe practices.

## Key Concepts

- **Joe Sullivan:** Former federal prosecutor and Facebook security executive who led Uber’s security organization.
- **Heaven/Hell:** Names for rider tracking and Lyft monitoring systems that reveal the dual use of security access.
- **Incident disclosure:** The obligation to notify affected people and authorities after a data breach.

## Mental Models

- **Separate protect, investigate, and compete:** Different permissions and review paths reduce the risk that one tool becomes a surveillance weapon.
- **Safety is a marketplace feature:** A ride is not successful if the platform protects the transaction but not the people in it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Keeping breaches quiet to preserve the brand:** Silence transfers the cost to victims and increases legal exposure.
- **Giving a security team an unlimited mandate:** “Offense” without purpose, authorization, and audit trails becomes abuse.

## Worked Example

After a Guadalajara driver was murdered, Uber faced the reality that anonymous accounts, cash payments, and weak identity controls could endanger drivers as well as riders. Sullivan asked for a blank check to build a serious security organization, but he was expected to support Uber’s competitive battles too. That ambiguity foreshadowed the later conflict over surveillance, Greyball, and the hidden 2016 breach.

## Key Takeaways

- Trust and security cannot be bolted on after a marketplace reaches global scale.
- Security access is powerful enough to require explicit limits and independent review.
- A leader who reports directly to the CEO still needs the authority to challenge the CEO.
