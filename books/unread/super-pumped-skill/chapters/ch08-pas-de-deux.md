# Chapter 8: Pas de Deux

## Core Idea

Chapter 8 explains the venture-capital system that made Uber’s rise possible and the incentives that shaped the relationship between founders and investors. Venture firms raise money from limited partners, operate on long fund cycles, expect high returns, accept that many portfolio companies will fail, and rely on a small number of outsize winners; that “power law” logic makes moonshots rational even when the path is uncertain. The chapter describes the founder as a quasi-messianic figure who moves fast, breaks things, and is treated as the captain of a pirate ship, while investors compete with money, prestige, and access to secure a place in the next large company. Dual-class stock, including Google’s ten-votes-per-share structure and later examples involving Facebook and Snap, shows how founders can preserve control as outside capital enters. The distinction is between economic ownership and governing power: a shareholder may bear financial risk without having a practical say in how the company is run. This system can fund extraordinary ambition, but founder worship and weak information rights can turn the “dance” into a one-sided bargain.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Venture power law:** A fund can tolerate many failures because one or two investments may return the fund.
- **Founder worship:** The founder’s identity becomes a proxy for the company’s future, making intervention feel like destroying value.
- **Dual-class control:** Different share classes separate economic ownership from voting authority.

## Key Concepts

- **Limited partners (LPs):** Pension funds, endowments, family offices, and wealthy investors who supply venture funds.
- **Seed/Series rounds:** Staged fundraising that trades ownership and rights for capital as the company grows.
- **Moonshot:** A high-variance bet justified by the possibility of extraordinary scale.

## Mental Models

- **Ask who can say no:** Map voting shares, board rights, information rights, and contractual vetoes separately from valuation.
- **Portfolio logic is not company logic:** A VC can rationally accept failure in one investment; a company’s workers and customers cannot diversify that risk away.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating valuation as accountability:** A rising private price can coexist with weak controls, hidden liabilities, and negative externalities.
- **Assuming founder control guarantees mission fidelity:** Control may protect long-term vision, but it can also shield poor judgment.

## Worked Example

Google’s class B shares gave founders ten votes per share, allowing them to retain control after public financing; Facebook and Snap later used similar structures. Uber adapted the same logic for Kalanick, Camp, and Graves. The structure aligned with the founder-friendly ideal until investors needed to respond to scandals and discovered that economic ownership did not give them easy control.

## Key Takeaways

- Capital markets reward a credible story about scale, especially when a marketplace could dominate a category.
- Governance terms determine whether investors remain partners or become spectators.
- A founder can be both the source of a company’s edge and the concentration of its risk.
