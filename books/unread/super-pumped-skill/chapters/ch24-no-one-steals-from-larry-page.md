# Chapter 24: No One Steals from Larry Page

## Core Idea

Chapter 24 brings the autonomy race into the open through Waymo’s lawsuit and a driver video that exposed Kalanick’s leadership style. Waymo alleged that Anthony Levandowski had taken 14,000 files—about 9.7 gigabytes of lidar-related material—before leaving Google, and a supplier email suggested that Otto’s design matched Waymo’s; Larry Page treated the matter as a personal and corporate betrayal. At the same time, Jeff Jones’s research showed that riders liked Uber’s service while disliking Kalanick, and Bloomberg’s video of Kalanick arguing with driver Fawzi Kamel over a $97,000 vehicle-loss dispute made the abstract culture visible. Kalanick apologized and admitted he needed leadership help, but the video reinforced the impression that he saw people through the lens of performance and loss. The distinction is between an executive’s private intent and the public evidence of how power is exercised: a polished apology cannot erase a system that repeatedly makes the weaker party absorb the downside. The chapter also demonstrates why technical ambition and behavioral culture became one crisis rather than two.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Reputation–reality gap:** Strong service metrics can coexist with deep distrust of the person and culture behind them.
- **Trade-secret provenance:** Technical work must be traceable to authorized sources when teams move between rivals.
- **Leadership as observed behavior:** A single interaction can reveal how a company’s values operate under stress.

## Key Concepts

- **Waymo lawsuit:** Legal action alleging that Uber benefited from proprietary self-driving information.
- **Fawzi Kamel:** Uber driver filmed in a heated exchange with Kalanick about falling driver economics.
- **Jeff Jones survey:** Evidence that customers separated the product’s utility from their negative view of the CEO.

## Mental Models

- **Stress reveals the operating system:** Watch how leaders handle a person with less power when money or status is at stake.
- **Provenance is a control, not paperwork:** It protects both the employer and the employee when sensitive knowledge crosses company boundaries.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating a legal risk as a public-relations problem:** A better message cannot substitute for clean evidence and independent review.
- **Apologizing without changing authority:** Admission matters only if it changes incentives, oversight, and the experience of the affected group.

## Worked Example

In the driver video, Kalanick argued that Kamel had accepted lower fares and blamed him for losing money, while Kamel pointed to Uber’s changing economics. Kalanick later said he needed leadership help, but employees and the public saw a founder who could not separate a personal disagreement from the company’s power over a driver. The scene gave human scale to the broader data, safety, and culture controversies.

## Key Takeaways

- Brand damage can come from a leader’s behavior even when the product remains popular.
- Autonomous-vehicle competition raised intellectual-property controls to an existential issue.
- Public trust depends on patterns of conduct, not only on the sincerity of an apology.
