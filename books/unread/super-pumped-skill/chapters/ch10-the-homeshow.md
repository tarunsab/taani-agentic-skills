# Chapter 10: The Homeshow

## Core Idea

Chapter 10 presents fundraising as theater, sales process, and governance design. Emil Michael—an adaptive, polished dealmaker recruited by Gurley—became Kalanick’s business-development chief and close friend, blurring the line between executive partnership and personal loyalty. In the “Homeshow,” investors were invited to Uber’s offices for a tightly controlled week with only three meetings per day, creating scarcity and making access to Kalanick itself feel valuable. Kalanick’s deck emphasized a hockey-stick curve, “negative churn,” and the claim that a customer’s second or third ride predicted a lifelong user, while Michael and the team read investor reactions and used valuation spreadsheets to close the round. Outside capital, including sovereign and hedge-fund money, brought fear of missing out; in exchange, Kalanick secured super-voting shares and stripped away information and influence from investors. The chapter distinguishes persuasion from evidence: a compelling narrative can coordinate capital, but selective metrics and weakened oversight make it harder to tell whether growth is durable.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Scarcity fundraising:** Limit access and compress the schedule so investors compete for a place in the round.
- **Metric narrative:** Select a small set of leading indicators that turn uncertain future behavior into a confident growth story.
- **Capital-for-control exchange:** Accept money while negotiating voting, board, and information rights that preserve founder power.

## Key Concepts

- **Emil Michael:** Chief business officer and Kalanick ally whose dealmaking complemented the founder’s showmanship.
- **Homeshow:** Uber’s concentrated investor process, designed to create urgency and competitive allocation.
- **Negative churn:** The claim that existing customers become more valuable over time through repeated use and referrals.

## Mental Models

- **Separate signal from staging:** Ask which numbers are audited, which are proxies, and what counter-metrics are omitted.
- **Every financing round has a constitutional layer:** The economic price matters less if the rights make challenge impossible.

## Anti-patterns

- **Mistaking a crowded room for validation:** FOMO can make investors compete against one another rather than test the business.
- **Using control to avoid accountability:** Information restrictions may protect a founder from meddling while also hiding operational risk.

## Worked Example

Uber scheduled a handful of investor meetings over a single week, with Kalanick presenting the vision and Michael closing the room by reading body language and timing. GV wanted to lead a $250 million Series C at a $3.5 billion valuation, while TPG joined the round and sought a meeting with Larry Page. The process showed how scarcity, social proof, and founder charisma could turn a financing round into a contest for access.

## Key Takeaways

- Fundraising is a product that must be designed, sold, and governed.
- Retention and repeat-use metrics can be meaningful without proving profitability or social legitimacy.
- Capital concentration and voting design can leave a fast-growing company with less effective oversight than a smaller one.
