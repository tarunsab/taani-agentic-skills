# Chapter 28: The Syndicate

## Core Idea

Chapter 28 follows Bill Gurley as he moves from private concern to coordinated investor action. Kalanick ignored his promised leave, continued directing employees, and maintained enough super-voting stock and board allies to threaten a return, while Benchmark’s founder-friendly identity made a permanent ouster feel like a betrayal of its own model. Gurley built a syndicate with First Round, Lowercase, Menlo, and other shareholders, consulted governance experts and lawyers, and prepared a letter demanding that Kalanick leave. The group also identified a “nuclear option”: forcing Class B super-voting shares to convert to one-vote Class A shares, a move that would reduce Kalanick’s control but also weaken the investors’ own power. The chapter distinguishes coalition governance from ordinary board oversight: when rights are fragmented and a founder controls information, shareholders may need coordinated pressure to make formal governance work. The strategy was shaped by urgency because Gurley believed Kalanick would exploit any opening if the group failed to impose a deadline.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Shareholder syndicate:** Align investors’ economic and voting interests to confront a founder who can defeat them separately.
- **Founder-control countermeasure:** Use charter provisions or share conversion to restore one-share-one-vote governance.
- **Deadline pressure:** Limit the founder’s ability to delay, charm, or divide a coalition.

## Key Concepts

- **Syndicate:** Coalition of major Uber shareholders coordinating the request for Kalanick’s resignation.
- **Super-voting Class B stock:** Shares carrying ten votes each and enabling founder control.
- **Nuclear option:** Potential conversion of Class B shares into ordinary Class A shares.

## Mental Models

- **Power must be mapped as a coalition problem:** A minority of aligned holders may control more than a larger but fragmented group.
- **Rights can be weapons and restraints:** The same charter terms that protect a founder can later be used to force a power reset.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming private influence will eventually work:** A founder can use time to recruit allies and normalize continued interference.
- **Leaving governance disputes ambiguous:** Unclear authority invites parallel chains of command and strategic leaks.

## Worked Example

Benchmark, First Round, Lowercase, and Menlo collectively held more than a quarter of Uber’s economic stock and over 39 percent of its voting shares. They planned a private demand first, a public New York Times disclosure if Kalanick refused, and share conversion as a last resort. The coalition shows how investors converted diffuse anxiety into a credible sequence of escalating actions.

## Key Takeaways

- Founder-friendly investing requires a credible path for intervention when the founder becomes the central risk.
- Investor coordination can be necessary when formal board rights are weakened by share design and personal alliances.
- Escalation works only when participants agree on both the remedy and the cost they are willing to bear.
