# Chapter 15: Empire Building

## Core Idea

Chapter 15 follows Uber’s attempt to turn ride-hailing into a global empire and shows how scale magnified fraud, violence, and strategic overreach. China became a weekly contest of $40–50 million in incentives, fake trips, fraud investigations, and a rival DiDi supported by local networks and Tencent’s WeChat; Uber eventually lost the market and received a 17.7 percent stake in DiDi. India and Southeast Asia brought further competition, driver and passenger safety failures, and the 2014 Delhi rape that forced a shutdown and exposed the distance between rapid growth and trustworthy operations. Uber spent heavily on Pittsburgh, autonomous-vehicle research, and global offices while employees operated in a culture that framed every retreat as persecution. The distinction is between geographic expansion and institutional integration: opening an office is easy compared with understanding local payments, identity, regulation, violence, and partner power. Empire-building without local legitimacy creates a large footprint that can be strategically weaker than a smaller, better-governed network.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Global growth race:** Enter every attractive market quickly because ceding territory is treated as irreversible defeat.
- **Subsidy warfare:** Use incentives and investor capital to compete against local incumbents until one side capitulates.
- **Empire office:** Build visible hubs and specialized teams as symbols of reach, even while core controls lag.

## Key Concepts

- **DiDi:** Uber’s major Chinese rival, whose local relationships and platform advantages helped force Uber’s exit.
- **Fraud squad:** Teams built to detect fake rides, stolen cards, and coordinated incentive abuse.
- **Global safety failure:** The Delhi rape and later violence against drivers showed that local risk could not be solved by app growth alone.

## Mental Models

- **Local capability is part of the product:** Payments, identity, safety, labor, and enforcement are not optional regional add-ons.
- **War chests have opportunity costs:** Money spent to defend every market is unavailable for controls, product quality, or sustainable economics.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming a global template beats local incumbents:** A playbook that works in San Francisco can fail against government, payment, and cultural systems elsewhere.
- **Treating safety incidents as isolated anomalies:** Repeated harm indicates a system design problem, not merely an individual bad actor.

## Worked Example

In China, Uber’s incentives reached tens of millions of dollars per week and attracted coordinated fake-trip schemes, while DiDi benefited from local platform and payment advantages. In India, the rape of a passenger by a driver led to a Delhi shutdown and intensified questions about screening and accountability. Uber’s later sale of Southeast Asia to Grab for a 27.5 percent stake illustrates that the empire was ultimately a portfolio of markets, not one uniformly controlled network.

## Key Takeaways

- Scale magnifies both the value and the consequences of a platform’s operating assumptions.
- Local rivals can win through relationships and infrastructure that money alone cannot buy.
- Safety and fraud controls must travel with expansion rather than follow it years later.
