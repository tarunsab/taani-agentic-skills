# Chapter 30: Down but Not Out

## Core Idea

Chapter 30 shows that removing Kalanick as CEO did not remove his power, energy, or willingness to fight. He went to Tahiti to recover after losing his mother and company, then turned the leaked account of his ouster into a reason to prepare for war. Uber’s fourteen-person executive leadership team tried to run the company, but Kalanick contacted executives and directors, sought their votes, and requested information; they warned the board that they would resign en masse unless he was stopped. Joe Sullivan revoked Kalanick’s access to internal systems, and the board searched for a successor strong enough to prevent a comeback, first favoring Meg Whitman before leaks forced her to withdraw. Benchmark sued Kalanick over his board-seat rights, while Shervin Pishevar offered to buy the firm’s stake and Masayoshi Son’s SoftBank began circling for a discounted investment. The distinction is between formal removal and practical separation: a founder remains a governance risk when he retains board rights, allies, information access, and a personal network inside the company.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Founder afterlife:** A removed founder can continue shaping decisions through relationships, rights, and symbolic authority.
- **Access revocation:** Disable systems and permissions when a former leader’s informal influence threatens operations or privacy.
- **Strategic investor opportunism:** A crisis can create a discounted entry point for a new investor with enough capital to reshape governance.

## Key Concepts

- **Executive leadership team (ELT):** Fourteen senior leaders who temporarily ran Uber and warned against Kalanick’s interference.
- **Meg Whitman:** Candidate for CEO whose condition was complete separation from Kalanick; leaks ended her candidacy.
- **Masayoshi Son/SoftBank:** Investor seeking a large, discounted stake in a weakened but potentially valuable Uber.

## Mental Models

- **Resignation is not a control plan:** Define former-leader access, board rights, communication channels, and succession authority explicitly.
- **A crisis investor buys influence:** Evaluate the governance terms a new source of capital will demand, not only its price.

## Anti-patterns

- **Leaving the ousted founder as an informal operating layer:** Parallel instructions confuse employees and undermine the successor.
- **Allowing search leaks:** Candidate confidentiality is a prerequisite for recruiting a strong leader from a public company.

## Worked Example

After Kalanick’s departure, the ELT documented his requests for private emails, votes, and hidden conversations, and threatened to resign if the board did not intervene. Sullivan then removed Kalanick’s access to Google Drive, chat rooms, wikis, and employee forums. The controls reduced immediate interference but did not settle the board and ownership fight that SoftBank would later resolve.

## Key Takeaways

- Founder succession needs technical, legal, and social separation.
- A board that has not settled authority can make a new CEO impossible to recruit.
- Distressed capital often arrives with the leverage to change a company’s constitution.
