# Chapter 5: Upwardly Immobile

## Core Idea

Chapter 5 introduces Garrett Camp’s original UberCab idea as a response to a visible coordination failure in San Francisco: unreliable taxis, long waits, and poor access to transit. After selling StumbleUpon to eBay, Camp imagined ordering a black car from an iPhone, using GPS and a stored payment method to make the ride feel immediate and controlled. The early concept was deliberately narrow and premium—an exclusive fleet of high-end cars—not yet the mass-market, peer-to-peer network Uber would become. Kalanick entered as a “shepherd” for funding and strategy, and a conversation in Paris persuaded him that the idea could be much larger. The chapter distinguishes invention from scaling: Camp identified the interface and user pain, while Kalanick supplied the aggressive expansion logic that would turn a black-car service into a global marketplace.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Frictionless service design:** Remove uncertainty from discovery, dispatch, payment, and post-ride settlement.
- **Premium wedge:** Start with a controlled, high-value segment where reliability and experience can justify a new behavior.
- **Founder complementarity:** Pair a product insight with an operator who can recruit, finance, and expand the system.

## Key Concepts

- **UberCab:** The original working concept for requesting a private black car by phone.
- **GPS moment:** Camp’s realization that a phone could locate both rider and vehicle in real time.
- **Black-car wedge:** A premium initial market with cleaner cars, professional drivers, and fewer taxi-like constraints.

## Mental Models

- **Design the whole transaction:** A great front end depends on supply quality, payment, dispatch, and trust behind it.
- **Start where control is possible:** A constrained segment can reveal behavior before a company accepts mass-market complexity.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming the app is the product:** The user experience cannot be separated from driver supply, regulation, or physical execution.
- **Scaling before the wedge is understood:** A large market claim can distract from the conditions that made the initial service valuable.

## Worked Example

Camp’s taxi frustration became concrete when he imagined a car summoned from an iPhone, tracked by GPS, and paid for automatically. The early debate over owning cars versus coordinating existing drivers exposed the central strategic choice: Uber could be a fleet operator or a marketplace. Kalanick’s arrival pushed the idea toward the latter and toward a much more expansive ambition.

## Key Takeaways

- New marketplaces often begin with a simple, emotionally legible improvement to a broken routine.
- Premium positioning can reduce early uncertainty, but it may not predict mass-market economics.
- The eventual company is shaped as much by the scaling founder as by the original invention.
