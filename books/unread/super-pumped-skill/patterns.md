# Patterns

## Launch before permission

**When to use** — When a regulated market is slow to authorize a genuinely useful service.

**How** — Enter with a local operator, seed supply and demand, make usage visible, and negotiate after the network has political weight. Uber’s city-launch method used Craigslist, bonuses, free rides, and local autonomy to make the service feel inevitable (Prologue; Chs. 6, 12).

**Trade-offs** — It can reveal demand quickly and create leverage, but it externalizes legal and safety risk and can turn regulators into permanent enemies. Use only with explicit stop conditions, compliance ownership, and a plan for legitimate authorization.

## Buy the chicken

**When to use** — When a two-sided marketplace has too little supply and demand to start itself.

**How** — Subsidize both sides until availability, repeat use, and retention create a real flywheel. Measure the point at which incentives can decline rather than treating gross growth as proof of health (Ch. 9).

**Trade-offs** — Subsidies can overcome coordination failure, but they can also purchase fragile liquidity and invite fraud. The book’s China and global expansion episodes show how a cash race can consume capital without winning local legitimacy (Ch. 15).

## Frictionless consumer surface

**When to use** — When a fragmented service can be made easier through one discovery, dispatch, and payment flow.

**How** — Hide operational complexity behind a simple interface, card-on-file payment, live location, and predictable fulfillment (Chs. 5–6).

**Trade-offs** — Convenience drives adoption, but removing visible steps can also hide worker costs, safety choices, or meaningful consent. Treat every removed friction as a control review, not an automatic improvement (Ch. 14).

## Founder-protected financing

**When to use** — When a founder’s continuity is strategically valuable and outside capital could otherwise push short-term decisions.

**How** — Separate economic ownership from voting rights, then document information access, board independence, and intervention triggers (Chs. 8, 10).

**Trade-offs** — Stable control can support long-term bets, but super-voting shares and weak information rights can make a harmful founder hard to remove. Uber’s Grand Bargain shows that financing terms may later be needed to undo the protection (Ch. 31).

## Metric theater and scarcity fundraising

**When to use** — When a high-growth company must raise capital before its long-term economics are proven.

**How** — Present a small set of leading indicators, create a limited meeting window, and use investor competition to accelerate commitment (Ch. 10).

**Trade-offs** — Focus improves the story and can attract resources, but selective metrics and FOMO reduce scrutiny. Pair the narrative with counter-metrics, audited definitions, and explicit downside cases.

## Data-as-leverage

**When to use** — When fraud, abuse, safety, or competition requires signals from devices, accounts, or behavior.

**How** — Define the purpose, limit collection, log access, and separate protective use from competitive intelligence. Greyball, Heaven, Hell, and the Apple episode show what happens when the same capability is hidden from users or oversight (Chs. 16–17, 25).

**Trade-offs** — More data can reduce abuse and improve security, but covert targeting can violate privacy, platform rules, and institutional trust. Require independent review before deployment.

## Performance-over-conduct

**When to use** — Treat as a failure pattern to diagnose, not a default to adopt.

**How** — Notice whether high performers receive exceptions, complaints move the complainant rather than the offender, and HR labels lack consistent consequences (Chs. 1, 14, 22).

**Trade-offs** — Short-term output may rise, but misconduct, attrition, and legal exposure compound. Fowler’s account and the Holder report show why the pattern eventually becomes a board and leadership problem.

## Crisis-to-governance reset

**When to use** — When repeated incidents show that culture, oversight, or founder control—not one employee—caused the crisis.

**How** — Commission an independent investigation, change decision rights, appoint independent oversight, replace leaders where necessary, and disclose what can responsibly be disclosed (Chs. 23, 27, 31; Epilogue).

**Trade-offs** — Reform can reduce short-term valuation and founder autonomy, but it makes the company more legible and resilient. New slogans without authority changes are not a reset.
