# Patterns and Practices

## Social-universe map
**When to use**: You feel disconnected, overextended, or unsure which relationships need attention.  
**How**: List the people who shape your days, place each by contact frequency and whether the connection generally energizes or depletes you, then draw an arrow toward the relationship state you want.  
**Trade-offs**: The map simplifies complex bonds and should guide reflection, not dictate whom to cut off.

The point is to make hidden patterns visible: an old friend may be highly energizing but rarely seen, while a frequent contact may leave you tense. The authors recommend starting with relationships that already work, then nudging borderline or important difficult connections. A depleting label can indicate an opportunity for repair, a need for boundaries, or a need for less contact; it is not automatically a verdict.

## Support-keystones inventory
**When to use**: Your social world looks full but a particular kind of support is missing.  
**How**: For each important person, mark whether they provide safety, growth, confiding, identity/shared history, romantic intimacy, practical help, or fun/relaxation; repeat the exercise for support you give.  
**Trade-offs**: No one relationship needs to supply every kind of support; forcing one person to fill every gap creates pressure.

Use the gaps as diagnostic clues rather than as evidence that your whole life is failing. The exercise can reveal that a person you take for granted supplies security or identity, or that you rely on a single person for all practical help. The reciprocal pass matters because giving support can generate positive feeling and reveal actions within your control.

## Work from the top down
**When to use**: You want to improve relationships but feel overwhelmed by a long list of problems.  
**How**: Appreciate and strengthen energizing relationships first; nudge near-neutral ties; then decide which depleting ties merit outreach, a difficult conversation, a boundary, or less exposure. Put recurring plans on the calendar.  
**Trade-offs**: Positive ties still require novelty and maintenance; difficult ties may not become safe or reciprocal.

This ordering creates momentum and protects against a deficit-only view of social life. It also turns goodwill into behavior: a call, a visit, a recurring meal, or an explicit expression of appreciation. The book’s examples show that small acts accumulate into a social environment that supports health and future choices.

## Generosity and radical curiosity
**When to use**: A relationship feels passive, self-focused, or low in vitality.  
**How**: Offer the kind of support you would like to receive, ask genuine questions about the other person’s experience, listen, and communicate back what you understand.  
**Trade-offs**: Generosity is not self-erasure, and curiosity does not excuse harmful behavior or remove the need for boundaries.

The authors describe both practices as upward spirals: generosity creates good feeling that makes further helping more likely, while curiosity invites disclosure, understanding, and connection. Curiosity can begin with one question and a willingness to follow the answer rather than steer the conversation back to the self. Communicating an accurate or good-faith understanding is often more important than being perfectly right.

## Attention audit and tech holiday
**When to use**: Life feels rushed, distracted, or dominated by screens and postponed plans.  
**How**: Identify where time and mental presence actually go, create a recurring distraction-free period with people who matter, check your mood after social-media use, ask loved ones how your phone habits affect them, and run a short technology-free control period.  
**Trade-offs**: Online connection can sustain belonging and provide access for isolated people; the test is its effect and use, not a universal ban.

The book distinguishes time as priority from attention as present-moment awareness. A tech holiday is an experiment, not a moral test: compare how scattered, connected, or attentive you feel with and without the platform. Prefer active contact over passive scrolling and remember that online interaction may not build every in-person social skill.

## W.I.S.E.R. response loop
**When to use**: An emotion is arriving fast, a conflict repeats, or you are caught between avoidance and escalation.  
**How**: **Watch** context and body; **Interpret** stakes and assumptions; **Select** goals, options, resources, and timing; **Engage** with practice and care; **Reflect** on results and revise.  
**Trade-offs**: The model takes effort and sometimes must be revisited; cultural norms, time pressure, and the other person’s response limit control.

W.I.S.E.R. is designed for the difficult minority of moments when automatic behavior does not serve you. The Clara–Frances example shows that a carefully chosen conversation can still go partly wrong, yet reflection yields information for the next attempt. The aim is not perfect control or instant resolution; it is a more flexible response aligned with the outcome you seek.

## Reflective listening and self-distancing
**When to use**: A partner or friend is hard to understand, or a conversation is emotionally loaded.  
**How**: Listen without correcting, paraphrase without judgment, ask whether you understood, offer a grounded statement of why the person’s reaction makes sense, and view your own experience as if advising someone else.  
**Trade-offs**: Empathic effort improves connection even when accuracy is imperfect, but listening is not agreement and should not replace direct problem-solving.

The authors’ couples study found that empathic effort was especially important for relationship satisfaction, and their intimacy guidance treats emotions as clues to deeper needs. Self-distancing creates enough space to notice thoughts as temporary rather than as commands. Together, these practices make difficult conversations more curious, less defensive, and more repairable.

## Catch kindness and learn new dance steps
**When to use**: Intimacy has become routine, conflict-avoidant, or dominated by disappointment.  
**How**: Notice and name small acts of kindness, add welcome novelty to routines, pause before reacting, explore what a recurring disagreement means beneath its surface, and practice mutual vulnerability.  
**Trade-offs**: Novelty must fit the relationship, and vulnerability needs reciprocity and safety; “keeping the peace” can hide disconnection.

The dance metaphor treats missteps as information about how two people coordinate, not as proof that the relationship is impossible. Affection and empathy create a bedrock from which differences can be discussed. The authors caution against expecting a partner to supply every friend, interest, source of meaning, and form of support in one’s life.

## Corrective-experience and family-ritual loop
**When to use**: Old family roles or childhood expectations make every interaction feel predetermined.  
**How**: Notice positive surprises, catch relatives behaving well, stay open to changed roles, create recurring meals/calls/visits, and ask older or younger relatives about shared memories.  
**Trade-offs**: Openness does not require re-entering unsafe situations; rituals can be adapted when contact is limited or harmful.

The family chapter treats childhood influence as real but malleable. A caring adult, a secure partnership, therapy, or another positive relationship can contradict an old model of what people are like. Regular contact and stories preserve family connection, while the “You Always / You Never” trap warns against freezing people in past versions of themselves.

## Coming-home-upset reset
**When to use**: Work stress is leaking into family or intimate time.  
**How**: Name the work emotion before acting, allow a short nonjudgmental decompression, shift attention to the present environment, tell the household you had a hard day, and ask the other person about theirs.  
**Trade-offs**: A reset does not solve structural overload, harassment, inequity, or an unsafe job; those may require organizational or economic changes.

The authors’ daily-spillover research found rough workdays linked to more anger for women and more withdrawal for men in the evening. Ignoring or hiding emotion can increase arousal, while acknowledging it can interrupt the negative cycle. Work relationships, not only workload, may be the source, so the same curiosity and W.I.S.E.R. tools can be applied at work.

## Friendship maintenance and weak-tie cultivation
**When to use**: Friends have drifted, your social world is narrow, or a life transition removes an old source of contact.  
**How**: Listen, repair rifts with a small olive branch, schedule recurring contact, cultivate casual interactions with strangers and acquaintances, and join activities that fit your current life stage.  
**Trade-offs**: Friendship needs differ; one person may thrive with two close friends and another with a broad activity network.

Strong ties buffer adversity, while casual ties provide daily uplift, bridges to new communities, information, and opportunities. The authors reject the idea that men are naturally incapable of intimacy and note that gender differences in friendship expectations are smaller than stereotypes suggest. Friendship is voluntary, so it is easy to neglect; the remedy is purposeful care calibrated to the kind of connection that actually nourishes you.
