# Chapter 5 — Attention to Relationships: Your Best Investment

## Core Idea

Attention is the most basic way we invest in people. The authors distinguish time as a priority in the schedule from attention as actual mental presence, and argue that both are finite expressions of a life. Modern communication can maintain belonging and provide access, but passive scrolling, constant switching, and divided attention can make connection thinner. The practical challenge is not to become perfectly mindful; it is to notice where attention goes and deliberately return it to the people and experiences that matter.

## Source Sections Retained

### NO TIME TODAY BUT PLENTY OF TIME TOMORROW

People often postpone a call, visit, or conversation on the assumption that time will be available later. The chapter pairs this ordinary procrastination with late-life reflections from Harvard participants, who tended to regret worry and neglected family time more than time spent caring about what mattered. Since the future is unknown, “later” is not a neutral storage place for relationships.

### THINKING ABOUT WHAT IS NOT HAPPENING

Attention can be directed toward an absent person or future task, but persistent mental absence carries a cost. Mind wandering is part of normal cognition and can support planning; the problem is when it repeatedly removes a person from the interaction in front of them. The authors ask readers to notice not just activity but the thoughts and alternatives competing for the mind.

### THE OWL AND THE HUMMINGBIRD

The owl symbolizes sustained, focused attention; the hummingbird represents rapid movement among stimuli. The latter can feel efficient while actually producing continuous partial attention and more switching. Focus makes room for thoughtful conversation, creativity, and the perception of small cues that divided attention misses.

### ONE FAMILY’S LIFE OF ATTENTION

Leo DeMarco was busy as a teacher, father, and friend, yet his time with his children and partner was marked by presence. Rachel later recognized that attention to her father was not secured by good intentions alone and deliberately carved out time. The family example shows how attention accumulates through ordinary moments rather than requiring constant availability.

### THE MODERN MEANS OF CONNECTION

Phones and online platforms change the conditions under which people connect. They can preserve contact across distance, support people with rare conditions, and help marginalized communities find belonging. They can also create the illusion of connection while displacing face-to-face time; the authors treat the medium as a tool whose effects depend on how it is used and what it replaces.

### OUR ATTENTION, ONLINE

The chapter asks readers to observe whether an online session leaves them connected, informed, and energized or scattered, envious, and depleted. A large smartphone-sampling study cited by the authors found that people spent about half their waking moments thinking about something other than the activity at hand, and mind wandering was associated with lower happiness. The study does not establish that every digital activity is harmful; it highlights the relationship between attention, activity, and felt well-being.

### THE GIVE-AND-TAKE OF SOCIAL MEDIA

Active participation—messaging, responding, sharing a meaningful exchange—differs from passive consumption. The authors recommend checking the mood produced by a platform and asking loved ones how one’s phone behavior affects them. This turns technology use from a moral argument into an observable feedback loop.

### ISOLATION AND CONNECTION

During the pandemic, video calls and online groups prevented some people from becoming completely cut off, yet many also experienced “skin hunger” and increased depression or anxiety. Physical presence supplies sensory and embodied information that screens cannot fully reproduce. The lesson is not that online connection is worthless, but that no machine is a complete substitute for being together when in-person contact is possible and safe.

### DON’T SCROLL, ENGAGE

When reaching for a platform, choose an active form of contact when possible: send a message, ask a question, or make a plan. Passive scrolling can be a useful rest in moderation, but it should not quietly consume the time intended for people. The authors also propose a short technology holiday as a control period for learning what changes when a device or platform is temporarily absent.

### BEING (AND STAYING) ALERT

Mindfulness is presented as a practical form of alertness: asking what is here that has not yet been noticed. Returning to breath, body, voice, or surroundings creates a pause in automatic distraction. It can make a person more available to another’s changing expression without requiring a mystical or permanently calm state.

### A LITTLE MORE ATTENTION, EVERY DAY

Small increments matter because relationships are made of repeated encounters. A person can protect one meal, commute, walk, call, or weekly visit from avoidable distraction and then extend the practice. The target is a little more reliable presence, not an impossible promise to attend perfectly all day.

### A FOR EFFORT

The chapter distinguishes empathic accuracy from empathic effort. Understanding another person exactly is difficult, but attempting in good faith to understand can itself communicate care and improve the relationship. Effort is an action within reach even when the observer’s interpretation is incomplete.

### LEO GETS A B+ FOR EFFORT

Leo’s relationships show that attention is a practice with imperfect grades rather than a fixed virtue. He cannot be present everywhere, but he repeatedly returns to the people and activities he values. The example invites a realistic standard: notice lapses, repair them, and keep investing.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Time versus attention**: calendar priority and mental presence are related but distinct.
- **Owl versus hummingbird**: sustained attention versus rapid switching and continuous partial attention.
- **Active versus passive online contact**: evaluate technology by its use and effects.
- **Technology holiday**: run a temporary control period to observe what a platform displaces.
- **Empathic effort**: good-faith trying matters even when perfect accuracy is impossible.

## Key Concepts

**Attention** is both a scarce resource and a relational signal. **Mind wandering** is normal but can become costly when it repeatedly steals the present. **Continuous partial attention** is a state of monitoring many streams without fully engaging one. **Empathic effort** is the deliberate attempt to understand another’s experience.

## Mental Models

### Attention as a budget

Treat attention like a daily budget: every notification, task switch, and passive feed spends it. Allocate a small protected amount to people who matter before the budget is consumed by defaults.

### The replacement test

For any online habit, ask “What does this replace?” If it replaces isolation with meaningful contact, it may serve the good life; if it replaces a conversation or sleep with depletion, the trade is different. The test avoids universal rules while making opportunity cost visible.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming good intentions will preserve a relationship without scheduled contact.
- Calling multitasking efficiency when it is repeated attention switching.
- Treating a screen as either the cure for loneliness or the cause of all disconnection.
- Using mindfulness as a demand for perfect calm rather than a return to what is present.
- Believing inaccurate empathy excuses failure to make a good-faith effort.

## Worked Example

Choose one recurring interaction that matters and protect it from avoidable device use for a week. Before and after each interaction, record where your mind was, how connected you felt, and whether the other person responded differently. Run a short technology holiday or replace passive scrolling with one active message, then compare the results without treating them as a universal law. Ask the person beside you what they experienced; their report is part of the data.

## Key Takeaways

1. Time and attention are the raw materials of relationships.
2. Mind wandering is human, but chronic distraction makes connection harder.
3. Active online engagement and passive consumption have different effects.
4. Physical presence offers information technology cannot fully replace.
5. Empathic effort is a practical form of love even when accuracy is imperfect.
