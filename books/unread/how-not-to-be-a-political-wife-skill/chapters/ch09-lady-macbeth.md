# Chapter 9: Lady Macbeth

## Core Idea

After the Leave victory, Vine experiences the result as both national upheaval and a family security emergency: cameras surround the house, the pound falls, the children are watched, and Michael becomes one of Britain’s most wanted political figures. She is furious when David Cameron resigns almost immediately, believing a responsible prime minister should have stayed to stabilize the country and negotiate the result. That judgment exposes her own slide into political certainty—the ninth lesson warns against thinking a spouse knows better than the politicians how to run the country. As the leadership contest begins, Sarah gives Michael advice, an email is leaked, and the media turns her into “Lady Macbeth,” a gendered figure blamed for a male politician’s ambition. The chapter asks where support ends, where political agency begins, and how quickly a spouse can become both adviser and scapegoat.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Lesson Nine — think you know better than the politicians how to run things.** Vine’s ninth lesson turns her anger at Cameron’s resignation back on herself. She may have seen private consequences more clearly than distant commentators, but that did not make her the legitimate replacement for a prime minister or party strategist. The line between useful counsel and imagined authority is easy to cross when a spouse has access to rooms and confidences. Humility is therefore part of political boundary-setting.

**Victory can be a loss of control.** Leave wins 52–48, but the victory immediately produces market turmoil, a prime-ministerial resignation, and a leadership vacuum. Michael’s team has influence but not command, and the future depends on decisions made by people who were not all aligned during the campaign. A public result therefore does not guarantee that the people who secured it control its consequences. Families should expect the first hours after a win to be a period of heightened uncertainty rather than celebration.

**The overconfident spouse.** Vine’s ninth lesson is self-critique disguised as a joke. Anger at Cameron’s resignation and confidence that she understands the grown-up option reveal how proximity to political arguments can produce an exaggerated sense of competence. The spouse may see private motives and operational detail, but she still lacks the full institutional mandate and information of office-holders. Good counsel can be offered without confusing closeness with authority.

**Gendered political agency.** When Sarah advises Michael and a private email is published, commentators describe her as a manipulative woman behind the man. The label makes her responsible for his decisions while denying him agency and ignoring the political context in which he is acting. Vine sees a double standard: men’s strategic advice is treated as politics, while a wife’s advice is cast as malign influence. The episode also shows why spouses should assume that written counsel may become public and decide in advance what role they are willing to play.

**Leadership as addiction and refusal.** Michael supports Boris partly because he does not want Boris to have an uncontested path, then decides to run himself. Sarah sees the ambition as dangerous for the children and does not want to surrender her journalism. Michael’s first leadership bid ends with Theresa May’s victory and his removal from government, but the episode reveals how political life keeps presenting office as a solution to the damage produced by office. The system’s promise of “one more contest” can prevent a family from stopping.

## Key Concepts

- **Immediate fallout:** Cameras, press interest, market decline, Cameron’s resignation, and the children’s presence turn the vote into a security and family event.
- **Broom-cupboard politics:** Dave, Boris, and Michael’s post-result positioning shows how private conversations can determine public succession.
- **“Anyone but Boris”:** George Osborne’s opposition and Michael’s eventual candidacy demonstrate that personal history and strategic calculation overlap.
- **The leaked email:** Sarah’s message urging Michael to extract specifics and hold his ground is sent with a colleague copied, then becomes evidence against her.
- **The Lady Macbeth frame:** The press simplifies a complex leadership choice into a story about a wife’s influence, reflecting both sexism and the appetite for a recognizable archetype.
- **May’s victory:** Sarah feels relief as well as disappointment because a failed leadership bid may spare the family an even more intense public role.

## Mental Models

**The result-to-vacuum sequence.** A binary result can remove an existing leader faster than a replacement coalition can form. In that interval, advisers, spouses, journalists, and factions may act as if they are governing while no one has a settled mandate. The safest assumption is that public messaging and private security will be unstable. Families should reduce exposure, verify information, and avoid treating an interim conversation as a final outcome.

**The proxy-blame trap.** A spouse is visible enough to attract blame but lacks the formal authority that would let her correct the record. Her advice then becomes a convenient story for explaining a politician’s decision, especially when the spouse is a woman and the story can be framed as manipulation. The trap is avoided by preserving the politician’s responsibility and refusing to let media archetypes replace facts. It also requires the spouse to decide whether public defense will clarify or create more material for the story.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating a narrow victory as a settled mandate with a clear governing future.
- Assuming proximity to political decisions makes a spouse the best available political authority.
- Sending strategic advice without considering that it may be published or selectively framed.
- Letting a gendered narrative make the spouse responsible for a partner’s adult political choices.

## Worked Example

Sarah’s leadership advice is the chapter’s concentrated case. After Boris offers roles around the succession and the political agreement shifts, she writes Michael an email urging him to establish the facts and be stubborn; Henry, a public-relations colleague, is accidentally copied. The message reaches the press and is presented as a poison-pen intervention by “Lady Macbeth,” while Michael remains the decision-maker who chooses to run. Theresa May wins, Michael is removed from government, and Sarah feels both the cost of the campaign and the relief of avoiding a still larger leadership burden. The example supports a narrow boundary: give private counsel only with clear consent, protect written communication, and keep responsibility for public choices with the politician.

## Key Takeaways

- A political victory can create a leadership vacuum and immediate family danger.
- Political proximity is not the same as institutional authority or complete information.
- Spouses may be blamed as manipulators precisely because their influence is informal.
- Public advice should be bounded, deliberate, and never a substitute for the politician’s own responsibility.
