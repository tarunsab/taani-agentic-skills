# Chapter 1: The Eye of the Storm — 23–24 June 2016

## Core Idea

The opening chapter places the reader inside the Brexit referendum result as a domestic emergency and a national turning point. At 4:45 a.m. on 24 June 2016, Vine wakes from a dream of a house fire to find cameras and reporters outside the family home, while Michael sleeps after correctly treating election night as a long, familiar professional exercise. The house becomes a literal stage: Vote Leave colleagues celebrate, Remain friends grieve, relatives in Italy and Spain watch the markets, and children experience the public gaze at their front door. Vine’s own European background and mixed feelings make the night emotionally unstable; she understood the attachment to Europe while sharing Michael’s frustration with Brussels. The chapter’s central image is therefore not triumph but a household in which a national decision makes every private relationship feel politically legible.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Momentous and mundane.** Vine says historic moments are made of ordinary details: bad clothes, wine, tired children, a small dinner, a telephone call, and a television prediction. The contrast prevents the result from becoming a clean myth of political destiny. A person can be deciding the future of a country while worrying about a leopard-print outfit or a friendship that will not survive the morning. The model is useful whenever public significance tempts people to forget the domestic conditions under which decisions are made.

**The journalist-wife double bind.** Vine was a journalist while married to a politician, and the editorial positions around her were not uniform: Paul Dacre’s Daily Mail strongly supported Leave, while the Mail on Sunday was Remain. Michael was campaigning while also serving as Justice Secretary, which made her work and his public choices appear to overlap even when she had not authorized that interpretation. She was expected both to understand politics and to remain a loyal private spouse. The result was a boundary problem in which any public voice could be read as an intervention in the marriage.

**Friendship as a political fault line.** The referendum divides a dinner group that had previously tolerated disagreement through jokes and conversation. Remainers and Leavers had once shared holidays, godparent roles, and family life; after the vote, children and adults stopped speaking. The damage is not presented as a simple difference of opinion but as a shift in how people interpret one another’s motives. Political affiliation becomes a test of moral identity, which makes repair much harder.

## Key Concepts

- **Public scrutiny:** The cameras outside Barlby Road turn a family address into a broadcast location, making the household part of the story before anyone has spoken.
- **Mixed motives:** Vine’s European upbringing, bilingual education, family in Italy and Spain, and sympathy with Michael’s sovereignty argument prevent a simple Remain/Leave self-portrait.
- **Political timing:** The narrow Newcastle result, Sunderland’s Leave vote, market panic, and the night’s rapid forecasts show how quickly uncertainty can become collective shock.
- **Domestic spillover:** The vote changes friendships, family arguments, online abuse, and the children’s relationship to their father’s public role.

## Mental Models

**The house-as-stage model.** Once political attention reaches the front door, the family no longer controls the meaning of ordinary domestic space. A kitchen, attic, dinner table, or school run can be interpreted as a political image. The pressure is not only the amount of coverage but the loss of a private place in which people can disagree or recover. A boundary response begins by deciding which parts of the house and family remain off-stage.

**The mixed-loyalties map.** Vine’s relationships cross several axes at once: Europe versus sovereignty, journalism versus marriage, Leave colleagues versus Remain friends, and national victory versus personal grief. These axes do not line up neatly, so the same person can feel relief, fear, pride, shame, and sorrow in one evening. Mapping the competing loyalties helps explain why simple accusations of betrayal are so destructive. It also makes room for a spouse to have a view that is not identical to the politician’s.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming a public victory creates a private duty for every friend or spouse to celebrate.
- Treating a spouse’s newspaper affiliation as proof of her personal vote.
- Allowing cameras and online commentary to define the family’s emotional reality.
- Mistaking a narrow electoral result for a settled social consensus.

## Worked Example

The dinner at Barlby Road contains the chapter’s whole argument. Michael opens a large bottle of claret while a mixed group of friends jokes about the result; some believe Remain will win, and others expect a respectable Leave vote. A warning that the mood may turn nasty reveals how quickly the shared room could become a hostile political space. By morning, the same people are separated by the result, markets are in turmoil, and the family’s front door is on television. The event shows why a couple should discuss in advance how they will handle public disagreement, family invitations, and the possibility that a political campaign will alter friendships permanently.

## Key Takeaways

- Historic politics arrives through ordinary domestic details and relationships.
- A spouse may hold a mixed or independent view even when the politician is publicly aligned with a campaign.
- Public attention can turn the home and children into political material without consent.
- Protecting friendship requires separating disagreement from moral condemnation where possible.
