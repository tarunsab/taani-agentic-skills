# Chapter 7: Boiling the Frog

## Core Idea

The chapter’s frog metaphor describes a political life that becomes dangerous through gradual normalization rather than one visible rupture. A&E staff, a police officer, journalists, friends, and children increasingly treat Michael not as an ordinary person but as a political object who can be mocked, used, or hunted. Vine applies the same process to policy: Michael’s education reforms—harder qualifications, a revised grading system, academies, free schools, Ofsted, and attention to teacher quality—turn him into a figure that opponents and comedians can hate without engaging his motives. The hostility then reaches Beatrice and William at school, where their father’s public reputation becomes a social weapon. The chapter argues that a public role changes the moral weather around a family long before anyone admits that the family is in danger.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Lesson Seven — entering the political coven turns a person into an object or prey.** Vine’s seventh lesson is the chapter’s sharpest description of dehumanization. It includes the comic recognition of Michael as a minister in A&E and the darker realization that the public feels entitled to mock, use, and threaten him. The same permission can reach a spouse or child through school, press, and online attention. The boundary is to keep treating people as people even when their policies are being contested.

**Person to object to prey.** Vine’s seventh lesson marks a loss of ordinary personhood. Michael’s kidney-stone episode is comic because medical staff recognize the minister before they treat the patient; elsewhere the same recognition enables ridicule, booing, threats, and the assumption that he is fair game. Once the public sees a politician as an object, the spouse and children can become adjacent objects or leverage. The boundary is to insist on human needs even when the office is controversial.

**Gradual escalation.** The frog is not boiled by one decision. The household accepts a little more media attention, a little more policy hostility, a little more school commentary, and a little more need to defend the politician. Each step can be explained as the price of the role, until the accumulated temperature becomes intolerable. Regular check-ins are necessary because endurance can disguise escalation.

**Reform versus coalition management.** Vine supports more rigorous state education and greater school autonomy, drawing on her own experience of teachers and standards. She also thinks Michael and Cummings often choose confrontation where negotiation, flattery, or a softer hand might have protected the reform. Her account is admiring and critical at once: she sees a substantive ambition, a combative method, and a political culture that rewards spectacle. The framework separates the value of a policy aim from the wisdom of the way it is pursued.

## Key Concepts

- **Education reform:** Michael pushes harder exams and qualifications, changes to grading, academies and free schools, Ofsted, and a focus on teacher quality; opponents frame him as destructive or elitist.
- **The “Blob”:** Vine uses the term for institutional and ideological resistance in education, but it is her political framing, not a neutral description of everyone who disagreed.
- **Cummings’s style:** Dominic is depicted as a “mad professor” whose intelligence and aggression can break through complacency but also produce bullying and settlement costs.
- **Family spillover:** Beatrice is targeted at a birthday party and school; William uses covert skills and hears the political insult attached to his surname.
- **Media dehumanization:** Question Time booing and jokes about Michael’s appearance or character create a public permission structure for contempt.

## Mental Models

**The temperature log.** Track not just major incidents but frequency, intensity, recovery time, and who is absorbing the cost. A single joke may be tolerable; repeated public hostility plus school harassment, illness, and security exposure is a changed environment. The log helps a family decide when to reduce visibility or seek protection before a crisis. It also distinguishes an unusual event from a pattern that has become normal.

**Policy-person fusion.** When a reformer becomes the symbol of a policy, criticism of the reform is redirected into contempt for the person. The person’s class, appearance, friends, or children then become easier targets than the evidence. Vine believes this happened to Michael, though her diagnosis reflects her loyalty and political stance. The protective response is to keep policy arguments specific while refusing to treat personal cruelty as legitimate debate.

## Anti-patterns

- Accepting dehumanizing treatment because the person holds office.
- Allowing a child’s school to become an unmonitored extension of political conflict.
- Confusing a combative method with proof that every opponent is corrupt or incompetent.
- Waiting for a spectacular threat before acknowledging a gradual pattern of exposure.

## Worked Example

Beatrice’s school experience shows the frog’s temperature rising. She is hated because of her father, and a birthday-party incident involving bullying, a mother’s threat, and a knife misunderstanding brings adults and police into the family’s orbit. William, meanwhile, hears “Gove” used as a reason to push him to the back of a line and turns to covert, tactical behavior learned from the family’s circumstances. These are not parliamentary events, but they are consequences of a parliamentary identity. The practical lesson is to listen to children, coordinate with schools, and treat repeated symbolic spillover as a safety and wellbeing problem.

## Key Takeaways

- Political danger often arrives through small, repeated changes that a household learns to tolerate.
- A policy disagreement should not license treating a politician, spouse, or child as prey.
- Separate the merit of a reform from the style and consequences of its advocacy.
- Use regular temperature checks to decide whether exposure is still acceptable.
