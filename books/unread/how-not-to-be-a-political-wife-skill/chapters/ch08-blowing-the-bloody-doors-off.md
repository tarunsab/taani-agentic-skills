# Chapter 8: Blowing the Bloody Doors Off

## Core Idea

Vine’s Brexit chapter is an attempt to describe the referendum from inside a marriage and a political friendship network that had not yet admitted how far apart its members were. She acknowledges that her account may be self-serving, that memory is disordered by exhaustion and stress, and that she held back while married to protect Michael. David Cameron’s desire to settle the referendum, George Osborne’s political calculations, Michael’s sovereignty convictions, Boris Johnson’s ambition, and Dominic Cummings’s campaign strategy converge around a choice that the friends treat as both constitutional and personal. Sarah initially supports changing Europe from within and later reassures Cameron that Michael is ultimately onside, even though Michael is privately moving toward Leave. Once he becomes a public face of Vote Leave, friendship language turns into demands for control, and the campaign’s “day after” is less prepared than the victory rhetoric suggests.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Lesson Eight — there is no such thing as a friend in politics.** Vine’s eighth lesson is intentionally broader than the claim that every political relationship is fake. It means that friendship is exposed to strategic pressure whenever office, succession, access, or public loyalty is at stake. The referendum reveals how quickly affection can be reinterpreted as a resource or a betrayal. The practical response is to distinguish private trust from political alignment and to make that distinction explicit.

**The soft-sofa danger.** Vine uses comfortable sofas and relaxed domestic conversations as a motif for talks in which political daggers are concealed by intimacy. A holiday or supper can appear to be an ordinary family occasion while participants are positioning for a referendum, a leadership contest, or a future government. Comfort lowers defenses and makes the later sense of betrayal sharper. The boundary is to distinguish hospitality from a political negotiation and to state the stakes plainly.

**Loyalty versus truth-telling.** Sarah wants to preserve the Cameron friendship and reassures Dave that Michael will be onside; Michael avoids an early confrontation and compartmentalizes his thinking. Each attempt to protect relationships postpones the honest conversation that could have made the disagreement manageable. By the time the campaign is public, everyone interprets delay as deceit. Political loyalty is sustainable only when people can state their actual position before strategy turns it into a surprise.

**Friendship as political capital.** The chapter’s eighth lesson says there is no such thing as a friend in politics, but the memoir also shows genuine affection and shared history. The useful distinction is not that every friendship is fake; it is that political power gives each friendship a strategic value that can override intimacy. Cameron’s reaction to Michael’s defiance and the Remain group’s reaction to Sarah’s column demonstrate this conversion. A spouse should ask which part of a relationship survives when the political utility disappears.

**Campaign victory without governing design.** Vote Leave develops a persuasive story about money, experts, sovereignty, and ordinary people, with Michael, Boris, Cummings, and Gisela Stuart as prominent figures. Vine says a Life After Victory plan existed in outline but that the personnel and operational aftermath were not worked through, partly because people assumed Cameron would remain. The absence becomes consequential when Cameron resigns and the leadership contest begins. The lesson is to treat the post-result plan as part of the campaign rather than an optional later exercise.

## Key Concepts

- **Referendum timing:** Cameron’s promise follows the Scottish vote and is presented as a way to manage internal pressure, leadership timing, and the party’s future.
- **Different European arguments:** Vine associates Michael’s position with sovereignty, Brussels, fisheries, Scotland, his adopted father, and a desire to save or serve the country; her own instincts are more European and reformist.
- **Compartmentalization:** Michael can debate Europe intensely while withholding the depth of his intentions from Sarah and Cameron, leaving home and work in separate mental rooms.
- **Boris, George, Dave, and Dom:** The chapter presents a collision of personal ambition, strategic calculation, campaign skill, and long-running friendship rather than one simple conspiracy.
- **Jo Cox:** Her murder pauses the campaigns and makes the human danger of political division impossible to ignore.
- **The family’s duty of care:** Sarah’s health, the children’s age, and the need to protect them complicate any claim that the campaign is purely a public duty.

## Mental Models

**The hidden-stakes conversation.** Before a friendly meeting, ask whether participants are also deciding status, succession, policy, or public alignment. If so, name the subject and give each person a clear opportunity to disagree. This reduces the later claim that someone used a domestic setting to spring a political betrayal. It also prevents the spouse from becoming an unacknowledged intermediary between political men.

**The result-to-reality gap.** A campaign result changes constitutional facts immediately, but it does not supply a government, a timetable, or a stable coalition. The wider the gap between the victory message and the operational plan, the more likely that institutions and families will improvise under pressure. The model applies to referendums, elections, and leadership contests. “We won” is the start of the implementation problem, not its solution.

## Anti-patterns

- Reassuring a friend about a spouse’s position before speaking honestly with the spouse.
- Treating a domestic meal or holiday as politically neutral when the participants are negotiating power.
- Assuming a campaign’s persuasive story contains a complete governing plan.
- Allowing children and a spouse to absorb the consequences of a public decision they were not included in shaping.

## Worked Example

At Chequers and in later dinners, Sarah hears competing arguments about the referendum while believing the friendships can survive an eventual difference. She reassures Cameron that Michael is ultimately onside; Michael then chooses Leave, becomes a central campaign figure, and is told by Cummings that the public role is necessary. Sarah’s Mail column attempts to explain the choice and preserve the relationship, but Cameron reads it as an attack, and Samantha sees the family’s position as betrayal. After the vote, the expected continuity disappears, Cameron resigns, and the campaign’s incomplete aftermath becomes a leadership crisis. The example shows why honest alignment, explicit boundaries, and a staffed day-after plan matter before a political disagreement becomes a public rupture.

## Key Takeaways

- Comfortable friendship settings can conceal decisions about power and succession.
- A spouse cannot sustainably mediate a political disagreement that the principals refuse to state directly.
- Genuine affection may coexist with strategic self-interest; test what survives outside office.
- A campaign must plan governance, personnel, and contingencies before the result arrives.
