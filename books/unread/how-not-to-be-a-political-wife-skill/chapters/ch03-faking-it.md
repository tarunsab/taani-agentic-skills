# Chapter 3: Faking It

## Core Idea

Vine presents impostorhood as a feeling that predates Westminster: she was told as a child that she was an unwanted product of a summer romance, grew up under her father’s criticism, and learned to read confidence in other people as proof that she did not belong. The family history she recounts includes money anxiety, emotional abuse, wartime trauma, alcoholism, post-natal depression, and stories whose details cannot always be verified. These experiences help explain why she became a people-pleaser who could function impressively while privately expecting to be exposed. The selection for Surrey Heath intensifies the feeling because she is pregnant, working, and observing a political world that assumes the candidate’s wife will understand its codes. William’s serious illness then exposes the cost of being physically present in one system while the politician is absorbed by another.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Lesson Two — remain in the incredulous out-of-body experience.** Vine’s second lesson names the recurring feeling of looking around and wondering how she arrived in a room whose rules other people seem to know. It is not a statement that she lacks ability; it is the emotional afterlife of early rejection and conditional approval. Political selection makes the feeling concrete because the household is now visible to people with inherited confidence and institutional status. The work is to recognize the feeling without allowing it to decide who is entitled to participate.

**Impostorhood as a learned posture.** Vine does not reduce the feeling to a lack of ability; her career shows that she repeatedly acquires difficult skills and succeeds under pressure. The feeling comes from early messages that love, approval, and status are conditional on performance. In political settings, inherited confidence can look like competence because people with wealth and status are used to speaking without apology. The practical response is to separate actual capability from the old expectation of humiliation.

**Intergenerational patterns without false certainty.** Vine links her father’s behavior to his own father’s harshness and to her mother’s family history of war, loss, alcoholism, and mental illness. She uses these stories to show how unprocessed pain can travel through parenting and marriage. She also signals that family legends are partly reconstructed and that a memoir cannot prove every causal link. The framework is therefore explanatory rather than diagnostic: notice inherited patterns, but do not turn a family story into a complete medical or moral verdict.

**The candidate’s wife as an unchosen public employee.** Once Michael wins the selection, Sarah is expected to be useful to a constituency and a political association even though she is not the candidate. She is barred from the final selection room because her pregnancy might confer an unfair advantage, yet still becomes part of the household’s cost base and public identity. Her later recollection that this was initially a “him” project shows the gap between formal status and practical responsibility. A role can be unofficial and still consume labor.

## Key Concepts

- **Conditional approval:** Roger’s comments about appearance, weight, violin, tennis, and achievement teach Sarah to anticipate criticism and to manage other people’s moods.
- **Class confidence:** The contrast between Sarah’s self-doubt and the upper-class ease she observes in the Cameron circle becomes a recurring political and social theme.
- **Selection culture:** Surrey Heath is an aspirational, lower-to-middle-class Conservative constituency, distinct from the gentleman-farmer image Vine associates with Witney; local political culture still contains old-fashioned grandees.
- **Pregnancy and decision-making:** Vine later treats her pregnancy-era agreement to major changes as a warning about accepting promises while physiologically and emotionally overwhelmed.
- **William’s mastoiditis:** A dismissed ear concern becomes a rare, dangerous infection requiring transfer and surgery; Sarah’s guilt is intense, but the child ultimately recovers through expert care.

## Mental Models

**The confidence gap.** People who inherit status often speak as if they are entitled to occupy a room, while outsiders can mistake that ease for superior judgment. Sarah’s professional competence does not immediately change her internal expectation that she is trespassing. The gap can be narrowed by naming the evidence of capability and by learning the rules without accepting the room’s hierarchy as a measure of human worth. It is especially important in political life, where confidence is frequently performed as authority.

**The split-screen household.** During William’s illness, Sarah is in hospital pacing beside a sick baby while Michael commutes to London, works for Michael Howard, and prepares the Conservatives for the 2005 election. Neither frame fully cancels the other: his work is politically important, and her emergency is personally absolute. The split-screen model shows how a political career can make unequal availability look inevitable. A family needs explicit arrangements for emergencies rather than relying on the assumption that the public mission always wins.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating self-doubt as evidence that a person lacks the right to participate.
- Expecting a pregnant or caregiving spouse to become an unpaid political operative by default.
- Turning inherited trauma into a fixed explanation for every later choice.
- Allowing a professional deadline to make a child’s emergency invisible or secondary.

## Worked Example

The final Surrey Heath selection captures the role’s ambiguity. Sarah is pregnant with William, caring for one-year-old Beatrice, and back at The Times, so she has little involvement in the early hustings. She is excluded from the final room and waits in a bar with mineral water, chatting with Jim Davidson, while Michael wins more than half the vote. The family then rents out Barlby Road, moves to Camberley, absorbs extra childcare and travel costs, and tries to keep Michael’s political ambition financially viable. A few months later, William’s mastoiditis takes Sarah from domestic support into a hospital crisis, while Michael remains tied to the election machine; the lesson is to design family emergency capacity before the role makes unequal availability seem unavoidable.

## Key Takeaways

- Impostor feelings may be learned through family and class experience, not a reliable assessment of ability.
- Unofficial political labor is still labor and should be named, negotiated, and resourced.
- Major family changes need a plan for money, childcare, emergencies, and recovery.
- When a child is ill, expert care and direct advocacy matter more than the parent’s self-blame.
