# Patterns

## 1. Distance-traveled assessment
**When to use**: A person’s current performance looks ordinary or weak.
**How**: Compare outcome with starting resources, relevant peers, obstacles, trajectory, and evidence of learning.
**Trade-offs**: Context improves fairness but is harder to measure than a score.

Use this pattern when a snapshot hides the route. Grant’s examples show that opportunity and prior exposure can look like innate talent, while a steady or upward slope may signal more potential than a high starting point. Do not convert it into a single adversity number; individual reactions and barriers differ. Use it as a richer forecast alongside present, role-relevant evidence.

## 2. Task-matched discomfort loop
**When to use**: A learner is relying on a comfortable method or waiting to feel ready.
**How**: Match method to task, use the skill early, seek a tolerable edge of awkwardness, and set a mistake budget.
**Trade-offs**: Discomfort accelerates learning only when it is relevant and safely designed.

The method that feels best may not teach the target skill: reading supports analysis, listening supports sound and emotion, and doing supports communication and memory. First-day use creates feedback that study alone cannot provide. Framing awkwardness as progress helps people persist, while systematic exposure or flooding can be selected for the fear and setting. The aim is not pain for its own sake but more useful practice.

## 3. Advice-filter loop
**When to use**: Input is vague, contradictory, or ego-threatening.
**How**: Ask what to do better next time, then filter sources by care, credibility, and familiarity; test the advice.
**Trade-offs**: Filtering protects against noise but can become ego-protective avoidance.

Advice shifts a conversation from judging the last attempt to coaching the next one. A credible source still does not know every route, so treat each suggestion as a hypothesis. Mellody Hobson’s response to Bill Bradley shows that hard truth can be absorbed when the relationship signals care and expertise. The sponge’s job is both to retain useful nutrients and to reject toxins.

## 4. Imperfectionist quality loop
**When to use**: A project is stuck in polishing or the standard is unclear.
**How**: Set a precise difficult target, identify acceptable flaws, ask independent judges for 0–10 scores, revise recurring problems, and make yourself the final judge.
**Trade-offs**: External scores reveal quality gaps but can create unhealthy dependence on social approval.

“Do your best” is too vague to focus effort or show when enough is enough. A temporary judging committee supplies convergence and a signal about whether the work is becoming lovable, while an aspirational and acceptable threshold prevent endless revision. The creator still decides whether the work represents the core purpose and values. Wabi sabi means selective compromise, not careless lowering of standards.

## 5. Four-feature scaffolding
**When to use**: A challenge is overwhelming, mistimed, or mismatched to current capacity.
**How**: Identify the source, obstacle, pivotal time, and removal plan for the support.
**Trade-offs**: The right support is powerful; generic or permanent support can waste effort or create dependence.

Grant’s Tetris example makes the mechanism concrete: visual-spatial play after a distressing scene interfered with imagery consolidation, while unrelated trivia did not. The support came from knowledgeable people, targeted a particular obstacle, arrived within a critical window, and lasted only minutes. Apply the same diagnostic logic to coaching, study routines, or workplace support. Do not generalize a narrow intervention into a cure for unrelated problems.

## 6. Deliberate-play practice
**When to use**: Repetition is producing burnout, boreout, or narrow skill.
**How**: Turn subskills into games with novelty, constraints, immediate measures, self-competition, and recovery.
**Trade-offs**: Play improves motivation and flexible learning, but it does not eliminate the need for serious practice or feedback.

Payne’s basketball drills preserve fatigue and technical demands while adding a score and a time to beat. Interleaving similar challenges makes the learner retrieve and adapt rather than run one memorized routine. Unlike cosmetic gamification, deliberate play redesigns the task itself. Scheduled breaks protect harmonious passion, incubate ideas, and improve recall.

## 7. Roundabout navigation
**When to use**: A plateau feels like a permanent ceiling.
**How**: Back up, find a compass, gather pins from multiple guides, test a new method, and track small wins.
**Trade-offs**: A new route initially lowers performance and a detour may not be causal.

Learning a better method often requires unlearning a familiar one, so decline before improvement is normal. Experts may lack beginner-friendly directions because of the curse of knowledge and tacit skill. Multiple guides provide partial landmarks that must be adapted rather than copied. A side project can restore the sense of progress needed to return to the main route.

## 8. Interdependent bootstraps
**When to use**: Confidence or competence is insufficient for the obstacle.
**How**: Pool resources, divide teaching, coach one another, and connect effort to a shared purpose.
**Trade-offs**: Interdependence takes coordination and does not excuse avoiding individual responsibility.

The Golden Thirteen learned faster because each person became both learner and teacher. Teaching strengthened competence; coaching strengthened confidence and accountability. A shared identity turned private effort into responsibility for the group and future successors. The pattern is stronger than rugged individualism because it multiplies resources already in the room.

## 9. Opportunity-culture redesign
**When to use**: A school or organization systematically loses late bloomers.
**How**: Inspect assumptions, change values, then build practices for professional support, continuity, agency, voice, and second chances.
**Trade-offs**: Structural change is slower and context-sensitive; imported recipes can fail when motivation or local conditions differ.

Finland’s example shows how the assumption that every child is teachable supports teacher autonomy, looping, early intervention, play, and interests. The same logic scales to organizations: make opportunity routine rather than dependent on one exceptional mentor. Measure progress and well-being, not only rank. Finland’s later PISA decline is a reminder that opportunity works only when learners remain motivated to use it.

## 10. Collective-intelligence system
**When to use**: A complex problem needs diverse expertise and low-status voices.
**How**: Create cohesion around a meaningful mission, choose prosocial listeners, use brainwriting, run parallel options, and provide lattice paths to sponsorship.
**Trade-offs**: More ideas increase variance and require judgment; multiple plans consume resources.

The Chile rescue succeeded by using both insiders and outsiders, not by locating one genius. Brainwriting protects independent generation from ego threat, noise, and conformity before collective selection. A lattice keeps one gatekeeper from killing an idea and lets a young contributor reach someone willing to listen. Psychological safety is most useful when paired with a process that evaluates ideas seriously.

## 11. Potential-revealing selection
**When to use**: Credentials, experience, or a stressful interview dominate a forecast of future performance.
**How**: Contextualize difficulty, inspect trajectory, give a relevant live work sample, invite strengths and passion, and ask whether a do-over is needed.
**Trade-offs**: Richer evaluation costs time and judgment; no single metric eliminates uncertainty.

Live demonstrations show what a candidate can do now, while trajectory and context explain how far the candidate has already traveled. Call Yachol’s welcoming interview and Harvey’s reset show why a first anxious attempt can be unrepresentative. Past performance remains useful when the skills transfer to the new role, but credentials and years of experience are weak shortcuts. Algorithms may aggregate evidence well, but the book’s limit is clear: they are inputs to human judgment, not substitutes for it.
