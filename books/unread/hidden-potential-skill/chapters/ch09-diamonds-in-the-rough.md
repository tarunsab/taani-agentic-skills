# Chapter 9: Diamonds in the Rough

## Core Idea

Admissions and hiring decisions are forecasts about future performance, yet most selection systems are optimized to detect already-polished excellence. That bias confuses credentials, years of experience, or a single past performance with potential and hides people whose achievements required unusually difficult routes. Grant proposes looking at degree of difficulty, performance trajectory, learning capacity, character skills, and real-time work samples. José Hernandez’s path from migrant farmworker to astronaut shows how a candidate can repeatedly improve while remaining invisible to a form designed around conventional accomplishment. The chapter also warns that difficulty cannot be reduced to a universal adversity score: people experience the same event differently, and group-based remedies can create doubts about competence if implemented carelessly. Better interviews set candidates up to show what they can do, make room for passion and support, and allow a do-over when the first snapshot is distorted by anxiety or circumstance.

## Source Sections

- **The Sorting Hat Is Out of Sorts** — Credentials and experience are weak proxies: a Yale student was only 1.9% better than a Cleveland State student on collaborative consulting work, and a 44-study meta-analysis found prior experience had virtually no bearing on performance.
- **Uncut Gems** — NASA’s screening system saw work history, education, and honors but not José’s migrant labor, language, or adversity; false positives were prioritized over false negatives.
- **Quantifying the Unquantifiable** — Difficulty matters, but the failed SAT adversity score and the double-edged effects of affirmative action show why context must be handled carefully and individually.
- **Rise over Run** — Grade-point trajectory predicts college completion and later income better than a collapsed average alone; José’s GPA rose from 2.41 to 2.90, 3.33, and 3.56 across his early college semesters.
- **Interview without a Vampire** — Call Yachol uses welcoming hosts, passion questions, work samples, support, and do-overs to reveal the strengths of people with disabilities and other disadvantages.
- **A Window of Opportunity** — José kept adapting, reached a NASA finalist interview, earned a 91/99 score, later joined the astronaut office, and flew in space at 47.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Degree of difficulty**: Use when performance appears weak or ordinary. Ask what access, constraints, and obstacles shaped the result, then compare the person with relevant peers.
- **GPT, grade-point trajectory**: Use alongside GPA to measure rise over time. An upward slope is a clue that the candidate learned, adapted, or overcame adversity.
- **Transfer-valid performance**: Use past performance only when the old task requires skills similar to the new role; otherwise it can reproduce the Peter Principle.
- **Real-time work sample**: Give every candidate a common, role-relevant problem and observe what they can do rather than relying only on claims or past artifacts.
- **Candidate as judge and do-over**: Ask whether the interview represented the person well; if not, change the conditions and allow a second attempt.

## Key Concepts

- **Potential-aware selection**: Forecasting future growth rather than only rewarding past polish.
- **Degree of difficulty**: The challenge and opportunity context surrounding a performance.
- **Trajectory**: The direction and rate of improvement over time.
- **GPT**: Grant’s proposed grade-point trajectory measure.
- **Peter Principle**: Promotion based on prior success until a person reaches a role beyond their abilities.
- **Work sample**: A direct demonstration of a candidate’s skill.
- **False positive**: Accepting someone who will not perform as expected.
- **False negative**: Rejecting someone who could have performed well.
- **Stereotype threat**: Performance disruption caused by fear of confirming a negative group stereotype.

## Mental Models

- Evaluate **rise over run**, not just the current point.
- Ask **what did this person have to overcome?** before translating outcome into ability.
- Use **present demonstration plus past trajectory** as complementary evidence.

## Anti-patterns

- **Credential fetish**: Treating pedigree as proof of future contribution.
- **Experience counting**: Assuming twenty years on a résumé means twenty years of learning.
- **One-shot interrogation**: Making anxiety and stereotype threat part of the test.
- **Trajectory blindness**: Collapsing improvement into a single average.
- **Single-metric correction**: Replacing one crude shortcut with an adversity score or quota that creates new ambiguity.

## Limitations & Boundaries

Difficulty is real but subjective, and the failed SAT adversity score shows why it resists a universal formula. Trajectory is a valuable clue, not a sufficient selection metric; maintaining performance through a major setback can itself be achievement. Work samples and algorithms add evidence but take time, inherit blind spots, and cannot remove uncertainty from a forecast.

## Worked Example

Call Yachol, Gil Winch’s disability-inclusive call center, begins interviews with a questionnaire about passions and treats candidates as guests rather than interrogating them. Candidates then complete familiar, role-relevant work samples, can ask for help, rate whether the interview showed them at their best, and receive a do-over if it did not. Harvey, who is autistic, arrived distracted after a difficult morning; a break let him reset, and he aced the second attempt. He went on to excel in a rejection-heavy cold-calling role for eight years, showing why a supportive live sample can reveal resilience that an anxious first interview hides.

## Key Takeaways

1. Contextualize performance by access, obstacles, and relevant comparison groups.
2. Preserve trajectories instead of collapsing them into averages.
3. Use live, role-relevant work samples to observe learning and contribution.
4. Let candidates show their strengths and recover from a noisy first attempt.
