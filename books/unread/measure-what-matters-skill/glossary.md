# Glossary

**Active transparency** — Openly sharing information, goals, and truth so people can participate, challenge, and coordinate rather than merely observe. (Ch 18, Ch 20)

**Alignment** — Connecting individual and team work to organizational strategy and to peer dependencies so effort moves in the same direction. (Ch 7–9)

**Aspirational OKR** — A high-risk, future-tilting objective whose route or required resources are uncertain; Google expects about 0.7 on average, with high variance. (Ch 12, Resource 1)

**BHAG** — Big Hairy Audacious Goal: a clear, daunting, compelling focal point that galvanizes effort and team spirit. (Ch 12)

**Bottom-up OKR** — An objective or KR proposed by a contributor or team, often in consultation with a manager; Doerr recommends roughly half of OKRs arise this way when context permits. (Ch 2, Ch 7)

**CFRs** — Continuous performance-management practices: **Conversations**, **Feedback**, and **Recognition**, used with OKRs to add human context and reinforcement. (Ch 15–16)

**Committed OKR** — A goal the team agrees to deliver at 1.0, adjusting priorities and resources as needed; a miss calls for explanation and a postmortem. (Ch 12, Resource 1)

**Counter-effect KR** — A paired quality, safety, or other balancing measure that prevents a quantity target from being achieved by damaging an important outcome. (Ch 4)

**Key result (KR)** — A measurable, time-bound, verifiable milestone describing how an objective will be advanced; it should state an outcome and evidence, not merely an activity. (Ch 1, Resource 1)

**Low Value Objective (LVO)** — An objective that can be completed without anyone noticing or receiving direct user or economic benefit; rewrite it around the tangible value. (Resource 1)

**Management by objectives (MBO)** — Drucker’s results-driven, consultative approach to “management by objectives and self-control,” which Grove adapted and changed into OKRs. (Ch 2)

**Objective** — The significant, concrete, action-oriented, ideally inspirational **what** to be achieved; it is directional rather than a list of milestones. (Ch 1, Resource 1)

**OKR shepherd** — A designated person who maintains cadence, nudges completion, coordinates scoring/reviews, and keeps the system from becoming administrative wallpaper. (Ch 10)

**OKR score** — A completion signal, often the average of KR completion rates; Google’s color bands are red 0.0–0.3, yellow 0.4–0.6, and green 0.7–1.0. (Ch 10, Resource 1)

**One-on-one** — A recurring, contributor-owned meeting for mutual teaching, progress, coaching, problem solving, and deeper work issues; the manager learns and coaches. (Ch 15, Resource 3)

**Psychological safety** — The cultural condition in which people can take risks without feeling insecure or embarrassed, one of Google’s five Project Aristotle team factors. (Ch 18)

**Recognition** — Specific, frequent appreciation of actions, results, values, or progress; peer-to-peer recognition crowdsources meritocracy and reinforces goals. (Ch 15)

**Reflection** — Deliberate synthesis of what happened, what blocked or enabled it, and what to change in the next cycle; scoring alone is not learning. (Ch 10)

**Sandbagging** — Setting or managing goals defensively so attainment is easy or failure cannot be blamed, often because goals are tied to compensation. (Ch 2, Ch 15, Resource 1)

**Stretch goal** — An ambitious target beyond the team’s immediate grasp that can elicit innovation and higher performance when it is meaningful, supported, and not obviously impossible. (Ch 12–14)

**10x / Gospel of 10x** — Google’s preference, for breakthrough work, for exponential improvement that forces a rethinking of the category instead of a modest incremental gain. (Ch 12–14)

**Transparency** — Making objectives, progress, dependencies, and learning visible enough for people to align, help, and challenge the work. (Ch 7, Ch 9, Ch 18–20)

**Activity trap** — Mistaking busyness or volume of activity for output and results; Grove argues that emphasizing output raises productivity while activity chasing can reduce it. (Ch 2)

**Watch time** — YouTube’s chosen user-engagement metric in its billion-hour stretch effort, later supplemented by satisfaction and responsible-content signals. (Ch 14)
