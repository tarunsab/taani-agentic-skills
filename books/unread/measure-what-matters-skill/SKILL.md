## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### Objectives and Key Results (OKRs)
Use an OKR when a team needs a shared answer to what matters and how progress will be proved. The objective is the significant, concrete, action-oriented, ideally inspirational **what**; key results are the measurable, time-bound, verifiable **how**. A well-designed objective normally has three to five KRs, and completion of all KRs should be sufficient to achieve the objective. Write outcomes and evidence, not internal activity labels, and make the value visible to a rational observer. OKRs are a management protocol, not a substitute for judgment, leadership, or a healthy culture.

### Four OKR “superpowers”
Apply the superpowers as a reinforcing system: **Focus and Commit to Priorities** narrows scarce attention; **Align and Connect for Teamwork** makes goals public and dependencies visible; **Track for Accountability** turns goals into living data that can be revised; **Stretch for Amazing** makes room for ambitious, creative bets and intelligent failure. The sequence is not a rigid implementation order: a stretch goal still needs focus, alignment, and tracking. The stories of Intel, Google, the Gates Foundation, and others show the system working across companies, nonprofits, and different scales. The same stories also show that culture and leadership determine whether the mechanics help or harm.

### Focus and commitment
Keep only the few objectives with leverage beyond ordinary work—usually three to five per cycle—and make the tradeoff explicit by dropping, deferring, or deemphasizing other work. Use the mission or strategic plan to find top-line goals, then communicate both the what and the why repeatedly. Leaders must publish and model their own commitments; saying “all my goals are team goals” signals weak adoption. A quarterly cadence keeps feedback close to the work, while annual OKRs can hold longer strategy in view. Pair quantity with quality or counter-effect KRs so a target cannot be “won” by sacrificing safety, ethics, customer value, or reputation.

### Alignment, autonomy, and transparency
Make OKRs visible so people can see how individual work connects vertically to strategy and horizontally to peers. Cascading can create coherence, but rote top-down cascades become slow, inflexible, demotivating, and one-dimensional. Let contributors negotiate hows and create roughly half of their own goals when context permits; preserve direction while granting ownership over execution. Give every cross-functional dependency an explicit owner and supporting KR. Use transparency to expose duplication, invite help, and let good ideas emerge from the edges rather than turning visibility into surveillance.

### Tracking, grading, and learning
Treat OKRs as guardrails, not chains. Check in regularly, use a shepherd to keep the process alive, and choose among **Continue**, **Update**, **Start**, and **Stop** as conditions change. A simple Google-style score averages KR completion: 0.0–0.3 red, 0.4–0.6 yellow, and 0.7–1.0 green; scores are signals, not verdicts. Add a subjective self-assessment for context, then reflect on obstacles, design quality, and what to change next cycle. Dropping an obsolete goal requires notifying dependent stakeholders and learning from the decision.

### Committed, aspirational, and 10x goals
Separate **committed OKRs**, which teams agree to deliver at 1.0 by adjusting resources and priorities, from **aspirational OKRs**, which describe a valuable future state whose route and resources are uncertain. Google expects roughly 0.7 on average for aspirational work, while failure is a planning/execution signal for a committed goal. Stretch only where the organization can provide purpose, support, and psychological safety; an obviously impossible target damages morale. For category-changing innovation, ask what a 10x result would require rather than accepting a 10-percent improvement as the default. Reframe a moonshot in a meaningful context, and carry a relevant aspirational goal forward instead of hiding a persistent constraint.

### CFRs and continuous performance management
Pair OKRs with **CFRs**: **Conversations** that are regular, authentic, and contributor-centered; **Feedback** that is specific, two-way, peer-aware, and timely; and **Recognition** that is frequent, visible, behavior- and outcome-based, and tied to values or goals. These interactions supply the human context that a score cannot. Keep forward-looking goal conversations largely separate from backward-looking compensation decisions, or people will sandbag and avoid risk. Use one-on-ones for goal setting/reflection, progress updates, coaching, career growth, and lightweight performance review. Managers are teachers and coaches; the contributor owns the agenda and both sides can give feedback.

### Culture as the medium
OKRs and CFRs do not operate in a vacuum: culture carries their signals. Build trust, psychological safety, clarity, dependability, meaningful work, and visible impact; communicate values by example as well as words. Balance OKR “catalysts” (clear goals, autonomy, resources, learning) with CFR “nourishers” (respect, recognition, encouragement, and affiliation). If accountability and trust are missing, change the environment before deploying the tool, as Lumeris learned. Use real-time pulse feedback and mission-linked goals to keep values observable rather than merely aspirational.
