# Chapter 8: Align: The MyFitnessPal Story

## Core Idea

MyFitnessPal shows why alignment becomes a first-order problem when a focused start-up grows faster than its informal coordination can handle. In the early days, Mike and Albert Lee could select one keystone goal—such as launching an iPad version—and communicate daily, but tens of millions of users and multiple product leaders created entropy. Product managers had goals for Premium, an API platform, and the core login experience, while a shared engineering team had its own infrastructure priorities; engineers were repeatedly interrupted and no one knew which objective had precedence. A marketing OKR for personalized email failed because engineering had never agreed to support it, and the dependency was discovered only in the postmortem. MyFitnessPal responded with executive integration meetings, clearer capacity constraints, distinct ownership, and key results tied to launch dates when future revenue or usage was too hard to predict. After the Under Armour acquisition, transparent OKRs exposed even more hidden assumptions: ecommerce, data, and media sales each expected something from Connected Fitness that the group could not all deliver. Sharing constraints and priorities took eighteen months but helped the division cut roadmaps, coordinate apps, and make Premium the number-one objective. Alignment ultimately meant more than internal consistency: every feature and partnership had to stay true to the customer-centered North Star that the company succeeds when users succeed.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Explicit dependency integration**
  - **When to use**: When multiple teams rely on a shared function or deliverable.
  - **How**: Bring department heads together, list dependencies, test capacity, and put each material commitment into a team’s KRs.
- **Single-owner accountability**
  - **When to use**: When several teams contribute to one objective.
  - **How**: Keep one owner for the OKR and give parallel teams distinct KRs rather than shared blame.
- **North Star alignment**
  - **When to use**: When a feature, partnership, or revenue goal competes with customer value.
  - **How**: Ask whether the decision helps customers achieve the mission; give extra scrutiny to anything that does not.

## Key Concepts

- **Entropy**: The drift into different directions that occurs as more capable people pursue separate priorities.
- **Capacity constraint**: A real limit on engineering time, people, or other resources that must shape commitments.
- **Cross-team integration meeting**: A review where leaders identify buy-in, overload, and dependencies before execution.
- **Single owner**: The person accountable for an OKR even when other teams contribute.
- **North Star**: The enduring mission or customer value used to judge tradeoffs.

## Mental Models

- **Misaligned nails**: More pressure does not fix two priorities pulling in different directions; alignment must be corrected first.
- **Binary stars**: Focus and alignment reinforce each other; alignment without a small priority set still scatters effort.
- **Skate to where the puck is going**: Use shared goals to coordinate around the next opportunity, especially after a strategic merger.

## Anti-patterns

- **People-shaped company OKRs**: Creating an objective for every leader instead of matching people and teams to a few meaningful company goals produces a cornucopia with no center.
- **Unacknowledged dependency**: A well-written OKR still fails if the team that must support it has a conflicting priority.
- **Co-ownership without role clarity**: When two owners can blame each other, accountability weakens; use one owner and distinct KRs.
- **Roadmap absolutism**: Keeping every feature because it is good prevents the few features that matter most from shipping.

## Worked Example

MyFitnessPal eventually used an OKR such as **Help more people around the world**, with KRs to add 27 million new users in 2014 and reach 80 million total registered users. The numbers were meaningful only because the company paired them with a decision rule: when a business goal conflicted with customer success, align with the customer. After a merger, two apps independently built maps with different providers, producing duplicated effort and an inconsistent experience; a monthly check-in and later division-wide OKRs made the dependency visible. These examples show alignment as an operating practice, not merely a link in a goal hierarchy.

## Key Takeaways

1. Diagnose shared-team constraints before promising dependent outcomes.
2. Make dependencies explicit and review them before the deadline, not at the postmortem.
3. Give each OKR one accountable owner while allowing multiple contributors.
4. Use the customer or mission as the tie-breaker when alignment becomes a tradeoff.
