# Chapter 10: Superpower #3: Track for Accountability

## Core Idea

Tracking turns an OKR from a static plan into a living system that can reveal progress, obstacles, and changing relevance. Doerr describes three phases: set up a usable, visible home for goals; monitor progress through regular check-ins; and close the cycle with scoring, self-assessment, and reflection. A dedicated cloud platform is helpful at scale because it can expose goals, update status, show analytics, alert users, and connect work that would otherwise be buried in documents. An OKR shepherd keeps the cadence alive and nudges late adopters, while weekly or context-appropriate check-ins prevent a goal from becoming a zombie. At any point, a team can **Continue**, **Update**, **Start**, or **Stop** a goal; flexibility is a guardrail against pursuing an obsolete target, not permission for silent revision. Google’s simple score averages KR completion: 0.0–0.3 red, 0.4–0.6 yellow, and 0.7–1.0 green, but the number is only a signal. A subjective self-assessment supplies context that raw completion cannot, and reflection turns experience into a better next cycle. Accountability is therefore no-judgment responsibility for reality: rescue a failing commitment, learn from a failed experiment, celebrate progress, and tell everyone who depends on a changed or dropped objective.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The four tracking moves**
  - **When to use**: Whenever evidence or circumstances change during a cycle.
  - **How**: Continue an on-track goal; Update scope, timing, or resources; Start a needed goal; Stop an obsolete one and notify dependents.
- **Objective score plus self-assessment**
  - **When to use**: At cycle close.
  - **How**: Average KR completion, then add a contextual judgment about difficulty, effort, luck, and outcome quality.
- **OKR wrap-up**
  - **When to use**: Before planning the next cycle.
  - **How**: Score, assess, reflect on obstacles/design, decide whether to roll over or drop the objective, and acknowledge progress.

## Key Concepts

- **OKR shepherd**: The person responsible for cadence, completion, scoring, and reviews.
- **Zombie OKR**: A goal still written down but no longer connected to daily work or current reality.
- **Check-in**: A recurring progress conversation that surfaces evidence, obstacles, and adjustments.
- **Green / yellow / red**: Google’s completion bands for on track, partial/needs attention, and little progress.
- **No-judgment accountability**: Honest responsibility for results without turning every score into punishment.
- **Reflection**: Deliberate synthesis of experience into lessons for future goals.

## Mental Models

- **Guardrails, not chains**: Goals constrain attention enough to guide action but leave room to steer around new information.
- **Ladder against the wall**: Tracking tests whether effort is moving toward the right destination, not merely climbing faster.
- **Progress as motivation**: Seeing measurable headway can engage people more deeply than a distant bonus or final result.

## Anti-patterns

- **Goal graveyard**: Storing thousands of files without a daily home or live status makes public goals effectively invisible.
- **Set-and-forget**: Waiting until quarter-end allows the gap between plan and reality to widen.
- **Silent deletion**: Dropping a goal without telling dependent teams breaks coordination and hides the lesson.
- **Score absolutism**: Treating a completion percentage as a complete evaluation ignores context, difficulty, and quality.

## Worked Example

The Q2 1980 Crush objective was scored from four KRs: five performance benchmarks scored 0.6, repackaging the product family scored 1.0, the 8MHz production target scored 0, and the arithmetic-coprocessor shipment scored 0.9. The average was 0.625, a respectable result for an aggressively set objective even though it fell below expectation. A zero on production led to an explanation—the polysilicon problem pushed the target to October—rather than a fictional green. In a different case, Remind stopped treating a failed peer-to-peer payment goal as sacred and updated it toward an event-driven system that solved a clearer teacher problem.

## Key Takeaways

1. Give goals a visible, usable home and keep their status current.
2. Treat yellow and red as prompts for action, not embarrassment.
3. Combine objective scores with contextual self-assessment and reflection.
4. Stop or revise obsolete goals openly, then preserve the learning and affected relationships.
