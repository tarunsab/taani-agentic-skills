# Chapter 4: Superpower #1: Focus and Commit to Priorities

## Core Idea

Focus begins with a hard question: what is most important for the next three, six, or twelve months? Doerr argues that high-performance organizations select a small number of initiatives with disproportionate leverage and make the tradeoff visible, because no person or company can do everything. Senior leaders own the top-line choices, derive them from the mission or strategy, and model commitment through their own goals; a leader who claims to have only team goals undermines adoption. Objectives supply inspiration and direction, while KRs supply the measurable levers, deadlines, and evidence that turn priorities into action. A quarterly cadence keeps feedback near the work, while annual OKRs can hold longer strategy in view; the right cadence depends on the context. Communication must repeat the why, not merely publish the what, because many employees cannot name their company’s priorities. Quantitative KRs need quality or counter-effect partners so a team cannot win on speed, cost, or volume by sacrificing safety, ethics, customer value, or reputation. The chapter’s governing rule is “less is more”: three to five objectives and no more than five KRs per objective create the courage and discipline to say no.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Top-line focus**
  - **When to use**: At the start of a cycle or strategic reset.
  - **How**: Start from mission/strategy, select the few initiatives with leverage, assign owners, and say what is deferred.
- **Dual cadence**
  - **When to use**: When annual strategy and near-term execution both matter.
  - **How**: Run annual OKRs alongside quarterly or monthly KRs; let short-cycle work keep the annual plan honest.
- **Paired key results**
  - **When to use**: When an output target could create harmful side effects.
  - **How**: Pair a quantity or speed measure with a quality, safety, reliability, or customer measure.

## Key Concepts

- **Leverage**: An objective whose accomplishment produces a result beyond the value of ordinary activity.
- **Cadence**: The time rhythm for setting, checking, and grading goals.
- **Counter-effect**: A balancing measure that guards against optimizing one dimension at the expense of another.
- **Top-line OKR**: A company-level objective that signals what the whole organization should prioritize.
- **Selective goal setting**: Deliberately choosing a few commitments and rejecting or deferring the rest.

## Mental Models

- **Compass and baseline**: Top-line OKRs point the organization toward its destination and give it a basis for assessment.
- **Finite-resource portfolio**: Every commitment forfeits the capacity to commit elsewhere; adding work should displace work.
- **Wood behind fewer arrows**: Concentrated effort outperforms a large, impressive-looking list.

## Anti-patterns

- **The catchall wish list**: Too many objectives blur priorities and invite the next shiny thing to displace important work.
- **One-dimensional optimization**: Ford’s “under 2,000 pounds and under $2,000” Pinto targets omitted safety; Wells Fargo’s sales pressure produced fraudulent accounts.
- **Mission without measures**: A grand mission cannot tell a team what to do this quarter without concrete KRs.
- **Perfection paralysis**: Spending forever perfecting a line is less useful than launching a clear goal that can be updated as evidence arrives.

## Worked Example

YouTube product manager Rick Klau proposed a six-month objective to improve the login experience so more users could access saved videos and subscriptions. Larry Page elevated it to a Google-wide OKR and shortened the deadline to three months, which clarified the priority for other teams and attracted help; the team finished one week late but delivered. The chapter’s quality safeguard is the Pinto contrast: a target such as “three new features” should be paired with “fewer than five bugs per feature in QA.” A strong OKR makes the output and its counter-effect explicit, rather than allowing a cheap success to hide a dangerous one.

## Key Takeaways

1. Choose three to five objectives that deserve special attention and name what will not be done.
2. Communicate why the goals matter and model them visibly at the top.
3. Use quarterly execution goals to test annual strategy, while choosing a cadence that fits the context.
4. Pair quantity with quality and require all KRs together to be sufficient for the objective.
