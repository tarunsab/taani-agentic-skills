# Chapter 7: Superpower #2: Align and Connect for Teamwork

## Core Idea

Alignment means that people can see how their work connects to the organization’s vision, to the work of other teams, and to the dependencies that determine whether a result is possible. Public OKRs make priorities inspectable, expose duplication, invite help, and reduce the suspicion and politics that thrive in silos; the book cites survey evidence that most workers would be more motivated if colleagues could see their progress. A cascade from the top can create unity, but cascading every goal mechanically down many layers makes the system slow, inflexible, demotivating, and weak at horizontal coordination. Doerr therefore recommends a balance: leaders provide context and top-line direction, contributors negotiate the hows and create roughly half of their own OKRs when circumstances allow. Transparency also lets goals skip hierarchy, so an individual or team can connect directly to a relevant company objective without waiting for every managerial layer. Horizontal OKRs make dependencies explicit, so product, engineering, sales, and other peers can coordinate before a deadline is missed. The football example shows that alignment is not achieved by copying an objective downward; it requires measurable KRs, meaningful purpose, and room for local intelligence. The chapter’s ideal is a connected “team of teams” in which autonomy and common direction reinforce rather than cancel each other.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Transparent alignment**
  - **When to use**: When people cannot see the company’s priorities or how their work contributes.
  - **How**: Publish goals from the CEO down, connect them to mission, and invite peers to correct, support, or link them.
- **Balanced cascade**
  - **When to use**: When a company needs direction without bureaucratic lockstep.
  - **How**: Cascade outcomes or context selectively, negotiate KRs, and leave the method to the team closest to the work.
- **Horizontal dependency map**
  - **When to use**: When a project requires multiple departments or teams.
  - **How**: Name each material contribution in the relevant team’s KRs and let peers connect directly.

## Key Concepts

- **Vertical alignment**: Linking individual work upward to team, department, and company priorities.
- **Horizontal alignment**: Linking peer teams whose work must fit together.
- **Bottom-up OKR**: A goal generated by a contributor or team from local knowledge.
- **Rote cascade**: A mechanical chain of copied goals that is slow and excludes frontline judgment.
- **Autonomy**: Responsibility for deciding how to achieve rigorous standards and high goals.
- **20 percent time**: Google’s permission for engineers to pursue side projects, an example of productive unalignment.

## Mental Models

- **Vector addition**: If groups point in different directions, their effort cancels; common objectives let effort compound.
- **Market-based convergence**: Public goals allow visible teams to converge toward top priorities without every goal being cascaded by hand.
- **Rower alignment**: Two people rowing in opposite directions can work hard and go nowhere; shared goals make effort directional.

## Anti-patterns

- **Color-by-numbers cascade**: Waiting for goals to trickle through six or seven layers makes quarterly cycles impractical.
- **Unmeasurable KRs**: “Improve branding” or “improve media coverage” gives no objective basis for progress.
- **Meaningless top-level purpose**: “Make a wealthy owner wealthier” may not motivate the scout or intern who cannot see a meaningful connection.
- **Micromanagement**: Defining every how restricts initiative and turns professional employees into narrow task takers.

## Worked Example

The fictional Sand Hill Unicorns start with the objective **Make money for the owner**, measured by winning the Super Bowl and filling the stands to 90 percent. The head coach can turn winning into measurable KRs for passing, defense, and punt coverage, but the marketing cascade initially produces vague KRs such as “upgrade branding.” The example demonstrates two failures: poor measurement and a purpose that does not engage everyone. A better system would keep the shared direction, give teams measurable outcomes, and let a physical therapist or engineer propose a bottom-up OKR when local expertise reveals a valuable path. Google’s 20-percent Gmail origin story shows why some productive initiatives should emerge from the edges rather than from a full cascade.

## Key Takeaways

1. Make goals public enough to reveal duplication, dependencies, and opportunities to help.
2. Cascade context and outcomes selectively; do not dictate every local method.
3. Aim for a healthy bottom-up share and make cross-functional contributions explicit.
4. Preserve one common direction without confusing alignment with redundancy or micromanagement.
