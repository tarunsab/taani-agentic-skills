# Chapter 5: Focus: The Remind Story

## Core Idea

Remind illustrates focus as a practical discipline for a start-up whose mission is to improve communication among teachers, students, and families. Brett Kopf’s own difficulty with attention and school deadlines gave him a user problem, but early Remind lost time on investor meetings and elaborate website plans before speaking deeply enough with teachers. In the Imagine K12 accelerator, Brett set a ten-week objective to interview 200 teachers; 250 interviews revealed that teachers needed secure, private, low-friction messaging rather than another burdensome school system. The simple prototype validated the problem, and user-led growth quickly pushed the company from hundreds to millions of users. As scale made a thousand good ideas impossible to pursue at once, OKRs gave Remind a shared way to choose a few priorities and defer attractive features that would not move engagement. Brett kept three or four personal objectives visible, used “three buckets” as a daily focus question, and openly admitted when one was failing so the team could correct course. The company’s early overreach—seven or eight objectives when it could handle two—shows that adopting OKRs does not instantly create judgment. Focus became durable only through repeated cycles, quantified teacher metrics, and leaders willing to fire themselves from low-value operational jobs.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Solve, build, talk**
  - **When to use**: Before scaling a product or writing elaborate plans.
  - **How**: Solve a real problem, build the simplest useful product, and talk to users until the pain and value are clear.
- **Three-bucket focus**
  - **When to use**: For a leader’s daily choices amid a fast-growing operation.
  - **How**: Keep three or four visible objectives and ask what today’s work does to move one of them forward.
- **Teacher-obsessed metrics**
  - **When to use**: When registered-user counts hide whether a product actually delivers recurring value.
  - **How**: Track Weekly Active Teachers, Monthly Active Teachers, and retention.

## Key Concepts

- **Secure communication**: Remind’s product promise was messaging without exposing a teacher’s private phone number or social profile.
- **User discovery**: Direct interviews revealed the actual communication pain before the team scaled technology.
- **WAT / MAT**: Weekly Active Teachers and Monthly Active Teachers, measures of recurring teacher engagement.
- **Retention**: Whether users return, a more meaningful signal than registration alone.
- **Leadership transition**: Founders must hand off accounting, payroll, or other early jobs to focus on product and strategy.

## Mental Models

- **One big thing at a time**: A small team can make real progress by finishing its highest-value goal before opening the next front.
- **Mirror on the wall**: A visible list of objectives turns a physical reminder into a social commitment.
- **The user as compass**: A feature earns priority by improving the user’s important outcome, not by being requested often.

## Anti-patterns

- **Building for investors instead of users**: Remind’s early schematics and fundraising consumed scarce time while teacher problems stayed unexamined.
- **Vanity growth**: Registered users without return usage can conceal a weak product; Remind shifted to active-teacher and retention data.
- **Feature accumulation**: Repeated messages were highly requested but shelved because they were unlikely to move engagement enough to justify the engineering cost.
- **Overloaded OKR set**: Seven or eight objectives for a team with capacity for two make prioritization pretend rather than real.

## Worked Example

During the accelerator, Brett contacted 500 teachers and completed 250 one-on-ones against an objective of 200. Teachers described the limits of phone trees, permission slips, and ordinary texting: they needed a secure number, no personal data exposure, and less work. A paper prototype promised that a teacher could type a message, press a button, and reach students without revealing a phone number; teachers immediately recognized the solution. Later, a company objective to support hiring used KRs such as hiring a finance/operations director and sourcing product and marketing managers with interview-count evidence. These artifacts show the progression from user-learning to operational focus.

## Key Takeaways

1. Focus the product on a painful user problem before investing in scale.
2. Use behavior and retention measures rather than registrations alone.
3. Keep leaders’ few priorities visible and let new requests displace existing work explicitly.
4. Model failure openly; an admitted red is easier to fix than a hidden one.
