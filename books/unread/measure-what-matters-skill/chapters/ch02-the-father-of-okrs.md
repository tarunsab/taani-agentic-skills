# Chapter 2: The Father of OKRs

## Core Idea

Doerr traces OKRs to Andy Grove’s management practice at Intel and to Peter Drucker’s earlier idea of “management by objectives and self-control.” Grove kept Drucker’s results orientation and respect for individual responsibility, then made the system more frequent, measurable, transparent, and operationally rigorous. The chapter distinguishes output from the **activity trap**: knowledge workers should be judged by the results their work produces, not by how busy or knowledgeable they appear. Grove’s system also favored “achievement orientation”—what people can accomplish with what they know—over credentials alone. Compared with conventional MBOs, Intel OKRs were quarterly or monthly rather than annual, public rather than private, bottom-up or sideways as well as top-down, mostly separate from compensation, and aggressive rather than risk averse. Doerr’s own 8080 benchmark OKR shows the method’s practicality, while Grove’s creative-confrontation style shows that measurable goals need direct, egalitarian discussion rather than passive compliance. The chapter closes with “basic OKR hygiene”: choose few goals, negotiate them, stay flexible, dare to fail, keep goals separate from compensation, and be patient while the organization builds goal muscle.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Drucker’s management by objectives and self-control**
  - **When to use**: When common direction must coexist with individual strength and responsibility.
  - **How**: Consult people on goals, connect short- and long-range planning, and measure results through ongoing conversations.
- **Intel OKRs**
  - **When to use**: When knowledge work needs concrete output and fast feedback.
  - **How**: Set a directional objective, add measurable KRs, publish them, revisit them frequently, and keep them mostly outside compensation.
- **Creative confrontation**
  - **When to use**: When a management problem or proposal needs honest examination.
  - **How**: Face the issue directly, leave rank outside the meeting, and challenge the idea without attacking the person.

## Key Concepts

- **Achievement orientation**: Valuing the ability to translate knowledge into actual results.
- **Activity trap**: Increasing visible activity while accidentally reducing real productivity.
- **iMBOs**: Intel’s name for its management-by-objectives system, which Doerr calls OKRs for clarity.
- **Output**: The result produced by work, especially the meaningful result of a knowledge worker’s effort.
- **Goal muscle**: The organizational capacity to formulate, discuss, measure, and improve goals over repeated cycles.

## Mental Models

| Conventional MBO | Intel OKR |
|---|---|
| What only | What and how |
| Annual | Quarterly or monthly |
| Private and siloed | Public and transparent |
| Top-down | Bottom-up or sideways, roughly 50% |
| Tied to compensation | Mostly divorced from compensation |
| Risk averse | Aggressive and aspirational |

## Anti-patterns

- **Activity as a proxy for output**: More meetings, documents, or visible effort do not necessarily mean more value.
- **Stale or siloed goals**: Annual goals that cannot be updated or seen by peers lose operational force.
- **Compensation-bound goals**: If failure threatens pay, people avoid ambitious targets and protect themselves through sandbagging.

## Worked Example

Doerr’s first Intel OKR set the objective **Demonstrate the 8080’s superior performance compared with the Motorola 6800**. Its KRs were to deliver five benchmarks, develop a demo, create sales-training materials, and call on three customers to prove the material worked. The objective gave a direction; the KRs made it possible to verify both the technical work and its usefulness in the field. Because the hard copy was visible on Doerr’s carrel, it also protected focus: he could decline mid-quarter requests for unrelated data sheets without hiding his priorities.

## Key Takeaways

1. Measure meaningful output, not busyness or credentials.
2. Use frequent, public, measurable goals to make priorities operational.
3. Keep the objective directional and the KR testable; do not confuse the two.
4. Build the system through repeated cycles, not a single perfect launch.
