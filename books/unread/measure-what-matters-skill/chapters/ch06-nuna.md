# Chapter 6: Commit: The Nuna Story

## Core Idea

Nuna shows that OKR success depends on leaders committing both to the organization’s highest-impact work and to the goal-setting process itself. Jini Kim’s family experience with Medicaid and her work at Google gave Nuna a mission to use data to improve health care, but the start-up initially had no orders and had to learn its market through rejection. After winning a contract to build the first database spanning Medicaid’s 74.5 million members, Nuna had to scale its business, infrastructure, security, and people while delivering a historic platform within a year. Jini’s first company-wide OKR launch failed because she rolled out quarterly and annual goals to everyone at once, and many contributors never set or used them. The repair began with a smaller executive group, visible modeling, repeated follow-up, and the admission that executives may need a quarter or two to build the habit. Jini published her own goals and grades, invited contributors to improve them, and used a private OKR for a sensitive VP hire when public visibility would have complicated the early work. The chapter’s lesson is not that commitment means never changing a goal; it means staying with the purpose and repeatedly adapting the process until it becomes useful. Sustained executive example made deadlines feel clearer and more attainable, and helped Nuna use OKRs to act purposefully instead of reacting to every external event.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Commitment to the process**
  - **When to use**: When introducing OKRs to a new organization or after a weak first attempt.
  - **How**: Start with leaders, have them publish their own goals, inspect progress, and persist through resistance.
- **Model the behavior**
  - **When to use**: When contributors are unsure whether the goal system is real or merely administrative.
  - **How**: Leaders share their own OKRs, grades, gaps, and invitations for feedback; then hold themselves accountable.
- **Public and private OKRs**
  - **When to use**: Publicly for shared priorities; privately when a confidential commitment needs an initial push.
  - **How**: Keep the private goal limited to the accountable owners, then move the resulting work into the normal system when appropriate.

## Key Concepts

- **Goal muscle**: The capability to write, discuss, and use OKRs that grows through cycles rather than a launch event.
- **Executive buy-in**: Leaders’ visible willingness to use and improve the process before expecting contributors to follow.
- **Sustained commitment**: Continuing to inspect and support goals even when an aspirational objective becomes uncomfortable.
- **Public accountability**: Making a leader’s priorities and performance open to constructive evaluation.
- **False start**: A rollout that looks complete administratively but fails to change behavior.

## Mental Models

- **Stormy voyage**: If officers abandon an aspirational course mid-storm, the crew will not carry it into port; leaders must stay the course visibly.
- **Marathon training**: Introducing too much process too quickly creates pain; build capacity incrementally.
- **Hairy mission, stronger system**: The more daunting the mission, the more important precise goals and committed leadership become.

## Anti-patterns

- **Big-bang rollout**: Sending annual and quarterly OKRs to every employee at once can produce drawers full of goals rather than usage.
- **Leader exemption**: Asking contributors to publish goals while executives keep theirs private or unchanged undermines trust.
- **Complacent sponsorship**: An initial all-hands announcement is not enough; the shepherd and leaders must keep after the process.

## Worked Example

After relaunching OKRs, Jini published a hiring objective, **Continue to build a world-class team**, with KRs to recruit ten engineers, hire a commercial sales leader, and ensure every candidate had a well-organized experience. Her grades—0.8, 1.0, and 0.5—made the tradeoffs discussable rather than hiding behind a single success label. A second objective, to create a healthy and productive environment while scaling past 150 employees, measured completion of feedback cycles and prompt scoring of individual OKRs. For a sensitive VP hire, Jini and David used a private OKR to start stakeholder conversations and recruitment without prematurely unsettling the organization.

## Key Takeaways

1. Start with leaders when trust and goal-writing habits are unformed.
2. Publish imperfect goals and grades; the example carries more weight than the speech.
3. Treat early failures as feedback about rollout design, not proof that the method is useless.
4. Use privacy selectively for sensitive commitments, while keeping the larger operating system transparent.
