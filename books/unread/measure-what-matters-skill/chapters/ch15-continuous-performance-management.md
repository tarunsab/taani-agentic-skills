# Chapter 15: Continuous Performance Management: OKRs and CFRs

## Core Idea

Doerr pairs OKRs with continuous performance management because goals alone cannot supply the context, coaching, and human recognition required for sustained performance. He criticizes annual reviews as costly, biased, backward-looking, and too infrequent to help someone improve while the work is happening. **CFRs**—Conversations, Feedback, and Recognition—give OKRs their human voice: conversations make an authentic exchange, feedback is bidirectional or networked, and recognition appreciates contributions of all sizes. OKRs and CFRs reinforce one another; without goals, conversations lack a concrete subject, while without conversations a score cannot explain difficulty, motivation, or what to change. The proposed one-on-one cadence covers goal setting and reflection, progress updates, two-way coaching, career growth, and a lightweight performance review, with the contributor setting the agenda and the manager learning and coaching. Feedback should be specific and timely, and peer feedback is especially useful in cross-functional work; recognition should be frequent, attainable, visible, and tied to values or goals. Doerr recommends divorcing forward-looking goals from compensation and stack rankings because direct pay consequences invite sandbagging, defensive targets, and risk aversion. Annual compensation decisions may still use goal data as one context among many, but not as a mechanical conversion from score to bonus. The result is a more continuous, strength-based, multidirectional system that treats performance as something to develop rather than merely rate.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **CFRs**
  - **When to use**: When a goal system needs context, coaching, reinforcement, and relationship.
  - **How**: Hold regular conversations, give specific two-way feedback, and recognize meaningful contributions.
- **Five one-on-one conversations**
  - **When to use**: In recurring manager–contributor performance dialogue.
  - **How**: Cover goal setting/reflection, progress, coaching, career growth, and lightweight review.
- **Amicable divorce**
  - **When to use**: When designing compensation and goal systems.
  - **How**: Keep ongoing goals and development forward-looking; hold compensation as a separate, backward-looking conversation.

## Key Concepts

- **Conversation**: An authentic exchange aimed at driving performance and understanding the person behind the goal.
- **Feedback**: An observation-based opinion that helps a person know the impression they make and improve.
- **Recognition**: Appreciation of a contribution, result, value, or effort, often peer to peer.
- **Contributor-owned one-on-one**: A meeting whose agenda and tone are primarily set by the person doing the work.
- **Intrinsic motivation**: Engagement from meaningful progress, purpose, mastery, and growth rather than only financial reward.

## Mental Models

- **Goalposts and huddle**: OKRs mark the target and yard lines; CFRs are the huddles, coaching, postmortems, and celebrations that let the team play.
- **Forward versus backward**: Goal conversations steer future work; compensation reviews summarize past contribution.
- **Networked performance**: People learn from managers, peers, cross-functional partners, and direct reports rather than one annual evaluator.

## Anti-patterns

- **Annual verdict**: Waiting for a year-end review delays correction and magnifies recency bias.
- **Goal-to-bonus arithmetic**: Treating a stretch score as a pay formula makes people avoid hard goals and hide context.
- **Vague feedback**: “Good job” or “you are disorganized” gives too little information to repeat or change behavior.
- **Recognition as scarcity**: Reserving appreciation for a single annual award misses small wins and makes peers competitors.

## Worked Example

Pact replaced its annual review with four recurring elements: monthly manager–employee one-on-ones, quarterly OKR progress reviews, semiannual professional-development conversations, and ongoing self-driven requests for specific feedback. A presentation could prompt “What one thing did you like about it?” rather than ending with a generic compliment. In Doerr’s compensation contrast, a contributor who reaches 75 percent of extreme stretch goals may have outperformed one who reaches 90 percent of easy goals, so scores cannot fairly determine bonuses on their own. The system must keep the data while restoring judgment and context.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use OKRs for direction and CFRs for the continuous human work around direction.
2. Make one-on-ones recurring, contributor-centered, and rich enough to include growth and coaching.
3. Make feedback specific, two-way, timely, and useful across team boundaries.
4. Separate goal setting from compensation to protect ambition and honest learning.
