# Chapter 13: Stretch: The Google Chrome Story

## Core Idea

Chrome shows a stretch OKR working through repeated misses, systematic problem solving, and a refusal to confuse failure with final defeat. Sundar Pichai’s team saw the browser as a next-generation applications platform rather than a modest improvement to existing browsers, so its 2008 annual objective was to develop that platform and reach 20 million seven-day active users. The target was deliberately uncomfortable: Chrome started at zero and struggled to reach three percent market share, while its Mac version lagged. A stretch goal still gave the team direction, a measurable barometer, and a reason to rethink distribution, compatibility, marketing, and architecture. The team’s V8 sub-OKR targeted a 10x JavaScript improvement, and Lars Bak achieved that within four months, eventually exceeding it. Chrome missed 20 million in 2008 and missed a 50-million target in 2009, but each miss created a sharper question about what to do differently. In 2010, Pichai and Larry Page settled on 111 million seven-day actives; OEM distribution, “Chrome Fast” marketing, Mac/Linux releases, and a passive alert for dormant users helped the team reach the goal. The story preserves a limit: Google’s resources and culture made repeated experimentation possible, but the lesson is not to ignore capacity; leaders must challenge teams without making the goal feel unachievable. By chunking a huge ambition into measurable annual and quarterly targets, OKRs made the next frontier—Chrome OS and Chromebooks—thinkable.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Uncomfortably excited**
  - **When to use**: When a team needs ambition without surrendering belief in the work.
  - **How**: Set a target at the edge of ability, explain why it matters, and use progress to drive new experiments.
- **Stretch sub-OKR**
  - **When to use**: When the main goal depends on a technical breakthrough.
  - **How**: Isolate the bottleneck, set a measurable improvement target, and let the specialist pursue the fastest route.
- **Try-fail, try-succeed**
  - **When to use**: After a meaningful stretch miss.
  - **How**: Keep the valuable objective, diagnose the barrier, change the approach, and reset the next measurable target.

## Key Concepts

- **Seven-day active user**: Chrome’s recurring-use metric for the annual stretch objective.
- **V8**: The project that targeted a tenfold JavaScript performance improvement.
- **Distribution deal**: An external route, such as an OEM relationship, for putting Chrome in front of more users.
- **Healthy disregard for the impossible**: A leadership stance that treats apparent impossibility as a prompt for better questions.
- **Chunking**: Breaking a large, qualitative leap into annual and quarterly quantitative targets.

## Mental Models

- **Start over**: A 500-mile-per-gallon car cannot be made by a small tune-up; some goals require a new architecture.
- **Troughs are information**: A dip in users or a missed target tells the team which constraint to investigate next.
- **Stars and moon**: Aiming at an extreme target can still produce a remarkable, nearer achievement.

## Anti-patterns

- **Safe stretch**: A goal that cannot fail is probably not testing the team’s limits.
- **Miss-and-abandon**: Dropping a valuable objective after one miss throws away the learning the stretch created.
- **Heroics without diagnosis**: Sleepless effort is not a strategy; Chrome paired experiments with specific causes such as distribution and compatibility.

## Worked Example

Chrome’s sequence was 20 million seven-day actives for 2008, 50 million for 2009, and 111 million for 2010. The first two targets were missed, at zero-to-20 million and 38 million respectively, but the misses triggered distribution deals, broader OS support, marketing, and reactivation experiments. The 2010 target was reached after users rose from 87 million to 107 million late in Q3 and then crossed 111 million shortly afterward. The technical companion example is V8: a tenfold JavaScript goal was met within four months and later exceeded twentyfold, showing why an aspirational sub-goal can uncover capability the team initially underestimates.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use a measurable stretch bar to make complacency impossible.
2. After a miss, preserve the mission-relevant objective but change the mechanism.
3. Break moonshots into bottleneck-specific sub-OKRs and quarterly experiments.
4. Keep the target challenging, meaningful, and believable enough to sustain commitment.
