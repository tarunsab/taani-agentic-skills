# Chapter 12: Superpower #4: Stretch for Amazing

## Core Idea

Stretching asks an organization to pursue results on the border between current ability and a compelling dream. Doerr argues that conservative goals can preserve comfort while innovation requires people to test limits, and he connects difficult, specific goals to higher performance, motivation, and task interest in Locke’s research. A **BHAG**—Big Hairy Audacious Goal—works when it is clear, daunting, and meaningful enough to unify effort, not merely large for its own sake. Google separates committed goals, expected to reach 100 percent, from aspirational goals whose uncertain route or resources justify an average around 70 percent. The mix is cultural and situational: a cash-constrained company in survival mode may need more commitments, while a company with room to experiment can carry more moonshots. Stretch goals require the other superpowers—focus, alignment, tracking, and support—because a target that is neither resourced nor psychologically safe becomes demoralizing. Intel’s 2,000 design-win rally and Gmail’s one-gigabyte storage leap show how a bold target can mobilize new thinking and redefine a category. Google’s “Gospel of 10x” asks teams to rethink the problem rather than pursue a routine ten-percent improvement. The chapter’s boundary is equally important: there is no magic stretch number, and a goal should be challenging without being obviously impossible or detached from the organization’s capacity and purpose.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Committed versus aspirational OKR baskets**
  - **When to use**: At the beginning of a cycle when a team is deciding what must ship and what should stretch.
  - **How**: Label commitments for 1.0 delivery and aspirational goals for learning and breakthrough; set the portfolio mix to current operating needs.
- **BHAG**
  - **When to use**: When a unifying, future-tilting goal can galvanize a team.
  - **How**: State a clear mountain to climb, connect it to meaning, and give people enough autonomy and support to find the route.
- **Gospel of 10x**
  - **When to use**: When incremental improvement would leave the category unchanged.
  - **How**: Ask what a tenfold result would require, then redesign the product, distribution, or problem frame accordingly.

## Key Concepts

- **Stretch goal**: A difficult target that tests the outer limits of ability and can elicit new capacity.
- **Committed OKR**: An agreed result that receives resource and priority adjustments to reach 1.0.
- **Aspirational OKR**: A high-risk, uncertain-path goal whose expected average is lower than 1.0.
- **Risk tolerance**: The organization’s current ability and willingness to absorb failed experiments.
- **10x thinking**: Exponential improvement that often requires starting over rather than tuning the existing system.

## Mental Models

- **Mountain to climb**: A BHAG gives the team a visible summit, even when the route is not known.
- **Stretch as a cultural variable**: The same failure rate can energize one organization and crush another.
- **Play again**: Meeting a hard goal is not permission to coast; it earns the right to set the next difficult objective.

## Anti-patterns

- **Unrealistic stretch**: If people know they cannot succeed, the goal damages morale instead of prompting creativity.
- **Stretch without support**: A large target imposed without resources, autonomy, or leadership backing becomes a long march to nowhere.
- **All-green aspiration**: If every aspirational goal is achieved easily, the portfolio is probably timid.
- **10x as a slogan**: Exponential language without a changed problem frame or measurable path is theatrics, not innovation.

## Worked Example

Intel’s sales force first considered 1,000 8086 design wins, then raised the rallying point to 2,000—roughly one win per salesperson per month for a product customers disliked. A Tahiti incentive and district-wide peer pressure accompanied the goal, but the key effect was that the number unified the organization around a new sales effort; the final tally exceeded 2,300. Google’s Gmail team provides a different 10x example: instead of releasing the contemplated 100MB of storage, Gmail launched with a full gigabyte, up to hundreds of times the norm, and changed user expectations for web mail. Both examples show that the goal must be vivid enough to provoke new behavior, not merely a larger forecast.

## Key Takeaways

1. Label must-deliver work separately from uncertain, aspirational work.
2. Use BHAGs and 10x questions to break out of incremental thinking.
3. Match the stretch mix to culture, cash, capability, and the current operating need.
4. Make failure safe enough to learn from, but never use stretch as an excuse for careless planning.
