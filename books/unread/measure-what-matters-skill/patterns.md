# Patterns and Anti-patterns

## Write the What/How Pair
**When to use**: A goal is important but its success criteria are still vague.
**How**: Write one significant objective, then three to five measurable, time-bound KRs that state outcomes and credible completion evidence.
**Trade-offs**: More KRs can look thorough but dilute focus; too few may be necessary but not sufficient.

An objective gives direction while KRs make the next cycle testable. The test is whether a rational observer could tell if the objective was achieved and whether scoring all KRs at 1.0 would actually achieve its intent. Use real dates and unambiguous metric definitions. Replace “launch X” or “analyze Y” with the user, economic, quality, or operational result that makes the activity matter. (Ch 1, Ch 4, Resource 1)

## Put More Wood Behind Fewer Arrows
**When to use**: A team has a long roadmap, many urgent requests, or unclear priorities.
**How**: Choose three to five high-leverage objectives; explicitly discard, defer, or deemphasize other work and require new work to displace something.
**Trade-offs**: Saying no creates short-term disappointment, but attempting everything produces scattered effort and hidden nondecisions.

Focus is a resource-allocation decision, not a motivational slogan. Remind and MyFitnessPal both learned that growth made the old short list insufficient unless the organization named what mattered most. Leaders must communicate the why and model the choices themselves. (Ch 4–5, Ch 8)

## Pair Quantity with Quality
**When to use**: A target could be hit by sacrificing safety, ethics, reliability, customer value, or reputation.
**How**: Add a counter-effect KR measuring the balancing dimension; review both together.
**Trade-offs**: The paired metric adds complexity, but one-dimensional targets invite Ford Pinto- or Wells Fargo-style failure.

The quantity target creates pressure; the quality target makes the pressure safe and useful. The pair should be concrete enough to expose a bad trade rather than merely restating a value. Use it for sales, throughput, code, service, and any other output where “more” can damage the system. (Ch 4)

## Align Through Visibility, Not Rote Cascades
**When to use**: Work crosses teams or people are unsure how their priorities relate.
**How**: Publish top-line OKRs, let teams negotiate the hows, make lateral dependencies explicit, and give each OKR one accountable owner.
**Trade-offs**: Full cascade provides direction in urgent conditions, but rote cascades become slow, rigid, and marginalize frontline insight.

Use transparency to expose duplicate work and attract help. Aim for roughly half bottom-up OKRs when the business can tolerate autonomy. Cross-team KRs should name each material contribution, while the objective keeps a single owner so accountability does not dissolve. (Ch 7–9, Resource 1)

## Run the Four Tracking Moves
**When to use**: A goal is underway and evidence or circumstances have changed.
**How**: **Continue** an on-track goal; **Update** a yellow goal’s scope, timing, or resources; **Start** a needed mid-cycle OKR; **Stop** a red goal that no longer serves the purpose. Notify dependent stakeholders and capture the learning.
**Trade-offs**: Flexibility prevents zombie goals, but silent edits destroy trust and conceal recurring planning problems.

Weekly or otherwise context-appropriate check-ins keep the plan close to reality. At cycle end, combine a completion score, a contextual self-assessment, and reflection. Keep a relevant aspirational goal visible across cycles rather than disguising a resource or prioritization problem by deleting it. (Ch 10–11, Resource 2)

## Separate Commitments from Aspirations
**When to use**: The team is deciding which goals must be delivered and which goals are meant to stretch.
**How**: Label committed OKRs (expected 1.0; escalate early if at risk) and aspirational OKRs (expected average about 0.7; use remaining capacity by priority).
**Trade-offs**: Mixing the types creates either complacency or defensiveness, and tying either directly to bonuses encourages sandbagging.

Committed work gets resources and a postmortem if it misses. Aspirational work should be ambitious but meaningful, supported by psychological safety, and carried forward if still relevant. Adjust the mix to the organization’s current operating need, risk tolerance, and capacity. (Ch 12, Ch 14, Resource 1)

## Pair OKRs with CFRs
**When to use**: A goal system has data but lacks context, learning, coaching, or human reinforcement.
**How**: Use regular contributor-led conversations, specific two-way feedback, and visible recognition tied to values and results; keep compensation conversations distinct.
**Trade-offs**: Continuous dialogue takes manager capability and time, but annual ratings alone are too late and distort risk-taking.

OKRs provide the goalposts; CFRs explain how people are progressing, what they need, and what deserves reinforcement. One-on-ones should cover goals, progress, coaching, career growth, and lightweight review. Recognition is strongest when frequent, attainable, peer-aware, and connected to the company’s priorities. (Ch 15–17, Resource 3)

## Culture Before or Alongside the Tool
**When to use**: People distrust transparency, accountability, feedback, or the organization’s stated values.
**How**: Diagnose cultural barriers, model the values, train managers, create psychological safety, then use OKRs/CFRs to sustain the change; pulse the culture in real time.
**Trade-offs**: Delaying rollout can feel slow, but Lumeris shows that a superficial launch creates compliance theater rather than execution.

Values become credible through repeated behavior, not slogans. Balance OKR catalysts—clarity, autonomy, resources, learning—with CFR nourishers—respect, encouragement, recognition, and affiliation. Use mission-linked OKRs to make culture operational, not ornamental. (Ch 18–20)
