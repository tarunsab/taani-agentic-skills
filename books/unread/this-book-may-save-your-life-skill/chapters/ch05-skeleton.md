# Chapter 5: Failure to Load

## Core Idea

Rajan treats the skeleton as living infrastructure rather than inert scaffolding: bone stores minerals, remodels under load, protects organs, anchors muscle, and lets humans stand and move upright. That success comes with engineering compromises, especially in the knee and spine, where stability, range of motion, and bipedal posture compete. Bone density peaks relatively early and then declines, while muscle, balance, cartilage, and proprioception also determine whether the frame remains usable. Exercise is framed as graded loading and coordination, not punishment for pain; inactivity can weaken the system, but inappropriate force can injure it. The practical message is to keep moving, build strength and balance, choose footwear and ergonomics sensibly, and escalate persistent or neurologically concerning pain instead of chasing a single miracle adjustment.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Living infrastructure**: Bones adapt to forces, repair microdamage, and depend on muscle, tendons, cartilage, nerves, and nutrition.
- **Load is information**: Appropriate weight-bearing and resistance tell bone and muscle to maintain capacity; zero load removes that signal.
- **Function before alignment stories**: Mobility, strength, balance, and red flags matter more than a simplistic promise that one manipulation will “put the spine back.”

## THE SHORTCOMINGS OF THE SKELETON

The adult skeleton has 206 bones, but Rajan’s point is not the count: the frame is a living tissue with a vascular outer periosteum, marrow, joints, ligaments, tendons, and cartilage. Bone density generally reaches a peak in the thirties and declines later, with menopause accelerating loss in many women. Calcium and vitamin D support mineralization, but the book rejects the idea that an unsafe tan is a sensible vitamin strategy. A strong frame therefore depends on food, muscle, sunlight or appropriate supplementation, movement, and the absence of avoidable toxins—not on one nutrient in isolation.

Upright walking was an evolutionary advantage, but it concentrates load through a narrow base and a spine that was not originally a perfectly vertical column. The skeleton protects the brain and organs while also creating pinch points, friction surfaces, and leverage problems. Rajan’s recurring distinction is between normal wear or an odd sensation and a pattern suggesting fracture, neurological compression, infection, or another condition that needs assessment.

### Close to the bone

Bone’s outer covering is pain-sensitive, which helps explain why fractures and inflammation hurt even though the hard mineral center may seem lifeless. Remodeling replaces old tissue and responds to forces placed through it. Weight-bearing activity and resistance training are useful because they challenge both bone and the muscles that stabilize joints, while prolonged inactivity, illness, and aging remove those signals. The advice has limits: a newly injured limb, suspected fracture, or severe osteoporosis needs individualized care rather than a generic loading program.

### Save yourself

Rajan’s baseline is regular weight-bearing movement, strength work, and attention to calcium- and vitamin-D-containing foods without treating supplements as automatic. Balance practice matters because a strong bone can still break in a fall. He also stresses not smoking and moderating alcohol, both of which undermine wider musculoskeletal and cardiovascular health. The book’s practical tone is “maintain capacity before it is needed,” not “exercise through any pain.”

## ON YOUR KNEES

The knee is primarily a hinge, while the hip is a ball-and-socket joint; the knee therefore sacrifices some stability to allow efficient walking and a useful range of flexion. The anterior cruciate ligament and other structures guide movement, and proprioceptive information tells the brain where the joint is even when the eyes are elsewhere. A knee that feels unreliable can reflect ligament injury, weakness, swelling, or coordination—not simply an age label. Cartilage has limited repair capacity, so repeated high load, injury, and inflammation can contribute to osteoarthritis.

### Save yourself

Rajan recommends keeping the surrounding muscles active and using gradual loading rather than abandoning movement at the first sign of stiffness. Low-impact aerobic exercise, strength work, and balance practice can support function, although the right program depends on the diagnosis. Swelling, locking, giving way, a major injury, or inability to bear weight are reasons to seek assessment. A “good knee” is not defined by never bending it; it is defined by useful, controlled movement without a dangerous pattern.

### We can rebuild it

Joint replacement and reconstructive surgery show that the frame can sometimes be repaired or substituted, but neither restores the original biology perfectly. Metal, plastic, grafts, and rehabilitation can return function when pain and damage have crossed a threshold. Rajan uses these interventions to distinguish repair from prevention: surgery is a valuable option for selected failure, not proof that joints can be abused indefinitely. Recovery depends on muscles, balance, and adherence to rehabilitation as well as on the implant or repair.

### Save yourself

The sensible knee rule is to distribute load, strengthen hips and legs, and progress slowly enough that tissues can adapt. Reducing excess body weight may lower repeated joint forces, but the author does not reduce knee pain to weight alone. Pain that is persistent, worsening, associated with fever or marked swelling, or accompanied by mechanical locking deserves clinical evaluation. Exercise should be modified to preserve function, not used as a test of toughness.

## FEET FIRST

Each foot contains 26 bones plus joints, ligaments, tendons, nerves, and a skin surface that must tolerate repeated impact. Shoes protect from heat, sharp surfaces, and infection, but rigid or poorly fitting footwear can constrain the toes and alter how forces travel up the leg. Rajan compares conventional shoes with periods of barefoot or minimalist movement, while acknowledging that adaptation must be gradual and that foot shape, diabetes, neuropathy, and terrain change the risk calculation. Toenails, skin breaks, numbness, and pressure points matter because a small wound can become a serious problem when sensation or circulation is impaired.

### Save yourself

Choose shoes that fit the actual foot, allow the toes room, and match the activity; do not switch suddenly to minimalist footwear and expect tissues to tolerate an unfamiliar load. Inspect feet regularly, especially if diabetes or reduced sensation makes injury hard to feel. Keep skin and nails cared for without cutting into inflamed tissue, and get persistent wounds, color changes, or numbness assessed. The chapter’s foot advice is maintenance and monitoring, not a claim that one footwear philosophy is universally correct.

## GOT BACK

The spine’s curves help distribute force, but the column also protects a non-regenerating spinal cord and contains discs that can bulge, degenerate, or irritate nerves. Back pain is common and often improves with time, movement, and appropriate physiotherapy, yet the commonness of a symptom does not make every presentation benign. Rajan is skeptical of simplistic “misalignment” narratives and of assuming that a scan’s incidental finding explains all pain. The useful target is function: gait, strength, range of motion, sleep, work setup, and the person’s trajectory.

### Save yourself

Avoid staying in one posture for long periods; vary positions, use a workable desk and lifting setup, and build trunk and hip strength gradually. Movement is usually preferable to prolonged bed rest for uncomplicated mechanical pain, but it should be adapted to symptoms. A pain plan may include physiotherapy, medication, and time, with specialist review when the pattern persists or a structural problem is suspected. Severe pain after trauma, progressive weakness, numbness in a saddle distribution, or new loss of bladder or bowel control is an escalation signal.

### Twists and turns

Rajan discusses spinal manipulation and the rare but serious possibility of vertebral-artery dissection associated with forceful neck techniques. This does not mean every manual therapy session causes harm, but it is a reason to ask what problem is being treated, what evidence supports the intervention, and what warning symptoms require urgent care. Massage and acupuncture may help some people with symptoms, yet their limits should be stated rather than converted into claims of structural correction. A treatment that feels dramatic is not automatically a treatment that changes the underlying disease.

### Pull yourself together

The chapter returns to posture and the modern habit of bending over phones. “Text neck” is used as a memorable load example, not as a new diagnosis that explains every headache. Let the neck and back change position, bring screens closer to eye level, take movement breaks, and strengthen rather than constantly brace. If symptoms radiate, worsen, or fail to settle after several weeks, the maintenance loop has become a diagnostic question.

## The skeleton of the foot

This anatomy pause reinforces that the foot is a coordinated arch and lever, not a stack of isolated bones. Its small joints and soft tissues absorb and return force while communicating with the balance system. That complexity is why footwear changes, surface changes, and injury should be introduced progressively.

## Health Hack

Rajan suggests that a prebiotic fiber or probiotic approach can be useful alongside antibiotics because gut bacteria may be disrupted during treatment. The mechanism is ecological rather than magical: provide substrate or selected microbes while the intestinal community recovers. He also implies an important limit—products differ, symptoms may have another cause, and probiotics do not make every antibiotic course safer or more effective. Follow the prescriber’s instructions and seek help for severe diarrhea, dehydration, or blood.

## Worked Example

The chapter’s manipulation warning is a useful risk–benefit case. A person with ordinary neck or back pain may be offered a forceful correction framed as a way to restore alignment, while the relevant evidence may concern symptom relief rather than permanent structural repair. Rare vascular injury makes the downside non-zero, so the safer reasoning sequence is diagnosis, conservative rehabilitation, informed consent, and clear red-flag instructions. Rajan’s broader lesson is to match the intensity of an intervention to the certainty and seriousness of the problem.

## Key Takeaways

1. Bone, cartilage, muscle, nerves, and balance form one load-bearing system.
2. Graded strength, weight-bearing, balance, good footwear, and varied posture maintain capacity.
3. Common back or knee pain is not automatically dangerous, but trauma, progressive weakness, saddle numbness, or bladder/bowel change are red flags.
4. Be skeptical of single-cause alignment claims and weigh rare serious harms before forceful manipulation.
