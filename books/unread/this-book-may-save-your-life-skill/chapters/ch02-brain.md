# Chapter 2: The Lights Are On…

## Core Idea

Rajan treats the brain as a high-energy, imperfect operating system: about 1.3 kg, roughly 2 percent of body weight, yet consuming around 20 percent of energy. Its billion-scale neural network enables language, art, memory, and prediction, but it also carries inherited shortcuts that fit a hunter-gatherer world better than a modern one. Brain function is distributed rather than cleanly divided into a dominant “left” or “right” half, and cases such as Phineas Gage show that injury can reveal specialization without making the organ simple. The chapter pairs this fragility with plasticity: exercise, learning, social contact, sleep, and stress management can preserve function, while repeated head injury, chronic stress, and multitasking exact costs. Pain is the clearest example of the brain's constructive role: nociceptors provide danger signals, but the experience is assembled from those signals, context, memory, emotion, and expectation.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Brain as legacy operating system**: Use it to spot mismatches between evolved circuitry and modern demands.
- **Prediction and simulation**: Perception is a filtered forecast, not a raw recording of the world.
- **Pain as Brain Inc.**: Separate noxious input from the brain's constructed experience.
- **Cognitive reserve**: Preserve function through sustained mental, physical, and social activity.

## ALL ABOUT THE BRAIN

### The organ that named itself

The two hemispheres communicate through the corpus callosum and do not support the popular claim that a person is simply “left-brained” or “right-brained.” Some regions specialize, but nearly every action recruits networks across the brain. Einstein's dissected brain had a few minor differences, including a denser corpus callosum, but was mostly ordinary anatomy; Rajan uses this to resist simplistic genius diagrams. Neuroscience learns especially from patients with damage because before-and-after changes reveal function without intentionally injuring anyone.

### When brains go boom

Phineas Gage survived a tamping iron passing through his frontal brain, but his reported shift from reliable and restrained to impulsive and rude linked frontal regions with self-control and higher-level behavior. His later recovery of social skills also prevents a one-region/one-personality conclusion. The case illustrates that memory and attention can remain relatively intact while other functions change. It is evidence of both specialization and compensation.

### Why bother with a brain?

The brain is the organ that supplies thought, language, self-awareness, and the ability to study itself, but Rajan repeatedly emphasizes that its complexity exceeds current explanations. Injury cases are more informative than treating a scan as a direct picture of consciousness. The book's “operating system” analogy is therefore deliberately incomplete: useful for noticing glitches, not a literal account of neural computation.

### Saturday night in the emergency room

Rajan's young assault patient arrived confused, nauseated, and barely lucid, with a fixed dilated pupil, slow pulse, high blood pressure, and irregular breathing. Together these signs suggested Cushing's triad from rising intracranial pressure; a CT showed a lemon-shaped extradural hematoma between skull and dura. The pterion, a thin junction behind the temple over the middle meningeal artery, is involved in many such bleeds. The clinical lesson is speed: a mass that compresses the brain can cause herniation and death, so recognition and neurosurgical care cannot wait.

### Punch drunk

Repeated low-grade head impacts can be damaging even without one dramatic bleed. The old label dementia pugilistica is now discussed as chronic traumatic encephalopathy, associated with repetitive traumatic brain injury across boxing and contact sports. Rajan cites the 15,000-person PROTECT study as finding worse cognitive effects with three or more concussions and worsening with each subsequent injury. He links trauma to disrupted cerebrospinal-fluid “washing” during sleep, while keeping the practical conclusion simple: protect the head and do not treat concussion as a harmless reset.

## HACKING THE BRAIN

### Eat yourself smarter

Gut microbes can make neurotransmitter-related chemicals and communicate with the brain through the vagus nerve; the route is two-way because stress can also inflame and disrupt the gut. Rajan connects a processed-food-heavy diet with brain fog and poorer blood flow through cholesterol, then names iron, vitamin D, protein, zinc, and omega-3 sources as nutrients involved in blood, neurotransmitter, membrane, or mood functions. He distinguishes food-derived omega-3 from supplements, noting that supplements have not shown the same effects in the book's discussion. The practical rule is balanced diet first, and clinical investigation for symptoms such as fatigue, low mood, or concentration change.

### In the fog

Long Covid is used as a current example of a post-viral brain problem: memory, motivation, focus, mood, and MRI structure can remain altered after the acute infection. Rajan compares it with encephalitis lethargica after the 1918 influenza pandemic, where fatigue, apathy, and depression were prominent. “Brain fog” is a useful description rather than a precise diagnosis; chronic fatigue, injury, post-viral illness, menopause, and emotional or social stress can all contribute. The chapter's limitation is explicit: the science of long Covid was still young when the book was written.

### “Have you turned it on and off again?”

The brain has no administrative root access over every body function. Goosebumps, arousal, fat storage, sleep, and dreams demonstrate that genes, reflexes, and organs can override conscious intention. Memory capacity is immense but memory is not a video: the brain stores salient fragments and reconstructs the rest, so recall can create false memories and become less accurate each time it is revisited. Legal cases involving pressured false memories illustrate why confidence is not proof.

## BECOMING BRAIN EFFICIENT

### Access restrictions

Reflexes are default pathways that can be useful for an ancestor and awkward for a modern human; goosebumps are the example. The brain manages some functions indirectly and cannot simply switch into developer mode. This is a good reason to treat bodily sensations as outputs of many systems rather than commands that can always be consciously overridden.

### Storage space and corrupt files

Rajan compares neural connections with computer storage but warns that the analogy is speculative because science does not know what one memory physically is. The brain can feel “full” while still having enormous capacity, yet recall slows with age and disease and irrelevant information can crowd access. The practical implication is to build retrieval and learning habits, not to assume memory is a literal hard drive.

### False memories

Memory is reconstructed from snapshots and generalization, not replayed as a faithful recording. Recalling an event means recalling the last reconstruction of it, so the memory can be altered through the process of retrieval. The book uses the image of a long game of telephone to explain why detail, confidence, and accuracy can diverge.

### Background music benefits

Multitasking is the wrong default for demanding work: switching between tasks raises errors, reduces efficiency, and rapidly uses oxygenated glucose in the prefrontal cortex and striatum. Rajan cites a small Sussex MRI study associating text-and-TV multitasking with reduced anterior cingulate density, while not presenting it as final proof. Low-key ambient music is a narrow exception because it can occupy unconscious attention and mask distracting environmental sounds. The distinction is between one focused task with a predictable buffer and simultaneous task switching.

### Rhythmic thinking

The ultradian rhythm is a roughly 90-minute cycle of rising and falling alertness within the day. Rajan proposes intense focus for up to 90 minutes followed by about 20 minutes of recovery, matching work to the body's ebb and flow instead of demanding constant peak output. The exact timing is not a rigid prescription; it is a way to notice that performance and recovery are cyclical.

## EXTENDING THE WARRANTY

### Switch on

Automation can become a cognitive crutch: navigation, arithmetic, and automatic gear changes remove opportunities to exercise memory and orientation. Learning a language or instrument recruits memory, motor coordination, attention, and hearing, and the book cites research associating musicians and bilingualism with lower dementia risk. The mechanism is use-dependent plasticity, not a guarantee against disease.

### Get ahead

The brain responds to practice and movement throughout life. Aerobic exercise improves blood flow and nerve-growth support and can increase hippocampal volume, which Rajan links to memory. Plasticity means an older brain can still learn, but inactivity allows function to atrophy.

### Find your tribe

Social isolation deprives the brain of mood support and cognitive stimulation. Contact with friends and family exercises memory, attention, and focus as well as emotional health. Social connection is therefore treated as a form of brain maintenance, not merely a pleasant extra.

### Rebel against retirement

Retirement can remove mental and social demands, lowering cognitive reserve. Rajan's point is not that rest is bad but that permanent disengagement deprives the brain of stimulus. Continuing to learn, contribute, and relate to people is the protective pattern.

### Relax

Short-term stress hormones can sharpen action, but chronic cortisol, adrenaline, and noradrenaline can damage attention, planning, emotional regulation, and the prefrontal cortex while enlarging the amygdala's fear response. Rajan describes his own insomnia and palpitations during an exhausting respiratory-ward year, then credits sleep, exercise, breathing, and reduced micro-stress with improvement. He also describes the mammalian diving reflex: cold water on the face while holding the breath activates trigeminal and vagal pathways, slows the heart, constricts limb vessels, and can reduce anxiety. This is a physiological reset, not a replacement for treating serious stress or panic.

## THE PAIN GAME

### Understanding pain

The brain itself lacks pain receptors, which permits awake brain surgery, but the meninges, vessels, nerves, and neck muscles can hurt and produce headache. Pain is meant to change behavior, yet chronic pain can remain switched on after the original threat. Rajan's example of phantom rectum after pan-proctocolectomy shows that an urge can persist even when the removed organ is gone.

### Name the pain

Pain education can provide indirect analgesic support because understanding the likely source and treatment makes the experience less mysterious. Rajan does not claim that knowledge erases pain; he claims that an intelligible model helps patients cope and choose appropriate care.

### Theater of pain

Pain is a brain-produced experience assembled from sensory input, memory, culture, explanations, emotion, and context. Hyperalgesia is an exaggerated response; sensitization describes a system that becomes more reactive, while referred pain shows that the brain can mislocate the source. Nociceptors detect potentially damaging stimuli, but there is no single “pain signal” that arrives as a finished sensation. This is why attention and expectation can influence acute pain without making the pain unreal.

### Fine-tuning the “pain signal”

Descending pain modulation allows the brain to turn nociceptive input up or down. Athletes may briefly dampen pain through intense focus, and placebos can produce analgesic effects through expectation and physiology. Looking away during a blood draw reduces an anticipated acute stimulus, but Rajan explicitly limits the trick: it does not reliably solve chronic pain or severe gallstone and abscess pain.

## KILLER INTELLIGENCE

The brain's intelligence also creates modern mismatches: cognitive biases, motion sickness, obesity, superstition, and workplace stress reflect circuitry adapted to a different environment. Rajan argues that cultural change outpaced biological adaptation, so modern life can trigger old threat systems through bosses, mortgages, screens, and constant alerts. Brain power also enlarged the skull and made childbirth more hazardous, a reminder that capability can carry a reproductive cost.

### Brain 2.0 (release date delayed)

Perception is a simulation built from incomplete sensory data. Menthol activates cold-sensitive TRPM8 proteins without lowering temperature, and “wetness” is assembled from cold, pressure, texture, and learned associations because humans have no dedicated wetness receptor. The brain predicts what will happen before conscious awareness, such as moving away from a thrown ball, but its models can be wrong. Artificial intelligence is presented as a possible future supplement, not evidence that the current brain is simple.

## Worked Example

Rajan's nightclub assault patient had a fixed pupil, altered breathing, low pulse, and high blood pressure before imaging revealed an extradural hematoma. The value was not a single symptom but the pattern: raised intracranial pressure can compress the brain and become fatal, so the response must be urgent. This is the chapter's clearest example of systems thinking plus escalation.

## Key Takeaways

1. The brain is powerful, plastic, energy-hungry, and imperfect.
2. Protect it from repeated injury and support it with movement, learning, sleep, and social contact.
3. Perception and pain are constructed from signals plus context; that explains modulation, not dismissal.
4. Chronic stress and multitasking are measurable costs, not badges of productivity.
