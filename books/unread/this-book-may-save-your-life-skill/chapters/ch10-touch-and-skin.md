# Chapter 10: What a Feeling

## Core Idea

Rajan calls touch the body’s largest and most intimate sense because receptors are distributed from the fingertips to the genitals and constantly report pressure, temperature, texture, and injury. Touch is also limited: unlike sight, hearing, or smell, it generally cannot warn about a distant threat before contact. Skin is a living barrier that prevents water loss, blocks radiation and microbes, houses immune cells, and renews itself through keratin-producing cells. Its surface is an ecosystem, so aggressive cleansing can remove protective oils and disturb microbial relationships even while ordinary hygiene remains important. The practical message is a simple barrier routine—cleanse gently, moisturize, use sunscreen, reduce avoidable irritation, and seek targeted care for persistent disease—combined with exercise, sleep, social contact, learning, and awe for brain maintenance.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Touch as local intelligence**: The skin monitors the body’s immediate physical environment but is not an early-warning radar for everything.
- **Barrier plus ecosystem**: Skin health depends on the physical stratum corneum, immune cells, oils, and microbial diversity together.
- **Simple routine, specific treatment**: Basic care is usually enough for ordinary skin; severe acne, eczema, rosacea, or changing lesions need diagnosis.

## HOW TOUCH CAN TAKE YOU OUT

Touch reaches nearly every part of the body and can produce comfort, pain, intimacy, disgust, or warning depending on which receptors are activated. Rajan calls it stronger than the concentrated receptors of the other senses because it investigates the body from the inside edge of the outside world. Its weakness is that contact is required: a dangerous object, heat, or chemical may already be on the skin before the nervous system can respond. Touch is therefore both a frontline defense and a record of exposure.

### Can’t touch this

The sensation of touching an object is not literal solid-to-solid contact at the atomic scale. Electrons in the atoms of the fingertips repel electrons in the object, and receptors report the resulting force and deformation to the brain. Rajan uses this physics joke to show that touch is an interpretation of an electromagnetic interaction. The tactile experience is real and useful even though the microscopic mechanism is less intuitive than the everyday word “touch.”

## SKIN IN THE GAME

Skin is the largest organ by surface area and is described as a border guard between the body and its environment. Without it, water would escape, sunlight would penetrate deeper tissue, and organisms would enter more easily. Its outer cells sacrifice themselves to radiation and abrasion so living cells below can remain protected. The barrier is not inert wrapping: it regulates temperature, senses the world, and coordinates inflammation.

### Human armor

Skin can weigh about 10 kilograms and account for around 15 percent of body weight. The epidermis is replenished by stem-cell-derived keratinocytes that rise toward the surface, become a waterproof layer, and eventually flake away; excessive rubbing can produce calluses. The dermis below contains blood vessels and immune cells, explaining both the bleeding from a shallow cut and the inflammation that follows injury. Collagen and elastin give skin shape and recoil, but smoking, poor sleep, and stress can accelerate their degradation and visible aging.

Rajan also introduces the cutaneous microbiome, which includes more than a thousand bacterial varieties living in different niches. Skin health is therefore a joint property of barrier cells, oils, immune surveillance, and microbial competition. A visible problem may be the result of a disrupted ecosystem rather than a lack of cleanliness.

### Skinned alive

Research on topical probiotics and microbiota transfer is presented as an emerging possibility for acne, psoriasis, eczema, or odor. Rajan recounts a 2017 twin case in which armpit microbes from the twin without body odor were transplanted to the twin with odor, illustrating the concept rather than establishing a general treatment. Skin microbes help train the immune system to tolerate harmless organisms while responding to genuine threats. Diversity is useful, but the book stresses that the science is young and strain-specific.

Overwashing can strip sebum, the oil that lubricates skin and feeds some resident microbes, creating dysbiosis that may worsen barrier problems. Demodex mites are another example of organisms that can live harmlessly on facial skin and eyelashes while causing disease if overabundant. Their unsettling biology is memorable, but the practical point is balance, not a mandate to sterilize the face. Skin is not improved by eliminating every organism that can be named.

### About body odor

Odor can be a by-product of protective microbial chemistry. Rajan describes Bacillus subtilis consuming sweat and oils and producing antifungal compounds, while Brevibacteria on the feet produce sour or cheesy smells as they metabolize dead skin. The same bacterial chemistry can be unpleasant in one context and useful or desirable in another, as with cheese-making. Body odor is thus a signal of an ecosystem, not proof of poor character or dirty skin.

### The balance of power

The skin is dry, acidic, and nutrient-poor, but hair follicles and oily areas provide niches where microbes can compete. Staphylococcus epidermidis may help restrain Staphylococcus aureus, while some strains of Cutibacterium acnes can inhibit pathogens and others contribute to acne. Environment, hormones, washing, products, and lifestyle can shift the balance. Rajan cautions that researchers are still learning which strains are friends or foes, so confident universal probiotic claims run ahead of the evidence.

### Squeaky clean

Hygiene protects against infection, but elaborate cleansing rituals can overcorrect by removing the oils and organisms that support the barrier. Rajan’s own move from almost no skincare to a more deliberate routine motivates a middle path: take the skin seriously without treating every marketing claim as necessary. The distinction is between removing dirt, irritants, and pathogens when needed and repeatedly scrubbing healthy tissue until it is dry and inflamed. Clean is not the same as sterile.

### Save yourself

Rajan’s basic routine is regular gentle washing, moisturizer, and sunscreen every day, including when the sky is cloudy and for darker skin. He also advises keeping a phone away from the bathroom because flushing can aerosolize microscopic droplets, and reducing face touching can reduce transfer from hands to facial skin. Severe acne, eczema, rosacea, or other persistent problems should be reviewed by a clinician or dermatologist. The routine is a low-cost default, not a substitute for treatment.

### What the doctor ordered

Cleansers use surfactants that bind oil and water into micelles so residue can be rinsed away; ordinary soap can strip too much oil, and abrasive plastic-bead scrubs can injure skin and the environment. Moisturizers retain water and improve barrier function, especially when eczema is driven by dryness. Occlusives reduce water loss and humectants draw water toward the surface, so a product may use both mechanisms. SPF 30 is described as blocking about 97 percent of UVB, but sunscreen is one part of broader sun protection.

### Save yourself

Rajan lists salicylic acid for exfoliation and oily skin, alpha-hydroxy acids and benzoyl peroxide for acne-related processes, and retinoids for cell turnover, acne, and some aging changes. These ingredients vary in strength and can irritate or increase sun sensitivity; retinoids in particular should be used according to prescription or professional guidance. Patch testing, label directions, and attention to an individual’s skin condition matter more than collecting every active ingredient. A simple routine that is tolerated consistently beats a complicated one that damages the barrier.

### The arrector pili muscle

This anatomy pause points to the tiny muscles attached to hair follicles. Their contraction produces goosebumps, a leftover mechanism that can raise fur in hairier ancestors and is now more visible as an emotional or temperature response than as useful insulation.

## Health Hack

Rajan’s brain-maintenance list is exercise, sleep, social contact, learning a language or instrument, and experiences of wonder or awe. Exercise supports blood flow, sleep helps clear accumulated proteins, social interaction keeps cognition engaged, and novel learning builds demand on neural circuits. The book does not claim that these steps guarantee prevention of neurodegeneration; it presents them as low-cost habits that support reserve and function across aging. They also reinforce the whole-body maintenance model introduced in the opening chapter.

## Worked Example

The twin body-odor case illustrates why a skin microbiome is more than a metaphor. One twin’s armpit bacteria were transferred to the other in an attempt to change odor, suggesting that microbial composition can influence a very visible trait. It is only a case study, so it cannot establish a safe or general-purpose transplant procedure. Rajan uses it to make a broader point: future dermatology may manipulate ecosystems, but current care should remain evidence-led and barrier-conscious.

## Key Takeaways

1. Skin combines a physical barrier, immune tissue, sensory receptors, oils, and a microbial ecosystem.
2. Ordinary care is gentle cleansing, moisturization, and daily sun protection; more products are not automatically better.
3. Overwashing, smoking, poor sleep, stress, and abrasive routines can undermine the barrier.
4. Persistent or severe skin disease belongs with a clinician; emerging microbiome treatments remain provisional.
