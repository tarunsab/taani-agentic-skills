# Chapter 13: Eleven — Surrogates

**Original section**: Eleven: Surrogates

## Core Idea

John McCain gives Lewis a way to study political courage from inside the official campaign without the normal protective shell. The two senators are used as surrogates for one another because both suffered war injuries, yet McCain has assimilated his trauma into an ethic of candor and forgiveness while Dole's campaign turns his wound into a packaged national identity. McCain plans to attach campaign-finance reform to legislation, forcing senators who praise reform while protecting big money to expose their contradiction. At the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, however, the conversation turns away from tactics toward the moral work of surviving war, apologizing, and refusing revenge. David Ifshin's account of his reconciliation with McCain reverses the usual journalistic search for dirt: each man makes the other look noble, and the differing details deepen rather than destroy the story. Clinton's repeated calls to the dying Ifshin and his attention to the children at the White House reveal a humane capacity that the campaign's suspicious atmosphere makes easy to ignore. The chapter's connection is that political character is not a slogan but a pattern of choices made when no immediate electoral benefit is available. Its limit is that private generosity and bravery do not dissolve policy disagreement or the possibility of later political compromise.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Surrogate identity**: Use when a candidate borrows another person's experience, credibility, or emotional authority to fill a gap in his own public image.
- **Trauma assimilated versus packaged**: Ask whether a person has interpreted an experience for himself or merely converted it into a campaign asset.
- **Forgiveness as political courage**: Use when the desire for revenge would be easier to understand publicly than the choice to release it privately.

## Key Concepts

- **Surrogate**: A person who speaks for a candidate or embodies a shared story on the candidate's behalf.
- **Campaign-finance reform**: McCain's substantive challenge to the money system that campaign rhetoric usually treats abstractly.
- **Vietnam Veterans Memorial**: A site where private memory and public political identity meet.
- **Fertile fallacy**: George Soros's idea for a mistake in framing that opens a better line of inquiry.
- **Reconciliation**: The reciprocal apology and friendship between McCain and Ifshin.
- **Political humanity**: The capacity to act with care when the gesture has no strategic payoff.

## Mental Models

- Ask whether a public story is **being lived or being deployed**.
- Compare the subject's conduct when **unobserved** with the conduct that earns a camera or a vote.
- In a conflict, look for **reciprocal moral recognition**, not only adversarial testimony.

## Anti-patterns

- **Reducing a war wound to a credential**: The experience may explain character, but it cannot substitute for present choices.
- **Assuming forgiveness erases responsibility**: McCain and Ifshin still name and interpret the wrong they did.
- **Treating any access as a license to expose**: McCain's warning about Ifshin's family limits what a journalist should turn into a story.

## Worked Example

Ifshin visits McCain to apologize for broadcasting antiwar propaganda into the prison where McCain was held. McCain accepts the apology and tells him not to spend his life looking backward. Years later, after McCain has attacked Ifshin's war record in a campaign speech, the two meet again; McCain apologizes, then publicly enters the Congressional Record to call Ifshin his friend. When Ifshin is dying, McCain worries that a harsher response would have left him with a different memory of himself. The reciprocal account matters because each man supplies the context that makes the other's act intelligible.

## Key Takeaways

1. A surrogate can expose the difference between a candidate's lived character and packaged biography.
2. Courage in politics includes making choices that do not improve the next poll.
3. The best evidence of forgiveness is changed conduct, not a declaration.
4. Intimate reporting requires boundaries around the people who bear the cost of the story.
