# Chapter 10: Eight — The Mouth of the Old South

**Original section**: Eight: The Mouth of the Old South

## Core Idea

After New Hampshire, Buchanan's campaign moves from personal retail politics to a national media operation, and both the press and the campaign struggle with the transition. Journalists now depend on one candidate for planes, rooms, and movement, while Buchanan's young staff cannot reliably manage the infrastructure that his new visibility requires. Lewis nevertheless grows attached to Buchanan because the candidate speaks plainly and has been reduced by critics to a caricature; the affection is evidence of proximity, not an endorsement of Buchanan's program. In South Carolina, the campaign's nostalgia attaches itself to the Citadel's reverence for discipline, tradition, and a past that outsiders condemn. Keyes's hunger strike, the cadets' enthusiasm, and the closed gates show different forms of outsider energy colliding with institutions whose instinct is self-preservation. Dole wins the state when authorities, party leaders, and economic interests rally behind order, demonstrating why trained followers make poor rebels. McCain's bluntness provides a counterexample: he can inhabit public office without the usual evasions, but his candor is itself a political risk. The chapter's mechanism is that passion can open a breach, yet institutions usually close it through authority, logistics, and the promise of normality.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Emotional geography**: Map a movement by anger, nostalgia, hope, or apathy rather than by region alone.
- **Institutional self-preservation**: Use when an institution appears to support rebellion; ask when order, hierarchy, or material interest will close its gates.
- **Nationalization cost**: A successful local insurgency needs infrastructure capable of serving media, supporters, and the candidate at once.

## Key Concepts

- **Nostalgia salesman**: Buchanan's ability to make a lost past feel like a recoverable political program.
- **Peasants with pitchforks**: Buchanan's self-conscious image for energized followers.
- **Media migration**: The shift from journalists finding candidates in one region to journalists attaching themselves to a single national campaign.
- **Closed gates**: The Citadel's literal and institutional defense against a disruptive political message.
- **Order and discipline**: The authorities' stronger allegiance than the cadets' temporary passion.
- **Candor risk**: McCain's willingness to speak plainly despite the career cost.

## Mental Models

- Use **local emotion plus national infrastructure** as a two-part test for an insurgent movement.
- Ask which group is **temporarily passionate** and which group controls access, jobs, and rules.
- Distinguish **sympathy for a person** from agreement with the person's politics; both can coexist in a report.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating a dramatic rally as a national organization**: Buchanan's rented plane is ready before his hotel and staff systems are.
- **Assuming tradition is automatically resistance**: The Citadel's reverence for the past ultimately reinforces authority.
- **Using symbolic solidarity without coalition discipline**: Keyes's protest gains attention but not durable influence.

## Worked Example

At the Citadel, roughly two hundred cadets skip class to hear Buchanan, while the school locks its gates for the first time in years. A comedian from *Saturday Night Live* needles the cadets about missing class, and the scene turns into a farce before Buchanan even arrives. The cadets care about his defense of their tradition, not necessarily his trade or abortion positions. Once the event ends, they can cheer through the bars, but the institution and its economic relationships remain intact. Passion has created a vivid image; authority still controls the campus.

## Key Takeaways

1. A movement's emotional force and its logistical capacity are different resources.
2. Institutions often absorb or outlast temporary rebellion through hierarchy and material incentives.
3. A candidate can be unfairly caricatured and still deserve critical scrutiny.
4. Candor is politically valuable partly because it is costly.
