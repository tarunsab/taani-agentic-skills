# Chapter 19: Seventeen — Chicago

**Original section**: Seventeen: Chicago

## Core Idea

The Green Party convention in Los Angeles mirrors the Taxpayers convention: left and right activists share intensity, merchandise, and contempt for the apathetic center even while disagreeing about policy. Ralph Nader uses the media to attack the two-party duopoly's corporate dependence and argues that politics has been trivialized away from questions of ownership, power, access, and voice. The Democratic convention then shows why the major parties are less interesting on television: they suppress intraparty conflict, read speeches from TelePrompTers, and substitute carefully managed grief for difficult economic arguments. Jesse Jackson briefly breaks the pattern by shutting off the prompts and speaking about the jobs, jail, and welfare canyon around the United Center. Donors occupy skyboxes, poor workers clean away untouched food for four dollars an hour, and party organization appears as a social hierarchy as much as a democratic gathering. John McCain's role as Dole's surrogate again reveals how a politician can be useful precisely by speaking with a degree of independence, while the Dick Morris scandal creates relief because an uncontrolled event punctures the stream of managed messages. Taylor's campaign manifesto and rented grizzly bears complete the comic parallel: the loser is trying to convert a political performance into a business identity. The chapter's lesson is that conflict creates information and television interest, while unity without substance produces a polished vacuum.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Conflict as information**: Use when an organization suppresses disagreement; ask what the conflict would reveal about power, money, or values.
- **TelePrompTer politics**: Use when public speech is optimized for delivery and broadcast rather than for thought under pressure.
- **Skybox democracy**: Map who pays, who speaks, who watches, and who performs the invisible labor of the event.

## Key Concepts

- **Green Party**: The left-wing outsider organization supporting Nader in 1996.
- **Two-party duopoly**: The corporate convergence Nader criticizes.
- **Protective imitation**: The safe copying that makes the major parties similar.
- **TelePrompTer**: The device that permits a speaker to simulate direct thought while reading a prepared text.
- **Skybox**: A privileged convention space for wealthy donors and corporate sponsors.
- **Dick Morris scandal**: The revelation that Clinton's strategist let a prostitute hear confidential calls, disrupting controlled politics.

## Mental Models

- Compare a convention's **sound level** with its **information level**; noise and passion are not identical.
- Use **camera, crowd, donor, worker** as four vantage points on the same political event.
- Treat an uncontrolled scandal as a **stress test** of an organization's moral and media response.

## Anti-patterns

- **Equating unity with health**: A unified platform can be the result of suppressing real disagreements.
- **Using tragedy as a universal substitute for policy**: Repeated grief eventually loses the capacity to clarify action.
- **Mistaking celebrity access for political influence**: A donor's proximity or a surrogate's jokes may not change the underlying decision.

## Worked Example

When Dick Gephardt speaks, four TelePrompTers let him rotate smoothly while delivering a speech that claims to offer a vision rather than a television show. Jesse Jackson later shuts the prompts off and describes two ballparks and a jail separated by a canyon of lost industry, welfare, and despair. His metaphors are messy, but the crowd responds because the speech connects the convention site to a social problem the party has not addressed. The contrast shows why risk in language can create more political information than flawless delivery. It also explains why the networks keep searching for outsiders and scandals inside a convention designed to eliminate both.

## Key Takeaways

1. Suppressed intraparty conflict reduces both public information and media interest.
2. A speaker becomes more credible when the words visibly have to be found.
3. Follow money and invisible labor to see who the convention actually serves.
4. An authentic scandal can be more informative than months of official messaging.
