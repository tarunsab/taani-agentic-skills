# Chapter 2: Prelude — The People

**Original section**: Prelude: The People

## Core Idea

Morry Taylor enters presidential politics because a factory worker, Moses, asks why the man who fixed a company cannot try to fix the country. Taylor is not a prepared political networker; he is a self-made tool-and-die maker who built Titan Wheel by buying distressed factories, saving jobs, and keeping practical knowledge of production. He reads the claim that 70 percent of Americans hate Washington as an untapped market, just as he would read a market for farm wheels. His first test comes at Ross Perot's United We Stand gathering, where a failed TelePrompTer forces him to speak without the polish he imagined politics required. The delegates respond to the unplanned, uncredentialed businessman more strongly than to several professional candidates, turning their enthusiasm into an organization before Taylor has made a campaign. The episode establishes both the appeal and the limit of outsider politics: authenticity can recruit people quickly, but the crowd's recognition is not the same as a viable electoral path.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Outsider legitimacy**: A person can enter politics with no résumé in politics when practical achievement and independence supply a different kind of authority.
  - When to use: When institutions are distrusted and professional credentials have become liabilities.
  - How: Translate a concrete record of solving problems into a public claim, then let supporters organize around the gap.
- **The People as a political market**: Perot's delegates are not a passive audience but a pool of committed participants looking for a vehicle.
  - When to use: When a movement's energy is stronger than its formal leadership.
  - How: Observe whom the participants choose when several candidates compete for them.

## Key Concepts

- **Empty refrigerator**: Taylor's self-description of having no political debts, memberships, or secrets.
- **Self-made man**: In this chapter, someone whose wealth comes from actual work and company-building rather than inheritance.
- **United We Stand**: Ross Perot's gathering of delegates and would-be leaders.
- **The People**: Perot's name for the movement's delegates.
- **Untapped market**: Taylor's business metaphor for citizens who dislike Washington.
- **Political organization**: The volunteers, ballots, and local energy that the delegates create for him.

## Mental Models

- Think of outsider entry as **supply meeting latent demand**: a movement may already exist before it knows its candidate.
- Use the **failure-of-script test**: when the prepared apparatus breaks, what remains may be closer to the speaker's actual capacity.
- Distinguish **recognition, organization, and votes**; each is a different conversion step.

## Anti-patterns

- **Equating a good business record with governing competence**: Taylor's factory success explains his appeal but does not settle national policy questions.
- **Assuming a receptive audience guarantees victory**: The Dallas response creates volunteers, not a nomination.
- **Borrowing professional political mannerisms**: Taylor's lack of thumb-wrestling gestures is part of why the delegates can see him as different.

## Worked Example

At the Dallas event Taylor expects a few eccentric supporters, so he leaves after his speech to eat dinner. His staff calls him back because roughly one thousand of the four thousand delegates are crowding the campaign suite, even while Perot is giving the closing speech. The volunteers have consumed the food, signed up to work, and begun treating Taylor as Perot's successor. Taylor did not manufacture this response through a polished message; his ordinary businessman identity and lack of political choreography supplied the contrast. The scene becomes the campaign's founding resource, even though Taylor himself is still trying to understand what happened.

## Key Takeaways

1. A credible outsider may be discovered by a movement rather than launched by a résumé.
2. Concrete competence can be politically powerful when professional politics is distrusted.
3. Let the audience's behavior, not the candidate's self-description, reveal whether a movement is real.
4. Build a bridge from enthusiasm to votes before assuming the bridge exists.
