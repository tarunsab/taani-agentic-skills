# Chapter 27: Afterword — To the Vintage Edition

**Original section**: Afterword: To the Vintage Edition

## Core Idea

Lewis explains that the book was always meant to be called *Losers*, but commercial gatekeepers renamed it *Trail Fever* because “losers” was judged insufficiently upbeat. The retrospective title story reinforces his claim that the defeated candidates and their passions were more revealing than the winners' managed campaign. He then identifies the larger problem the book tried to solve: political journalism had retreated to the bleachers, where reporters could attack politicians without accepting the risks of intimacy or understanding. Lewis compares this absence with business journalism's willingness to let Carol Loomis explain Warren Buffett's point of view, and with older examples of John Hersey on Harry Truman and Ben Bradlee on John Kennedy. Sidney Blumenthal's sympathetic reporting on the Clintons shows both the value of access and the professional penalty for appearing to like a powerful politician. Lewis admits that his own access to John McCain changed his work into friendship, and that admiration can be a condition of understanding as well as a source of bias. The afterword's practical conclusion is not that journalists should become uncritical friends, but that they should disclose the cost of distance and the cost of access instead of pretending that hostility is neutral. Its limitation is self-reflexive: Lewis is defending his own method and may understate how genuine access can be manipulated by a subject.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Bleachers vs. inside the skin**: Distance makes attack safer but can make character illegible; intimacy adds understanding while creating obligations and bias.
- **Sympathy-for-access exchange**: A subject grants entry partly because the journalist promises not to begin with destruction, and the resulting account must acknowledge that bargain.
- **Access audit**: Before trusting a profile, ask who granted access, what the subject could withhold, what the journalist came to feel, and what the reader can independently check.
- **Friendship as stopping condition**: Once reporting becomes a relationship of mutual loyalty, the journalist may no longer be the right person to continue interpreting that subject.

## Key Concepts

- **Title politics**: The commercial preference for an upbeat name mirrors the campaign culture's suspicion of failure and negative space.
- **Bleacher journalism**: A form of political reporting that preserves critical distance by treating politicians as targets rather than people.
- **Carol Loomis model**: Business journalism's accepted practice of explaining how a powerful subject sees the world, even when the account is favorable.
- **Sidney Blumenthal case**: A demonstration that sympathetic access can produce valuable reporting and still be condemned as non-objective.
- **McCain access**: Open schedules, family settings, and unobserved acts allow Lewis to observe character beyond official performance.
- **Friends and Family**: The passenger-list label that reveals Lewis has crossed from observer into subject's circle.

## Mental Models

- Objectivity is not the absence of feeling; it is the disciplined disclosure and testing of how feeling entered the work.
- Evaluate any profile on two axes: **quality of access** and **quality of resistance to the subject's preferred story**.
- When a source becomes a friend, decide explicitly whether the work has changed from reporting to memoir, advocacy, or farewell.

## Anti-patterns

- **Hostility as neutrality**: Treating suspicion as inherently more objective than admiration.
- **Access worship**: Assuming proximity guarantees truth without asking what the subject wants the journalist to see.
- **Hidden bargain**: Presenting a sympathetic account as if it emerged without a relationship, invitation, or emotional cost.

## Worked Example

Lewis is kept away from Dole and Clinton by handlers, so he turns to candidates and politicians who will let him observe them, especially Morry Taylor and John McCain. McCain permits long days, home visits, and a glimpse of private habits such as visiting Mo Udall, giving Lewis evidence that a hostile profile would miss. Other reporters receive the same invitation, and many feel embarrassed that an approving impression might make them look naïve. Lewis eventually admits that his admiration has made him McCain's friend, which is why he is no longer fully available to write about him. The passenger list marking him “Friends and Family” supplies the material proof that access has changed the journalist's role.

## Key Takeaways

1. Political understanding requires proximity, but proximity creates obligations and bias.
2. A favorable account is not automatically corrupt; an undisclosed bargain is the larger problem.
3. The best access stories explain both what the subject revealed and what the relationship prevented the journalist from seeing.
4. Journalists should choose the form—profile, memoir, advocacy, or farewell—that matches the relationship they actually have.
