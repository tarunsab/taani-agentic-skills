# Chapter 22: Twenty — Experiments in Higher Living

**Original section**: Twenty: Experiments in Higher Living

## Core Idea

Jack Kemp is one of the few major figures willing to let a reporter near him after the primaries, partly because a running mate on a losing ticket has little left to protect. Kemp's political style is built from risk, enthusiasm, football metaphors, and an optimistic supply-side doctrine that promises growth to solve every problem. Lewis distinguishes the force of Kemp's conviction from the evidence for his economics: Kemp treats the Reagan years as confirmation and cannot name a result that would change his mind about lower rates producing more revenue. Football gives Kemp a language for race, business, leadership, and the American Dream, but those analogies allow audiences to import experience he does not actually possess. Lewis's Handycam produces a different kind of access: merely pointing it at staffers, spinners, and a plate of shrimp makes them behave as if a public audience is watching. The camera reveals how much “truth” in politics is a reaction to the imagined viewer rather than a property of the speaker. The final Kemp–Gore exchange, in which Gore praises an opponent's leadership instead of using the rebuttal to score, offers a small experiment in political conduct that breaks the campaign's habitual rivalry.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Risk as access**: A politician who allows unscripted moments can become more knowable, but openness does not guarantee intellectual correctness.
- **Metaphoric transference**: Audiences transfer competence from a familiar domain—football, business, combat—to policy domains where the analogy may not hold.
- **Falsifiability test for conviction**: Ask what evidence would change a candidate's mind. If the answer is “none,” the idea functions as faith rather than analysis.
- **Camera force field**: A recording device changes the behavior it is supposed to document by invoking an imagined public and professional consequences.

## Key Concepts

- **Kemp's football politics**: A physical, locker-room vocabulary used to make taxes, race, and leadership feel immediate.
- **Supply-side faith**: The belief that lower tax rates release enough growth to increase government revenue, held independently of disconfirming evidence.
- **Press pool**: The group of reporters assigned to follow a low-priority campaign figure, often revealing how news value is allocated.
- **The Laffer curve**: Kemp's recurring model for explaining a revenue-maximizing tax rate, even when the location of that point is not demonstrated.
- **SteadyShot force field**: Lewis's name for the social pressure created by a visible television camera.
- **Leadership by compliment**: Gore's refusal to turn a moral question into a partisan attack, briefly restoring disagreement without contempt.

## Mental Models

- Separate **energy**, **novelty**, and **truth**; a speaker can be compelling without offering a testable account.
- When an analogy carries an argument, inspect which experience is being transferred and which relevant experience is missing.
- Treat observation as an intervention: ask what the camera, journalist, or audience caused people to do.

## Anti-patterns

- **Enthusiasm as proof**: Mistaking Kemp's sincerity and salesmanship for empirical support for his tax claims.
- **Five-minute access theater**: Calling a tightly timed interview “access” when the format prevents understanding.
- **Camera naïveté**: Assuming recorded behavior is automatically more authentic than unrecorded behavior.

## Worked Example

When Lewis asks Kemp what evidence would dissuade him from believing that lower rates increase revenue, Kemp answers by reinterpreting the Reagan years as proof and moves on to the point where the Laffer curve bends backward. The response reveals a closed belief system more clearly than a conventional policy speech would. On the Dole plane, staffers who normally ignore Lewis become intensely attentive when he raises a camera, even trying to hide a plate of shrimp. In the later debate, Gore's praise of Kemp interrupts the same incentive structure: for once the rebuttal is used to recognize a useful opponent rather than to manufacture a wound. Together the scenes show that political behavior changes when the reward for performance changes.

## Key Takeaways

1. Openness can make a politician more legible without making the politician more right.
2. Ask for disconfirming evidence whenever a candidate presents faith as policy analysis.
3. Metaphors create competence by association; test the transfer before accepting it.
4. Small acts of generous disagreement can be more politically distinctive than another attack.
