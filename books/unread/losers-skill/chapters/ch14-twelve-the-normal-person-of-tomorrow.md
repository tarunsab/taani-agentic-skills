# Chapter 14: Twelve — The Normal Person of Tomorrow

**Original section**: Twelve: The Normal Person of Tomorrow

## Core Idea

Lewis makes the book's central political distinction explicit: the deep fault line is not Democrat versus Republican but Insider versus Outsider. Insiders share a broad, risk-averse settlement and compete through tactics; Outsiders are structurally minded agitators who reach for issues the system cannot safely absorb, such as trade and campaign finance. Ralph Nader supplies the left's version of the outsider: he accepts market incentives but targets monopolies, public goods, and failures of consumer information, while treating active citizens as the political equivalent of informed consumers. His claim that “the eccentric of today is the normal person of tomorrow” describes a civic identity that can be mocked before it becomes ordinary. The MTV Choose or Lose bus presents the opposite version of youth politics: a corporate, content-free invitation to vote that borrows the symbols of rebellion while avoiding any reason to choose. Clutch's pirate radio and willingness to infringe on a frequency provide a small counterexample because he wants direct cultural and political confrontation rather than a branded attitude. The chapter's lesson is that participation needs content, risk, and a reason to act; the limit is that outsider identity can be as self-dramatizing as the corporate politics it opposes.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Insiders and Outsiders**: Use when party labels obscure the more important difference between risk-averse system managers and structurally disruptive challengers.
- **Active consumer / active citizen**: Nader's parallel: market problems need informed consumers, while political problems need informed, participating voters.
- **Normal person of tomorrow**: Use to evaluate an eccentric or minority practice without assuming present convention is the final measure of normality.

## Key Concepts

- **Power Alley**: The White House corridor where physical proximity to the president signals access and status.
- **Two-party duopoly**: Nader's description of the major parties as a system of protective imitation.
- **Active citizen**: A person who accepts ridicule and inconvenience to further a public value.
- **Choose or Lose**: MTV's youth-vote campaign and its content-light appeal to participation.
- **Corporate risk aversion**: The market-friendly version of political caution that turns dissent into a brand.
- **Naked Radio**: Clutch's pirate station, which uses technical interference as direct cultural opposition.

## Mental Models

- Use **target plus temperament**: the system is the target; risk tolerance identifies the outsider.
- Ask whether an invitation to participate supplies **a cause, a constituency, or only a mood**.
- Treat eccentricity as **a time-indexed judgment**, not an objective defect.

## Anti-patterns

- **Equating independence with political thought**: A person can be “independent” because no position has been demanded of him.
- **Selling participation without a choice**: Registration and voting pledges are weak when the issues remain unnamed.
- **Assuming all outsiders are liberating**: Structural criticism can coexist with poor judgment or extremism.

## Worked Example

The MTV bus asks students to pledge that they will vote because they want to “rock the system,” but the form supplies no particular political reason. The bus is loud, expensive, and decorated with rock-star slogans, yet the students drift in and out with practiced indifference. Clutch stands nearby handing out flyers that criticize the corporation for treating them as consumers rather than citizens. He describes how pirate radio moves into a weak signal area and takes over the frequency, turning a listener's routine into an unexpected confrontation. The contrast is between branded rebellion and an actual willingness to risk a rule in order to deliver a message.

## Key Takeaways

1. The most important political divide may be temperament and system orientation, not party.
2. Participation becomes meaningful when people know what they are choosing.
3. New norms often begin as eccentric practices, but eccentricity alone is not evidence of wisdom.
4. Corporate engagement can reproduce the detachment it claims to overcome.
