# Chapter 17: Fifteen — Ghosts

**Original section**: Fifteen: Ghosts

## Core Idea

Morry Taylor's plan for a political manifesto brings Lewis together with Barbara Feinman, a professional ghostwriter who has moved from trusted access inside the White House to suspicion in the Whitewater scandal. Barbara's experience illustrates the cost of a Washington culture that welcomes writers who package insiders but distrusts anyone who enters a politician's point of view. Taylor wants the book to simplify politics for ordinary businesspeople and persuade more of them to run; Barbara wants to channel a subject who will not sit still long enough to write his own book. The project then expands into the wider campaign's machinery: Dole's state map turns uncertainty into strategic categories, and the moviegoing stunt turns moral judgment into a scene arranged before the experience occurs. Dole's invocation of Hollywood values is internally contradictory because it says both that good movies win at the box office and that bad movies profit from cultural wreckage. Russell, Kansas, supplies a grounded contrast: small-town consideration is real because everyone knows everyone, yet the actual town is economically withering while the campaign treats it as a timeless moral prop. Lewis's method is to compare the ghostwritten narrative with the lived place and the behavior of the people who inhabit it. The chapter's limit is that close observation can still turn people into characters, while a community's real virtues do not rescue its economy from distant forces.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Ghosting as channeling**: Use when a writer must reproduce a subject's point of view rather than merely summarize the subject's ideas.
  - How: Listen for repeated language, observe habits, and test the draft against the subject's conduct.
- **Strategic map of uncertainty**: Campaigns divide states into safe, contested, and “maybe” categories; the last category invites costly bluffing.
- **Stage-set morality**: Use when a campaign invokes a place or cultural product as evidence of values; compare the claim with the site's actual history.

## Key Concepts

- **Ghostwriter**: A writer who supplies a public voice while the named author receives the credit.
- **First-person singular**: Feinman's proposed form for preserving Taylor's force and idiom.
- **Whitewater suspicion**: The political environment that turns a trusted insider into a possible enemy.
- **Strategist's map**: A representation of what campaigns believe about state competitiveness, not necessarily what is true.
- **Independence Day stunt**: Dole's staged moviegoing used to authorize a later speech about Hollywood values.
- **Russell values**: A campaign story built from a real town's habits and a national candidate's youth.

## Mental Models

- Separate **voice ownership** from **idea ownership** when a public text is ghostwritten.
- Treat a campaign map as **belief about the electorate**, and test the belief against spending and movement.
- Compare **community norms** with **economic structure**; friendliness does not equal prosperity.

## Anti-patterns

- **Pretending a staged experience is discovery**: Seeing a movie after the speech is written reverses evidence and conclusion.
- **Using positive language to hide negative action**: A campaign can describe itself as nice while attacking through implication.
- **Romanticizing small-town life**: Mutual consideration can coexist with decline, dependence, and pressure to conform.

## Worked Example

The Dole campaign decides that its candidate must see *Independence Day* before speaking about Hollywood. The speech, however, is written before the screening, and the ticket purchase and cameras are arranged to make the event look ordinary. Afterward Dole's indistinct comments are reconstructed by reporters into a moral assessment assembled from fragments. The campaign has not learned what Dole thinks; it has produced a tableau in which he appears qualified to judge. Lewis then visits Russell and finds the same method at a larger scale: the campaign uses a living town as a backdrop for values that its own economic history complicates.

## Key Takeaways

1. A ghostwriter can reveal a subject only if the form preserves the subject's actual contradictions.
2. Strategic categories describe campaign beliefs and can become self-fulfilling expenditures.
3. A moral claim should follow observation, not be used to script the observation.
4. Community warmth is evidence of social practice, not proof that a place is economically healthy.
