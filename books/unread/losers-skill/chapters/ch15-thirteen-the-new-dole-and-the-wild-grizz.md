# Chapter 15: Thirteen — The New Dole and the Wild Grizz

**Original section**: Thirteen: The New Dole and the Wild Grizz

## Core Idea

The Dole campaign recognizes that its primary strategy—wait, react, and let rivals supply the ideas—cannot survive a general election against Clinton's massive advertising operation. Dole therefore resigns his Senate seat and presents himself as a private citizen, an apparently moving act that would make him an outsider. The handlers immediately drain the gesture of its meaning by revealing that surprise was part of the plan and that a novelist, Mark Helprin, wrote the speech to create “Bob Dole.” The campaign then tours across the country at frantic speed, staging crime rallies, moviegoing, and moral speeches whose backdrops substitute for contact with local reality. Dole's effort to become a challenger fails because he still cannot speak in his own voice or explain what distinguishes him from Clinton; the more he moves, the less he learns. Russell, Kansas, supplies a second test: its small-town consideration is real, but its economic decline contradicts the campaign's idealized “Russell values.” Taylor, meanwhile, turns his campaign training into a corporate video, discovering that he has learned to sell himself as the product and finally to deploy the grizzly-bear persona he had wanted all along. The chapter's mechanism is rebranding without transformation: a performance can alter the frame, but it cannot create the underlying character or policy.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Manufactured outsider**: Use when an insider sacrifices an office or changes scenery to appear outside the system; inspect who wrote the gesture and who benefits.
- **Frequent-flier politics**: Movement can substitute for advancement and shield a campaign from self-examination.
- **Stage-set values**: Use when a place is invoked as a moral ideal; compare the actual community's habits and economic history with the myth.
- **Self as product**: Taylor's transferable lesson: a campaign teaches a business leader how to perform a person, not merely a product.

## Key Concepts

- **The New Dole**: The campaign's rebranded challenger after Dole resigns the Senate.
- **A Better Man for a Better America**: Dole's general-election identity built around war experience and Russell.
- **Russell values**: The small-town virtues Dole invokes, partly real and partly staged.
- **Frequent-flier politics**: Frantic movement that permits little reflection or local learning.
- **Establishing shot**: The visual image a television story needs to tell viewers what an event is about.
- **Wild Grizz**: Taylor's decision to make his unfiltered, bear-like persona part of corporate communication.

## Mental Models

- Use the **author-of-the-gesture test**: who conceived, wrote, and controlled the supposedly spontaneous act?
- Compare **place as lived community** with place as campaign shorthand.
- Ask whether a new label changes **behavior under pressure**; if not, it is rebranding.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating resignation as independence**: Leaving office can be a publicity maneuver rather than an ideological break.
- **Creating crises for a backdrop**: A crime event or movie visit does not become relevant because a podium is placed there.
- **Mistaking speed for progress**: A campaign that never stops moving may be avoiding the question of why it exists.

## Worked Example

At a Memorial Day parade, Dole's resignation speech is presented as a brave break with office. The campaign manager later admits that the surprise was essential; if the news had leaked, Dole might not have done it. The speech was also written by an outside novelist because Dole's life story needed to be packaged for him. The act therefore contains a real sacrifice and a manufactured political effect at the same time. Lewis uses this duality to show how a campaign can turn an authentic decision into a managed image before the public has time to judge it.

## Key Takeaways

1. A change in role is not a change in temperament.
2. Test “values” against the actual economy and social practices of the place invoked.
3. Movement, props, and a good establishing shot cannot substitute for a reason to govern.
4. The transferable skill Taylor gains is controlled self-presentation, not policy wisdom.
