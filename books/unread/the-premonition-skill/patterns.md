# Patterns

## Build a useful abstraction, then interrogate it
**When to use**: The real system is too large or entangled to inspect directly.

**How**: Represent the relevant actors, contacts, delays, and state changes; run interventions; compare outputs; then gather data where the model shows leverage.

**Trade-offs**: Simplicity exposes mechanisms but hides unmodeled behavior; complexity can look authoritative while becoming hard to inspect.

The Glass model mattered because it made transmission visible as interactions among people rather than as an undifferentiated population. Its school-closure result did not prove the world behaved exactly like the model; it told Carter where to dig, leading him to school buses, classroom density, and children’s social behavior. Use the output to form a better question and a falsifiable field check.

## Layer partially effective defenses
**When to use**: No single action can stop the threat, or any one action has unacceptable cost.

**How**: Identify high-contact networks; combine isolation, distancing, closures, testing, communication, and redundancy; scale measures to severity and compliance; revisit the layers as facts change.

**Trade-offs**: More layers cost more socially and operationally; late layers can impose cost without recovering the lost transmission time.

TLC and Carter’s Swiss-cheese reasoning share one mechanism: align enough imperfect barriers that their holes do not align. School closure was one layer, not the whole strategy. The approach fails when infectiousness overwhelms the layers or people do not comply, and it requires a decision owner who can tune the response rather than wait for a universal recipe.

## Convert lagging signals into leading action
**When to use**: The clearest evidence arrives after the intervention window.

**How**: Work backward from deaths, hospitalizations, travel, excess flu-like visits, or other delayed indicators; estimate the unseen upstream state; act on a range; update.

**Trade-offs**: Proxies are noisy and can create false alarms, but waiting for a clean signal can guarantee late action.

Carter treated deaths as old starlight and cases as embers that might already have ignited. The Diamond Princess, the Mann Gulch fire, and the 2009 H1N1 record each supplied a different proxy for the same hidden process. The method is disciplined inference under delay, not clairvoyance in the mystical sense.

## Do redneck epidemiology
**When to use**: A decision is urgent, the data are incomplete, and formal analysis will arrive too late.

**How**: State the denominator and delay; choose several analogies; calculate orders of magnitude; expose assumptions; ask what action follows if the range is right; revise when new observations arrive.

**Trade-offs**: Rough numbers can be wrong, and analogy can smuggle in a false pattern; transparent uncertainty is still more useful than an unexamined point estimate.

Lawler used international travel to infer that Wuhan had far more than its reported cases. Carter compared SARS, H1N1, and the cruise ship, then used ICU outcomes to estimate lethality before all deaths had occurred. Both treated speed and actionability as part of rigor.

## Route around the official channel
**When to use**: Authority is centralized but information, tools, or courage are not moving through the chain.

**How**: Find high-value nodes; ask for the smallest actionable handoff; use a trusted peer network; preserve a record; bring the result back into an accountable operating channel when possible.

**Trade-offs**: Back channels can move faster and expose truth, but they can also create legal, political, or coordination risk.

The Wolverines, the Red Phone, and Charity’s relationships with local health officers all compensate for broken formal routes. Their advantage is proximity and trust; their danger is that work remains invisible, cannot scale, or is later disowned. Route around blockage to create action, not to evade responsibility.

## Treat near misses as system data
**When to use**: An institution records catastrophe but hides the errors that almost became catastrophe.

**How**: Make reporting safe; record the near miss; inspect the barrier that caught it; redesign the environment so the error is harder to repeat; share the learning where the work happens.

**Trade-offs**: Psychological safety conflicts with blame-seeking and political optics; a suggestion box without uptake is not a learning system.

Carter’s VA work began with the insight that aviation investigates near collisions while medicine often learns only after a patient dies. His failed Lessons Learned website taught him that people learn what they freely seek, not what a formal meeting imposes. Learning must reach the hallway conversation where the important, sometimes taboo information actually lives.

## Make the hidden expert operational
**When to use**: The person with the best situational knowledge is buried under hierarchy or excluded from the meeting.

**How**: Identify the L6; give them direct access to the decision maker; translate their tacit model into visible data; grant the authority and support required to act; keep credit secondary to use.

**Trade-offs**: Discovery can surface a brilliant but politically exposed person; direct access without a defined role can create personal dependence rather than institutional capacity.

Todd Park’s phrase captured Charity’s position relative to Governor Newsom. The model team’s job was to move what she knew to the governor’s desk, and the resulting forecast supported California’s statewide stay-at-home order. The deeper lesson is organizational: a system that needs excavation to find its operator is already losing time.

## Close the last mile
**When to use**: A tool, result, or supply exists but is not reaching the person who needs it.

**How**: Test approval, billing, certification, supplies, data format, staffing, and ownership; design the handoff; measure time-to-use, not just discovery.

**Trade-offs**: Operationalization is less glamorous than discovery and may require changing contracts or rules, but without it the breakthrough is only a paper.

The Biohub could return a test in hours, yet hospitals stayed with slow commercial labs, local offices lacked fax capacity, and the Strategic National Stockpile sent Q-tips instead of medical swabs. Genomic results were even more valuable because they revealed transmission links and safe spaces, but they needed local health officers to receive and use them. The last mile is where institutional incentives become visible.
