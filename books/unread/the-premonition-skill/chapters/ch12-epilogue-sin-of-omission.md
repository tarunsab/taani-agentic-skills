# Epilogue: The Sin of Omission

**Contents position**: Epilogue | **after Part III**

## Core Idea

Charity Dean visits the desert cemetery to find Jerald “Jerry” Jones, a homeless patient whose death represents what neglect looks like when no institution owns the whole person. The pandemic has given America a glimpse of the threat Charity feared: a pathogen that is airborne, asymptomatic, and easiest to see only after transmission has already happened. She concludes that the country needs an institution for disease prevention but that prevention is a public good with little market incentive and little political reward. Her answer is The Public Health Company, a private venture staffed by public-health nurses, Biohub scientists, and advisers who can turn front-line expertise into a service that corporations can buy. Todd Park’s example of athenahealth supplies the mechanism: observe an expert’s tacit decisions, encode the useful pattern, and make it available to many users. The epilogue does not claim the new company will solve public health; it shows Charity trying to repair the sin of omission by creating a durable route from early warning to action.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Airborne and asymptomatic preparedness**: Use COVID as a preview of a pathogen whose hidden infectious period defeats rear-view-mirror surveillance.
- **Public-good entrepreneurship**: Use private capital and customers to fund prevention that government has failed to provide.
- **Expert-to-institution translation**: Turn the judgment of a rare front-line operator into a repeatable tool without pretending the tool removes the need for judgment.

## Key Concepts

- **Sin of omission**: What Charity regrets most is what she failed to say or do when a person needed her.
- **Public good**: A benefit whose value is broadly shared and therefore underprovided by ordinary profit incentives.
- **The Public Health Company**: Charity’s venture to build private capacity for public-health operations.
- **Airborne and asymptomatic**: The paired properties that make a pathogen hard to see and contain.
- **Tacit expertise**: Practical knowledge embedded in an expert’s judgments and habits.
- **Institutionalization**: Moving a capability from one person’s memory into a durable system.

## Mental Models

- Treat prevention as a **market failure** when the beneficiaries are diffuse and the costs of preparation are immediate.
- Ask whether an expert’s judgment can be **made portable** without deleting context and limits.
- Use the **graveyard of omission** as a prompt to name the harm created by not acting.

## Worked Example

Todd Park’s athenahealth story gives Charity a model for institutionalizing expertise. A billing expert named Sue Henderson knew how insurance companies changed rules and what language made them pay; Park’s brother sat with her and coded those reactions into software used by thousands of doctors. Charity’s analogous ambition is to make her local-public-health judgment available to companies that need to protect people and supply chains. The example clarifies both the promise and the risk: a tool can distribute a rare capability, but only if the people building it preserve the conditions under which the judgment works.

## Distinctions & Limits

Charity’s venture is a response to institutional failure, not proof that private business can replace public health. The epilogue is deliberately prospective: the company has funding, staff, and a mission, but its final form and effectiveness remain uncertain in the source. Turning an expert into software can extend reach, yet it can also flatten local context, legal authority, and the moral responsibility to act. The chapter’s argument is strongest as a design question: what would make prevention financially and operationally durable? It remains limited as an evaluation of whether the proposed company succeeds.

## Anti-patterns

- **Leaving prevention to a market that cannot capture its diffuse benefits**.
- **Keeping expertise inside one person’s head**.
- **Treating software as a substitute for authority, supplies, and human judgment**.
- **Allowing omissions to remain invisible because no single actor owns them**.

## Key Takeaways

1. Treat airborne, asymptomatic threats as a distinct preparedness problem.
2. Give prevention a durable economic and institutional home.
3. Convert tacit expertise into tools while preserving context and accountability.
4. Name what was omitted, not only what was done.
5. Use the pandemic’s warning to build capacity before the next pathogen arrives.
