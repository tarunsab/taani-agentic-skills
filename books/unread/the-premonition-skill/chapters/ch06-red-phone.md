# Chapter 6: The Red Phone

**Contents position**: SIX | **Part**: Part II

## Core Idea

Joe DeRisi’s laboratory demonstrates what becomes possible when pathogen discovery is organized around curiosity rather than a narrow prior hypothesis. His Virochip compares genetic material from an unknown sample with sequences from thousands of known viruses, and identifies the coronavirus responsible for SARS from a lung sample in about a day. The tool’s deeper value is epistemic: it lets nature reveal a viral family instead of forcing a human expert to guess what familiar disease the sample resembles. Joe’s later investigations into a snake pandemic, unexplained encephalitis, and Balamuthia show both the power of open-ended genomic search and the weakness of a system that cannot carry a discovery through diagnosis, approval, and treatment. The “Red Phone” is an informal route around that last-mile failure, but it usually rings only after ordinary medicine has exhausted its options. By January 2020, Joe is trying to build an early-warning network that catches novel disease in people near its point of emergence, while the institutions that should own prevention remain uninterested or unable to act.

## Narrative movement 1 — Virochip and SARS

The CDC initially dismisses DeRisi’s young lab, then sends a lung sample in an ordinary FedEx box because an unidentified pathogen has not yet been formally classified as dangerous. The lab dissolves the sample and washes its genetic material across a slide containing full or partial sequences from roughly 22,000 known viruses. Pieces match cow, bird, and human coronaviruses without forming a known pattern, revealing a novel coronavirus rather than a disease Joe had been trained to expect. The later search for a reservoir finds the virus in horseshoe bats, but the first identification comes from letting the genetic material point to its family. The method changes diagnosis from a slow fishing expedition—grow enough virus, guess what it resembles, test one hypothesis at a time—to an inspectable comparison against a large gene library.

## Narrative movement 2 — Curiosity, snakes, and proof

Joe treats an odd letter about a service boa as a possible lead and discovers a global die-off of snakes. Because snakes lack a completed reference genome, the lab first sequences a healthy boa, subtracts what belongs to the snake, and studies what remains in diseased animals. It finds a new arenavirus related to an ancient ancestor of Ebola, then uses Koch’s postulates by injecting the virus into healthy boas and pythons; boas sicken while pythons survive. The result helps zoos quarantine the virus, but Joe cannot complete the reservoir experiment because the high-containment Army lab is shut after safety violations. The episode shows the value of open-ended observation and the boundary set by biosafety: a plausible mechanism is not the same as a finished causal or ecological explanation.

## Narrative movement 3 — The Red Phone and the last mile

Neurologist Michael Wilson brings Joe a dying boy whose spinal fluid contains Leptospira; the boy’s swimming in a warm Puerto Rican lake supplies the missing social and environmental history, and penicillin saves him. A Chinese woman with a rapidly destroyed brain reaches the lab only after weeks of ineffective treatment and more than one million dollars in hospital costs. Of 19 million genetic sequences in her spinal fluid, 486 match Balamuthia mandrillaris, an amoeba that pathologists had mistaken for immune cells; the lab then screens 2,177 already-approved drugs and finds that nitroxoline kills it. The drug remains unapproved by the FDA years later, while the CDC continues to recommend an ineffective treatment, so the scientific answer is not yet a clinical answer. Joe calls this the last-mile problem: companies lack a market, academics stop after publication, government systems fail to curate or deploy the knowledge, and individuals must pick up the baton outside their job descriptions.

## Narrative movement 4 — Early-warning radar

The Chan Zuckerberg Biohub gives Joe a platform and a $600 million mission to eliminate disease, but its practical project is a global network of genomic trip wires. The earlier U.S. Predict program tried to find dangerous animal viruses and lost funding under Trump; Joe’s alternative is to identify unknown disease when it first appears in a human emergency room. Cambodia is valuable as a node near China and a destination for Chinese travelers, although China declines to participate directly. Joe trains Cambodian doctors to run the gene machine so a fever of unknown origin can reveal whether a pathogen is new to humans. On the return flight through Guangdong on January 10, 2020, fever-screening stalls and masked security reveal that the people on the ground know something he does not, turning the early-warning idea into an immediate premonition.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Hypothesis-free discovery**: Search broadly enough that the unknown pathogen can identify its genetic relatives.
  - When to use: When familiar diagnostic categories are failing or the pathogen may be novel.
  - How: Subtract known host material, compare the remainder with an expanding reference library, and investigate the unmatched signal.
- **Last-mile test**: Ask who will receive, approve, pay for, and use a discovery before calling it a solution.
- **Red Phone**: Maintain an accessible expert channel for cases that formal pathways cannot diagnose.

## Key Concepts

- **Virochip**: Genetic slide for matching unknown viral material to known sequences.
- **GenBank**: Federally funded, frequently updated store of genetic sequences.
- **Reservoir species**: Animal that harbors a pathogen before it jumps into humans.
- **Koch’s postulates**: Causal proof by isolating a pathogen and reproducing disease in a healthy host.
- **Dark genomic matter**: Genetic material with no known match.
- **Early-warning radar**: Distributed surveillance for novel pathogens near emergence.

## Mental Models

- Think of unknown genes as **puzzle pieces** in a warehouse of partial pictures.
- Treat “that’s weird” as an **investigation trigger**, not as a footnote.
- Use **network nodes** to move discovery closer to the first human case.

## Worked Example

The Balamuthia case begins with a patient whose brain is almost gone and whose expensive treatments have failed. Genomic subtraction leaves 1,863 non-human sequences, 486 of which match the partial Balamuthia picture; the lab then tests every approved drug it can obtain. Nitroxoline kills the amoeba, but the result cannot save the woman and does not immediately change national treatment guidance. This compactly shows the full chain from detection to intervention—and where the system breaks between each link.

## Distinctions & Limits

The Virochip is not magic: it depends on a reference library, preserved genetic sequences, usable samples, and a method for recognizing that partial matches form a novel family. The lab’s work identifies pathogens and sometimes a promising treatment, but it does not automatically prove transmission, establish a reservoir, or secure regulatory approval. Negative data are weak evidence when the search may have sampled the wrong animal, as the snake investigation shows. High-containment experiments can be stopped for good reasons, and the book treats biosafety failures as part of the system’s limits rather than as obstacles to ignore. Genomic speed matters only if a clinical and public-health network can act on the result.

## Anti-patterns

- **Searching only for what an expert expects**.
- **Equating identification with cure**.
- **Assuming a discovery will diffuse by itself**.
- **Treating biosafety as an administrative inconvenience**.

## Key Takeaways

1. Give unknown samples a way to reveal their relatives.
2. Build the reference library and the operating network together.
3. Keep curiosity connected to patient treatment and public-health use.
4. Measure time from sample to actionable intervention, not only time to publication.
5. Treat a neglected disease as a last-mile problem even when the science is excellent.
