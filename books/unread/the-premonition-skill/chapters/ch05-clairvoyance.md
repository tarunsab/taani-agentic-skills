# Chapter 5: Clairvoyance

**Contents position**: FIVE | **Part**: Part I

## Core Idea

After Bush leaves office, the pandemic strategy survives mainly in Carter Mecher’s memory, while files, roles, and institutional continuity disappear in the transition to Obama. When the 2009 H1N1 virus appears in California, Mexico, and Texas, Carter recognizes that the threat has jumped ahead of the plan and tells President Obama that a 1918-like outcome could mean two million deaths. The response is dominated by ambiguity: officials see intensive-care patients but do not know the denominator of all infections, and the CDC recommends reopening schools while Carter and Richard argue for a tactical pause. H1N1 turns out to be less lethal than feared, but the lucky outcome disguises the weakness of the decision process and the slow arrival of the vaccine. Carter’s lesson is that experience can harden into confidence in the wrong model, so a crisis needs several analogies, explicit lags, and a map or plan even when the map is imperfect. Charity Dean’s December 2019 “It Has Started” resolution carries the same premonition into California, where her career has trained her to look for the threat before the institution is ready to acknowledge it.

## Narrative movement 1 — Lost continuity and the 2009 alarm

The Obama transition removes hard drives, old emails, and the Biodefense Directorate, folding disease into a broader Resilience Directorate. Carter remains because there is nowhere else for him to go, but his institutional memory is treated as disposable and he spends months watching new officials learn the building. In April 2009, two apparently unrelated California cases and reports of young people dying in Mexico give him enough evidence to call the event an imminent national-security threat. Obama asks for the worst case, and Carter supplies the 1918 comparison: 30 percent infected and 2 percent dead would imply roughly two million Americans. The new virus has bypassed the original assumption that the United States would have time to prepare after an overseas emergence.

## Narrative movement 2 — The denominator and the false reassurance

Richard’s journal records how little anyone knows early in an outbreak: the ICU concentrates the badness, but its patients cannot tell you how many people survived mild infection. Mexico closes schools and distances people, while the CDC gives American schools discretion and removes political cover for local action. Forecasts place the attack rate between 20 and 30 percent and the case-fatality rate between 0.1 and 1.8 percent, a range spanning a bad flu season to more than a million deaths. The CDC’s decision to recommend reopening before the uncertainty is resolved is, to Richard, a failure of public-health duty rather than a vindicated judgment. H1N1 ultimately infects an estimated 40–80 million Americans and kills 12,469, but the outcome cannot retroactively make the choice to wait correct. The vaccine enters production quickly yet is not ready for mass distribution until late December, showing that the country has no new “buttons” to push once the crisis begins.

## Narrative movement 3 — Maps, scouts, and clairvoyance

Carter compares the 2009 experience to a map found by lost soldiers: the map may not depict the exact terrain, but it gives them a starting point and prevents panic when options run out. Heidi Avery keeps him in government as “Scout,” sending him ahead to look for what others do not see in crises such as Deepwater Horizon, Fukushima, and Haiti. He describes pandemic management as a car whose acceleration or braking takes effect fifteen seconds late, or as a star whose light shows the past rather than the present. The CDC is a “peacetime institution in a wartime environment”: excellent at reporting what happened, poorly fitted to decide while events are still unfolding. Charity’s own life story teaches her to interpret premonition as a demand to prepare; on December 21, 2019, she writes “It Has Started” without knowing what the referent is. Her intuition is not magic, but it is a practiced sensitivity to weak signals, delay, and institutional reluctance.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Denominator discipline**: Never infer lethality from deaths or ICU cases without estimating all infections.
- **Multiple movies in the head**: Use several analogies and historical models rather than clinging to one precedent.
- **Map-before-certainty**: A rough plan gives a team a starting point when the terrain is changing.
- **Scout role**: Send a person ahead to find the overlooked fact or consequence before the main organization commits.

## Key Concepts

- **Attack rate**: Share of the population infected.
- **Case fatality vs. infection fatality**: Detected-case deaths can differ sharply from deaths divided by all infections.
- **Tactical pause**: A short interruption to learn more before reopening or escalating.
- **Lagging indicator**: Evidence that describes an earlier state of the outbreak.
- **Peacetime institution**: An organization optimized for analysis and routine rather than fast action under attack.
- **Experience trap**: Confidence gained from an outcome that may have been luck.

## Mental Models

- Think of a death as **old starlight**: it reveals transmission from earlier weeks.
- Treat a plan as a **map**, not as a guarantee that the terrain matches.
- Ask whether the institution is built to **observe** or to **intervene**.

## Worked Example

Obama asks Carter what the new flu could do. Carter applies the old pandemic plan to the new facts, explains the infection and death assumptions, and warns that the virus has already bypassed the overseas preparation window. Richard then records how Mexico’s early social interventions differ from the CDC’s permissive guidance. The virus’s milder final outcome makes the warning look excessive to later observers, but the exercise shows why the decision had to be judged at the moment when the denominator, severity, and future trajectory were all uncertain.

## Distinctions & Limits

The chapter does not say that every premonition is reliable or that a single rough model can forecast a pandemic precisely. Carter repeatedly warns against mistaking a convenient analogy for reality, and the 2009 event demonstrates why: it resembles 1918 in some respects and H1N1 in others. The CDC’s failure is not simply slowness; it is a mismatch between an institution rewarded for accurate retrospective description and a crisis that requires prospective action. The map metaphor accepts that plans can be wrong while still being valuable. The chapter also leaves open whether personal clairvoyance can be institutionalized; its later answer is to build networks, tools, and decision rights rather than rely on a lone scout.

## Anti-patterns

- **Deleting institutional memory during a leadership transition**.
- **Treating a mild outcome as proof that the initial risk was mild**.
- **Using one precedent as the whole model**.
- **Waiting for the signal to be unambiguous before preparing**.

## Key Takeaways

1. Record what was knowable before the outcome.
2. Separate numerator, denominator, and time lag.
3. Keep imperfect maps and multiple analogies available.
4. Design institutions for wartime decisions, not only peacetime reporting.
5. Treat a weak signal as a prompt to investigate, not as proof of prophecy.
