# Chapter 10: The Bug in the System

**Contents position**: TEN | **Part**: Part III

## Core Idea

Joe DeRisi’s call with Governor Newsom exposes a public-health system whose first missing capability is fast testing. A ten-day result cannot guide isolation, hospital allocation, or contact tracing, so Joe converts the Chan Zuckerberg Biohub into a free production-line laboratory and discovers that the limiting factors are contracts, billing, certification, swabs, data formats, and institutional courage as much as science. The chapter then returns to Charity’s earlier TB case to show how a pathogen’s genome can reveal a hidden social network, a treatment failure, and a chain of transmission that ordinary records miss. COVID-19 sits in a genomic “sweet spot”: it mutates slowly enough that related genomes can track movement, but reliably enough that clusters and variants become visible. Mission District testing, the Alder Bay assisted-living outbreak, and the fish-packing plant show genomics preserving lives, finding superspreaders, and preventing an unnecessary shutdown. The system still fails because it sequences too little, cannot route the data to front-line officers, and protects institutional boundaries over operational use; the bug is not a missing gadget but a broken chain from signal to decision.

## Narrative movement 1 — Building a test factory

Newsom asks Joe for the three best and worst actions, and Joe says testing is the one prerequisite that makes any other plan possible. Labcorp and Quest return results too slowly, while hospitals treat every flu-like patient as if infected, consuming beds and protective gear and losing the ability to see where the virus is. Joe recruits hundreds of unpaid research scientists and organizes them into squads with separate staffing, training for replacement, and a production-line hierarchy. The Biohub reaches about 2,666 tests a day and can return results in a day or, in a pinch, three hours. The lab’s speed reveals the private sector’s just-in-time weakness: sample-to-answer machines are easy to use but depend on manufacturer-specific chemicals, and “Panther” machines sit idle when the supply chain cannot provide the reagents.

## Narrative movement 2 — The last mile of supplies and access

Free testing is underused because hospitals are tied to commercial labs, public offices are too busy to answer, and one hospital’s billing system cannot accept a zero-cost service. San Quentin sends one batch of samples and then stops because paperwork and a private contract matter more than early detection; a later transfer seeds an outbreak that infects more than 1,000 prisoners and kills 28. The Strategic National Stockpile promises 100,000 nasal swabs but the truck arrives without medical swabs, carrying Q-tips instead. Volunteers and private citizens then buy off-brand supplies, charge them to credit cards, and assemble kits on a basketball court. Local health offices often cannot receive results electronically, so the Biohub buys fax machines alongside test kits. The chain demonstrates that a tool can be technically ready and still be operationally absent.

## Narrative movement 3 — Zeferino and the social genome

Charity’s earlier investigation of Agustin Zeferino begins with four-plus TB in a crowded household and ends with a mutation first seen in Oaxaca that makes the bacteria resistant to multiple drugs. An inadequate regimen has allowed the pathogen to evolve during treatment, and a baby in the household is missed because the pediatrician leaves an abnormal X-ray in a pile while on vacation. Charity responds with quarantine, genetic screening, contact investigation, and a search for the mutation in children and adults who deny knowing Agustin. The pathogen’s genetic fingerprint reveals relationships that ordinary interviews cannot, while Agustin’s repeated escapes show the limits of legal orders without durable capacity. Dormant TB remains a future risk because it can reactivate, so the state’s refusal to fund broad sequencing leaves a trail of preventable disease.

## Narrative movement 4 — A movie of transmission

In San Francisco’s Mission District tract 022901, roughly 3,000 of 4,087 residents are tested; a little over 6 percent of Latino residents are positive, Latinos account for 95 percent of positives, and none of 981 tested white residents is positive. The social pattern matters, but Joe’s decisive evidence is the phylogenetic chart showing who shares nearly identical virus and who was infected earlier. It links households to a resident, workers to other households, and the daughter-in-law at Alder Bay to a meth dealer through a chain that killed four residents. At a fish-packing plant, two workers’ viruses are genetically far apart, showing that both infections came from outside work and allowing the plant to remain open. In January 2021, a new mutation in the Mission suggests antibody escape, yet by February the United States is sequencing less than a third of 1 percent of positive samples, far below the United Kingdom’s 10 percent. A state that can see the movie but will not route the footage to local operators has not built a system.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Test-to-action pipeline**: Judge testing by time to an actionable decision, not by the existence of a laboratory result.
- **Production-line resilience**: Separate squads, cross-train replacements, and create battlefield promotions so one infection does not disable the operation.
- **Genomic social map**: Use related mutations to infer transmission links, invasion of safe spaces, and emerging variants.
- **Last-mile audit**: Check contracts, billing, supplies, staffing, data transfer, certification, and ownership.

## Key Concepts

- **Sample-to-answer machine**: Automated device whose convenience depends on proprietary reagents and parts.
- **Just-in-time fragility**: A supply model that fails when global demand surges simultaneously.
- **Phylogenetic chart**: Visual family tree of related viral genomes.
- **Genomic sweet spot**: Mutation rate that is neither too slow nor too fast for tracing.
- **Safe space**: A setting where internal transmission must be distinguished from outside invasion.
- **Shadow public-health officer**: Private scientist forced to perform the unowned work of local government.

## Mental Models

- Treat testing as a **sensor-to-decision loop**; latency can make accuracy useless.
- Treat a genome as **DNA evidence**, not merely a positive/negative label.
- Use **redundancy and substitution** in the lab the way a battlefield unit uses replacement roles.

## Worked Example

The Mission chart starts with the Wuhan genome and follows mutations through households and workers. One resident carries the same virus as a household but has older infection and antibodies, suggesting he introduced it; the same branch may connect him to workers outside the neighborhood. In Humboldt County, sequencing later shows that a meth dealer infected his father-in-law, whose daughter-in-law infected Alder Bay, where more than a dozen staff and residents became ill and four died. The examples show how genomic evidence converts an invisible social chain into a targeted investigation.

## Distinctions & Limits

Genomics can infer close relationships and timing, but it does not by itself identify every social contact or prove the exact direction of transmission. The Mission sample is not a random portrait of all America, and the early asymptomatic and mutation findings require interpretation over time. Testing and sequencing also depend on a specimen, a reference library, turnaround, privacy rules, and a front-line user who can act. The source’s supply-chain stories show that public and private organizations can be both helpful and predatory, so “the market” is not one uniform actor. Genomic surveillance cannot repair a system that will not fund it, share it, or give local officers the authority to use it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Calling a ten-day answer a usable test**.
- **Assuming a free tool will be adopted without removing billing and workflow barriers**.
- **Publishing genomic findings without routing them to the investigators who can act**.
- **Protecting a contract or agency boundary while an outbreak grows**.

## Key Takeaways

1. Design the operational handoff with the assay.
2. Treat supply chains and data formats as part of public-health capacity.
3. Sequence positives when transmission links and variants matter.
4. Use genomic divergence to distinguish workplace spread from independent outside infections.
5. Measure whether local officers can use the result before the next transmission cycle.
