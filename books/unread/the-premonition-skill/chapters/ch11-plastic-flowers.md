# Chapter 11: Plastic Flowers

**Contents position**: ELEVEN | **Part**: Part III

## Core Idea

California’s testing task force succeeds because it combines private logistics, public authority, volunteer expertise, and an operator who knows what the crisis requires, yet its success also reveals how much the state has been performing competence rather than organizing it. Charity Dean’s plastic-flower story supplies the chapter’s governing metaphor: a small substitute used to fill a gap can become a system devoted to preserving the appearance that the real thing is still alive. The history of the 1976 swine-flu vaccination campaign explains why the CDC learned to fear visible decisions, retrospective blame, and political exposure. David Sencer acted under uncertainty, the pandemic failed to arrive, Guillain-Barré cases made the campaign unpopular, and later accounts treated the decision as a fiasco while discounting the cost of waiting. After Bill Foege’s resignation, the CDC director becomes a presidential appointment, selecting for political safety and short-term loyalty rather than continuity and battlefield courage. The chapter ends with Carter’s mother dying of COVID, turning institutional failure into personal grief and showing that the country’s bad example was not an abstraction.

## Narrative movement 1 — Success inside a broken apparatus

Paul Markovich, CEO of Blue Shield of California, joins Charity and Bob Kocher after Governor Newsom asks for a testing task force. They move California from roughly last in testing to roughly first, reaching 60,000 tests a day by the end of May and twice that by late June. The work requires ten million swabs, private credit cards, a flexible private sector, and a public university system capable of supplying talent. Yet the state’s computer and procurement systems cannot absorb the result; Markovich offers to replace the data system for free and cannot get a clear answer. Charity is visibly running the response while Sonia Angell, the formal chief health officer, is absent, and the state tries to keep that contradiction offstage. Success therefore shows both operational capability and a personnel system that cannot name the person doing the job.

## Narrative movement 2 — The plastic-flower descent

As a young woman, Charity fills dead spots on her porch with plastic flowers, then buys more fake flowers and spends months pretending to garden. The deception begins as gap-filling and becomes a burden of maintaining optics, even as real plants die beneath the cover. She sees the CDC’s pandemic response in the same way: it creates the appearance of containment expertise while defining containment as impossible and avoiding the dignity-risk of trying. When Ghaly asks her to run the response while Angell remains the public face, the state wants the work without the acknowledgment and authority. The Biohub meeting and the proposed genomic network give Charity a last flicker of hope, but bureaucracy, privacy fears about Zuckerberg’s name, commercial-lab contracts, and CDC control prevent the handoff. A system that cannot say who is in charge cannot make the best available tool part of its standard operation.

## Narrative movement 3 — The 1976 swine-flu affair

At Fort Dix in 1976, a new swine flu appears related to the 1918 virus, and experts know that a vaccine for 217 million Americans must begin before the severity is certain. CDC director David Sencer chooses vaccination rather than waiting, and 43 million people receive it before cases of Guillain-Barré syndrome lead to suspension; the pandemic never comes. Later, Richard Neustadt and Harvey Fineberg’s The Swine Flu Affair gives the episode a villain and treats the outcome as evidence that Sencer misused authority. D.A. Henderson objects that the authors judge a decision made without knowable odds from the safety of hindsight, because not deciding was also a bet. The episode creates a powerful institutional lesson: a public-health official can be punished for a visible error even when the invisible cost of omission would have been much larger.

## Narrative movement 4 — Independence, memory, and grief

Bill Foege’s 2020 letter asks CDC director Robert Redfield to face the truth, apologize, and reassert the agency’s independence from political interference. Foege’s own career explains why that independence eroded: after the White House pressured the CDC over AIDS research and aspirin’s link to Reye’s syndrome, he resigned, and the CDC director role was converted from career civil service to presidential appointment. The new arrangement makes competent appointees easier to fire and rewards the Chamberlain who avoids political risk. Richard and Carter try to restore institutional memory by bringing Sencer and other 1976 officials into the Obama White House, where the decision is finally understood in context. In November 2020 Carter’s father and mother contract COVID, and his mother dies eighteen days later; the man who had warned the country now buries a parent in the failure he tried to prevent. Personal grief is the book’s final evidence that a bad process cannot be rescued by a lucky outcome.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Plastic flowers**: When gap-filling becomes an ongoing effort to preserve appearances, the institution is maintaining optics rather than capability.
- **Retrospective-judgment check**: Reconstruct what was knowable at the decision point before evaluating the result.
- **Institutional independence**: Protect technical decision makers from political pressure while keeping responsibility explicit.
- **Continuity as capacity**: Preserve memory, files, and experienced people across administrations.

## Key Concepts

- **Optics machine**: Work arranged to look successful rather than to solve the underlying problem.
- **Sin of omission**: Harm from failing to act; often less visible and less punishable than action.
- **No-win decision**: Choice where either action or inaction carries serious cost.
- **Career civil servant**: Experienced official with continuity across political administrations.
- **Presidential appointee**: Leader selected and removable through the political chain.
- **Chamberlain vs. Churchill**: Avoidance of conflict versus early action on an existential threat.

## Mental Models

- Ask **is the plant alive, or have we filled the blank with plastic?**
- Judge a crisis decision with a **contemporaneous information set**, not with the outcome.
- Separate **independence** from lack of accountability: technical autonomy still needs an owner.

## Worked Example

The 1976 decision can be represented as two bets. Vaccination begins before the country knows whether the virus will return, creating the possibility of adverse events and political blame; waiting avoids those visible costs but risks losing the time needed to vaccinate 217 million people. When the pandemic fails to appear, the first bet looks foolish and the second looks wise, although the odds were unknowable at the time. Henderson’s criticism restores the missing counterfactual: if the pandemic had arrived, the same decision might have made Sencer a hero.

## Distinctions & Limits

The chapter does not say that the 1976 campaign had no avoidable mistakes or that political leadership never matters. It argues that outcome-based narratives erase uncertainty and create incentives to avoid decisions whose benefits are invisible. The plastic-flower metaphor diagnoses an institutional pattern, not every individual’s motive; people inside the CDC may have coherent reasons for preserving their agency’s reputation. Reinstating career independence alone would not solve fragmented local capacity, poor supplies, or weak last-mile systems. The chapter’s personal ending also cannot prove a single causal chain from one policy choice to one death, but it makes the cost of institutional omission impossible to keep abstract.

## Anti-patterns

- **Preserving the appearance of a living capability after the capability has decayed**.
- **Punishing visible action while ignoring invisible omission**.
- **Converting technical leadership into a political loyalty role**.
- **Deleting institutional memory and then relearning the same lesson**.

## Key Takeaways

1. Measure capability, not the appearance of capability.
2. Keep an explicit record of uncertainty and alternatives.
3. Protect independent expertise while naming the accountable decision maker.
4. Preserve files, people, and lessons across transitions.
5. Treat a lucky outcome as a reason to examine the process, not to certify it.
