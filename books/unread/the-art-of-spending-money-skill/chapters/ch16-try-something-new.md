# Chapter 16: Try Something New

## Core Idea

People cannot know in advance what spending will make them happy, so Housel recommends a wide funnel and a tight filter. Try many affordable forms of food, travel, clothing, hobbies, and experiences, then cut quickly and without guilt what is not working. He models this on reading: start many books, finish few, and refuse to turn an uninteresting book into a chore. The method protects individuality because price, popularity, advertising, and brand are poor guarantees of personal joy; brands primarily signal consistency, not automatic superiority. Experiments need not be large, and the freed money from failed trials can be redirected toward the person’s distinctive “thing,” which may be a material pleasure, giving, or independence. Variety also creates memories and keeps time from becoming a blur of monotony, while the filter prevents novelty from becoming indiscriminate consumption.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Wide funnel, tight filter**: Sample broadly, reject rapidly, and concentrate spending on what fits.
  - When to use: When you have not discovered what actually brings joy.
  - How: Run small trials within budget; stop repeating poor fits; keep the few experiences that deliver value.
- **Spend extravagantly on what you love, cut what you don’t**: A personal allocation rule associated in the chapter with Ramit Sethi.
  - When to use: When a budget is spread across many mediocre categories.
  - How: Find the high-value category through testing, then fund it by cutting low-value spending.
- **Brand as consistency signal**: Pay a premium when repeatability matters, not because price itself proves quality.

## Key Concepts

- **Wide funnel**: Willingness to sample many possible sources of value.
- **Tight filter**: Quick, low-guilt rejection of options that do not fit.
- **Personal “thing”**: The unusual category of spending that reliably creates joy for an individual.
- **Brand consistency**: A predictable product experience across places, not a guarantee of excellence.
- **Variety**: New experiences that create memories and slow perceived time.

## Mental Models

- Use the **ten-minute book test** as a reminder that sunk cost does not justify continuing a poor fit.
- Think of spending as **evolution by elimination**: failed options provide information if you stop funding them.
- Use **small experiments before lifestyle commitments**.

## Anti-patterns

- **Finishing every purchase**: Continuing a bad subscription, hobby, or experience because you already paid.
- **Price as a joy proxy**: Assuming the most expensive or advertised option will fit your personality.
- **Defaulting to society’s taste**: Copying popular luxury without testing whether it creates personal value.

## Worked Example

Housel disliked fiction and history until he tried enough books to find subjects he loved; some poorly reviewed books worked for him, while acclaimed books did not. He transfers the method to spending: try a $10 food, a $50 treat, or slightly better shoes, then stop if the experience does not improve life. The goal is information and fit, not a larger consumption total.

## Key Takeaways

1. Experiment within a budget rather than assuming you know your preferences.
2. Cut failed categories quickly and without shame.
3. Treat brands as consistency signals, not joy guarantees.
4. Use variety to discover a personal high-value category and create memories.
