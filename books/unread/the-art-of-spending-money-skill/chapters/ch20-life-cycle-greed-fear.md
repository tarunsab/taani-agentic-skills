# Chapter 20: The Life Cycle of Greed and Fear

## Core Idea

Greed and fear are not isolated opposites but stages in a recurring cycle that can begin with an innocent belief and end where it started. The belief that effort deserves reward makes it tempting to interpret success as proof that your views and actions caused the result. Recognition then encourages doubling down, even when luck, timing, competition, or unsustainable conditions contributed more than you admit. When the strategy stops working, denial and blame block feedback; repeated failure eventually becomes embarrassment, panic, and fear of further loss. Fear can be as blind as greed, because the person stops seeing opportunities and treats any prevented loss as a gain. Stabilization brings a new worldview, but the pain of the lesson can recreate the original conviction that one now deserves to be right, restarting the cycle. Mental liquidity and humility are the defenses: separate effort from outcome, preserve beginner’s mind, and update before excess becomes ruin.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Greed-and-fear cycle**: deserving to be right → reward and attention → extrapolation and doubling down → backfire → denial/blame → fear and damage control → stabilization → renewed conviction.
  - When to use: When past success is driving a larger financial, career, or lifestyle bet.
  - How: Identify which stage you are in, look for missing luck or feedback, and reduce exposure before the story becomes identity.
- **Beginner’s Mind**: Stay open to new evidence and ideas despite past success.
  - When to use: When conviction feels like proof that exploration is unnecessary.

## Key Concepts

- **Greed**: Excessive extrapolation of a prior success into a larger, less sustainable bet.
- **Fear**: A defensive state in which preventing further loss eclipses opportunity and agency.
- **Denial**: Refusal to admit that the original strategy or self-assessment was wrong.
- **Feedback immunity**: Losing the ability to learn because contrary information threatens identity.
- **Reversion to the mean**: The tendency for exceptional outcomes or strategies to return toward ordinary conditions.

## Mental Models

- Use the **causation audit**: what did your actions cause, and what came from luck, timing, or other people?
- Think of **success as a sample, not a verdict**.
- Use **beginner’s mind** when old strategies feel proven enough to stop exploring.

## Anti-patterns

- **Doubling down on what worked**: Scaling an unsustainable or luck-dependent strategy because the first result felt deserved.
- **Blaming the world**: Treating failure as proof that friends, bosses, media, or politicians are the cause while refusing self-examination.
- **Panic liquidation**: Assuming nothing is controllable after earlier assuming everything was.

## Worked Example

The chapter’s recurring examples move from “my company denied a raise” to finding a new employer, from “friends no longer admire my car” to buying a better one, and from repeated failure to blaming everyone else. Each response looks reasonable in isolation, but together they show the cycle: a past reward is treated as proof of skill, the person increases the bet, ignores feedback, then flips from opportunity-seeking to damage control. The correction is to update the story before the next escalation.

## Key Takeaways

1. Separate effort from outcome before increasing exposure.
2. Treat early success as evidence, not proof of permanent skill.
3. Notice denial and blame as feedback that the cycle is advancing.
4. Keep enough humility and flexibility to update before fear takes over.
