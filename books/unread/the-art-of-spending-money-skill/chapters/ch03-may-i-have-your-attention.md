# Chapter 3: May I Have Your Attention Please

## Core Idea

People often think they want nicer cars, larger homes, or better clothes when they actually want respect, admiration, and attention. Material possessions are a quick way to attract notice because they are visible and require no conversation, but the attention decays and is often aimed at strangers rather than the people whose love matters. Housel’s reverse obituary exposes the mismatch: most people want to be remembered as loving, helpful, wise, funny, or good parents, not for horsepower, square footage, salary, or imported marble. He distinguishes intrinsic pride in who you are from extrinsic pride that depends on other people’s opinions, warning that fixation on the latter consumes bandwidth needed for family, friendship, intelligence, and love. Social signaling is not worthless—belonging and professional fit can matter—but it is usually a weak substitute for durable respect earned through character and contribution. The better question is whose regard you want, what they pay attention to, and whether the spending is serving that relationship or performing for an audience.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Reverse obituary**: Write the qualities you want remembered, then use them to choose what to pursue.
  - When to use: When an external success target feels compelling but its meaning is unclear.
  - How: List eulogy virtues, cross out material signals that would not belong, and align spending with the virtues that matter.
- **Attention test**: Evaluate a signal by effectiveness, durability, and audience.
  - When to use: When a purchase promises admiration.
  - How: Ask how quickly the effect fades and whether the observers are people whose respect you actually seek.

## Key Concepts

- **Intrinsic pride**: Satisfaction in your own actions and character.
- **Extrinsic pride**: Pride constructed from the approval or evaluation of others.
- **Resume virtues**: Salary, title, net worth, and possessions that display achievement.
- **Eulogy virtues**: Love, respect, usefulness, character, and relationships that define a life to others.
- **Social signaling**: Using visible choices to communicate group membership or status.

## Mental Models

- Use **“show off the inside of your house”** when deciding whether display should benefit close relationships rather than strangers.
- Think of status spending as **respect junk food**: fast and tempting, with weaker durability and possible long-term cost.
- Treat desire for flashy goods as a **diagnostic signal**, not proof that the goods are the true goal.

## Anti-patterns

- **Buying admiration from strangers**: Visible novelty attracts notice but rarely creates the love or respect people imagine.
- **Letting extrinsic pride crowd out intrinsic pride**: Chasing public approval can reduce attention available for the activities that generate deep satisfaction.

## Worked Example

Chad Johnson explained why he did not need expensive jewelry: his name and talent were already larger than anything he could buy. Housel contrasts this with Jeff Bezos’s earlier Honda Accord and Steve Jobs’s sparsely furnished home; neither needed a luxury display to create respect. The transferable test is to identify what people already value you for, then stop asking possessions to do that work.

## Key Takeaways

1. Write the reverse obituary before making a status-heavy decision.
2. Measure attention by audience and durability, not by the initial rush.
3. Spend for the people whose respect you want, not for an anonymous ranking.
4. Build respect through character and contribution before using objects as signals.
