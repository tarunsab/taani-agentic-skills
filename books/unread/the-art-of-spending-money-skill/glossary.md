# Glossary

**Barbell approach** — Hold two apparently opposite practices together when each protects against a different failure, such as saving for tomorrow while enjoying today or controlling both large and small expenses (Ch 19).

**Contentment** — Durable satisfaction produced when expectations no longer exceed circumstances; unlike a short happiness buzz, it ends the chase for “what’s next” (Ch 4, Ch 7).

**Contrast** — The gap between current experience and prior experience or expectation that makes an ordinary or occasional treat feel valuable (Ch 7).

**Eulogy virtues** — Qualities such as love, respect, helpfulness, and character by which people are actually remembered, contrasted with resume virtues (Ch 3).

**External scorecard** — Judging success by other people's attention, admiration, envy, or rankings (Ch 6, Ch 11).

**FOMO** — Fear of missing out; social comparison combined with recklessness that causes someone to chase another person's sudden success (Ch 11).

**Frugality inertia** — Difficulty moving from a lifetime of saving into reasonable spending because “I am a saver” has become identity (Ch 15).

**Hedonic treadmill** — Adaptation that makes a former luxury feel ordinary, shifting desire toward the next upgrade (Ch 4, Ch 7).

**Independence spectrum** — A graduated model of financial control, from dependence through buffers, job choice, and debt avoidance to control of time (Ch 12).

**Internal scorecard** — Measuring life by whether it satisfies your own values and feeds your life, even when no one else sees it (Ch 6, Ch 14).

**Mental liquidity** — Ability to abandon beliefs or strategies when facts, circumstances, or the person holding them changes (Ch 15, Ch 20).

**Psychological wealth** — The gap between what a person has and what they want; low wants can make modest material wealth feel abundant (Ch 4).

**Quiet compounding** — Slow, private saving and investing guided by internal goals rather than public performance or comparison (Ch 14).

**Regret minimization framework** — Project yourself into the future and choose the path likely to minimize the regret you will carry later (Ch 10).

**Resume virtues** — Salary, title, net worth, and possessions that look impressive on a résumé but may not produce love or respect (Ch 3).

**Social debt** — Hidden obligations, envy, privacy loss, expectation inflation, or relationship changes created by visible spending (Ch 13).

**Status** — A purchase's signaling value: how it may change other people's view of your rank, success, or identity (Ch 9).

**Utility** — A purchase's ability to improve your own life through comfort, function, durability, convenience, or connection (Ch 9).

**Wealth** — In Housel's distinction, control over what money does to your freedom, personality, desires, relationships, and mental health—not merely cash to buy things (Ch 8).

**Wide funnel, tight filter** — Try many affordable options, then quickly reject the experiences or purchases that do not fit your personality (Ch 16).
