# Chapter 2 — Engines by GE, Body by Houdini

## Core Idea

This chapter shows how a startling technical idea survives contact with skeptical experts, budget limits, security restrictions, labor disruption, and flight-test danger. The Hopeless Diamond was not accepted because it looked promising; it was accepted after chamber and outdoor-range measurements matched the calculations closely enough to force a new design conversation. Have Blue then used a radical airframe around mostly available engines, computers, actuators, seats, displays, and navigation equipment. Rich's strongest organizational evidence is the combination of a compact project office, shop-floor integration, independent quality authority, and trust concentrated in a program manager.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Validate the decisive model before building the full system.
- Reuse mature components around the novel core.
- Keep engineering, manufacturing, inspection, and test in one loop.
- Let independent quality assurance report directly to the program leader.
- Preserve end-to-end authority during disruption.

## Key Concepts

Kelly Johnson initially rejected the faceted design because it violated the aerodynamic intuition that a beautiful airplane should fly well. Dick Cantrell and the flight-control team made the ugly geometry viable by using fly-by-wire computers to stabilize an aircraft that was naturally unstable in pitch and yaw. A ten-foot wooden model then returned roughly a thousand times less radar energy than the comparison drone in an electromagnetic chamber; a later White Sands test showed the predicted advantage against a Northrop aircraft. Birds, droppings, atmospheric layers, and a bright support pole complicated the range data, so the team improved the test fixture rather than dismissing inconvenient measurements.

The Air Force eventually funded two Have Blue prototypes, but the available budget was below Lockheed's estimate. Rich asked his board to carry a substantial share of the risk because he believed a successful demonstration could open a much larger market. Classification raised costs and slowed ordinary work: documents needed strict handling, access was compartmented, and even routine materials could become security problems. The team offset that drag by adapting off-the-shelf hardware, including GE J-85 engines and flight-control equipment derived from other aircraft.

## Mental Models

### Test the model at the right scale

The model did not prove every property of the fighter, but it answered the question that mattered first: could the geometry produce a radically smaller radar return? The ball-bearing demonstration Rich used with Pentagon officials made the invisible quantity intuitively legible. A good early test isolates the dominant uncertainty; it does not pretend to certify the whole system.

### Quality is a line function

Rich describes a layout in which designers worked near the shop floor, workers could contribute to design decisions, and inspection had a direct path to him. Assemblies were checked as they were made instead of being trusted until the end. This resembles modern quality-at-source practice: independent inspection can stop work, while the person doing the work remains responsible for catching defects.

### Rules are decision rights

The chapter's fourteen rules are less a checklist than a redistribution of authority. They make the program manager responsible for the whole result, keep the project office small, limit access, demand simple documentation, require monthly cost review, and give the contractor meaningful responsibility for testing and suppliers. They also require timely funding and trust between customer and contractor; a small team cannot compensate indefinitely for an absent sponsor.

## Worked Example (Study)

During a machinists' strike, Bob Murphy led a small group of managers and engineers who worked long shifts, kept an engine test operating outdoors, and completed Have Blue before its deadline. The improvised steel cabinet that became a heat shield is a memorable detail, but the mechanism is broader: preserve the critical path, use available material safely, and keep technical ownership with the people who understand the system. The result still required later flight testing and included a serious landing-gear failure that forced test pilot Bill Park to eject, so “resourceful” does not mean “risk-free.”

## Distinctions and Limitations

The memoir celebrates speed and direct authority, but classified aerospace work had unusual resources, highly selected personnel, and a customer willing to tolerate exceptional arrangements. The fourteen rules should not be copied into a regulated environment without adapting safety, labor, security, and independent verification requirements. Rich also emphasizes success more than the full population of failed experiments, so the chapter is better used as a mechanism study than as a universal productivity proof.

## Practical Use

For a technically uncertain program, make a table with three columns: “must be invented,” “can be adapted,” and “must be independently verified.” Put the highest-risk unknown in a physical or executable test. Then assign one leader authority over the interfaces and give quality or safety an independent stop right; the two roles are complementary, not competing.

## Key Takeaways

- Measurements that survive skeptical testing can overturn aesthetic or institutional intuition.
- Reusing proven components concentrates risk where the mission advantage actually lives.
- Integration and quality improve when the people who design, build, inspect, and test can talk directly.
- A compact rule set matters only when it gives real authority and timely resources.
