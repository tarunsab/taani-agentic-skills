# Chapter 16 — Drawing the Right Conclusions

## Core Idea

Rich closes by extracting an organizational model from the successes and failures in the memoir. He argues that advanced development needs a small, low-overhead, independent group with a specific difficult objective, modest prototype quantities, generalists, access to parent-company resources, and a senior sponsor willing to surrender day-to-day oversight. He also argues against perfectionism, paperwork detached from hardware, fragmented competition, late funding, and a shrinking industrial base. The chapter is a practical conclusion, but it remains a memoir argument: the model is a set of conditions to test, not a universal law.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Separate advanced prototyping from production bureaucracy.
- Fund small quantities and learn before committing to scale.
- Use off-the-shelf components and mission-specific quality thresholds.
- Replace documentation volume with useful traceability.
- Preserve skilled workers, suppliers, and hands-on leadership.
- Review security and oversight restrictions periodically.

## Key Concepts

Rich points to examples beyond Lockheed, including Ford's Team Mustang and other advanced-development groups, to argue that the pattern can travel. He imagines future work in vertical landing, hypersonics, composite structures, surveillance, drones, jammers, and nonlethal systems, while warning that every new defense measure creates a new countermeasure problem. The industrial base matters because prototype programs depend on experienced engineers, fabricators, suppliers, and inspectors who cannot be recreated instantly after downsizing.

Cost control is a recurring theme. The F-117 used adapted controls and an existing engine; Rich estimates that avionics and other complex components can dominate cost when priced by weight. He favors limited-life combat aircraft, scheduled replacement, contractor maintenance, and performance-based support where appropriate. He contrasts a short purchase order with the enormous documentation burden that can consume a conventional acquisition office, and he treats a fixed-price contract as a discipline that forces the contractor to own its estimates rather than shifting every surprise to the customer.

The final recommendations are organizational. Prototype and test before large investment; use concurrency only when feedback is fast; choose reliable suppliers; sunset security restrictions; fund multi-year work; develop leaders rather than managers who merely administer; and resist grand programs whose physics or mission is not clear. Rich also argues that remote or unmanned systems may provide future surveillance more economically, but he couples that prediction with a warning about unclear intervention and the need to identify actual threats before acting.

## Mental Models

### Small is a risk-control strategy

Small teams reduce coordination paths, but they also limit redundancy and capacity. Their value is highest while the question is uncertain and the prototype quantity is modest. Once the mission is proven, a different structure can manufacture and sustain it, provided the learning cell and decision authority are not erased.

### Perfection has a budget curve

The memoir repeatedly claims that the final increment of performance costs a disproportionate share of the total effort. The useful rule is not “accept defects”; it is “spend precision where the mission is sensitive.” Define which tolerances affect safety or the decisive capability, and avoid paying for cosmetic or noncritical precision.

### Bureaucracy is an interface tax

Forms, audits, and specifications are not inherently wasteful. They become harmful when their volume exceeds their value, when responsibility is fragmented, or when documentation arrives too late to guide a decision. The remedy is not no records; it is a small evidence system tied to cost, quality, configuration, and safety decisions.

## Worked Example (Study)

Rich compares a short Skunk Works purchase order with much longer conventional procurement packages, then connects paperwork to cost and schedule. He also cites the F-22's escalating complexity to show that advanced technology can be made unaffordable when every requirement is treated as essential. The practical experiment is to constrain the prototype objective, measure the mission effect of each feature, and make the trade visible before the program scales.

## Distinctions and Limitations

The concluding prescriptions favor contractor autonomy and defense R&D, so they should be balanced with public accountability, competition, safety regulation, civil oversight, and the risk of concentrated authority. Some exact cost ratios, labor counts, and future predictions reflect the book's early-1990s vantage point. Use the chapter's mechanisms as hypotheses and update them against present technology, law, and mission needs.

## Practical Use

Create a two-layer operating model: a small advanced cell with a hard problem, short evidence cycles, and direct customer access; and a separate scale organization with the controls needed for production and sustainment. Define the handoff contract between them, including who owns interfaces, field learning, quality, security review, and the right to continue experiments. Audit the model every few months for hidden oversight, missing skills, and requirements that no longer serve the mission.

## Key Takeaways

- Protect small, accountable prototyping teams even inside large institutions.
- Prototype before scale and re-estimate economics at every gate.
- Spend precision on mission-critical variables, not on generalized perfection.
- Keep records useful, security reviewable, and skilled people in the system.
