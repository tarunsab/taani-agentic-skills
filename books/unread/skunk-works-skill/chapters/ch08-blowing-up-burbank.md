# Chapter 8 — Blowing Up Burbank

## Core Idea

The Suntan/CL-400 project is the memoir's clearest study of ambitious failure. Rich and Kelly pursued a liquid-hydrogen aircraft that might fly far above Soviet defenses, but performance estimates were wrong in several coupled ways: lift-to-drag was poorer, fuel consumption higher, and range shorter than the mission required. A half-scale test and a dramatic hydrogen fire exposed the gap between a persuasive concept and a workable system. Kelly canceled the aircraft, returned most of the funding, and redirected the useful technology; the chapter treats this as disciplined leadership rather than defeat.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Put the dominant physics into a test before committing to scale.
- Treat safety controls as part of the experiment, not as paperwork.
- Separate useful enabling technology from a failed mission concept.
- Cancel when measured performance misses the operating requirement.
- Preserve customer trust by returning or redirecting unneeded resources.

## Key Concepts

The CL-400 imagined a Mach 2.5 aircraft at roughly 100,000 feet with very long range. Liquid hydrogen offered high energy per pound but introduced extreme thermal and handling problems: a large temperature difference across the structure, difficult storage, and a specialized production and test environment. At Fort Robertson the team built an unusually capable cryogenic facility and maintained strict safety procedures, yet the narrative also reveals how enthusiasm can make a technical risk feel more manageable than it is.

The decisive experiment used a half-scale model in a heated enclosure to test the thermal and aerodynamic assumptions. A fire beside a large hydrogen supply became a near-disaster; Rich describes controlling it by releasing the fuel as a fog and letting emergency crews enter. The episode produced a practical safety lesson but did not rescue the aircraft's performance model. When the predicted range fell short by roughly a thousand miles, Kelly stopped the project after spending a fraction of the authorization and returned the balance.

The work was not wasted. Hydrogen-handling equipment supported later programs, the team learned about large supersonic structures and engines, and the failure helped redirect the organization toward the Blackbird. Rich contrasts this with projects that continue because their sponsors cannot admit the original premise was wrong.

## Mental Models

### Validate coupled assumptions together

The problem was not one bad number; aerodynamic efficiency, engine consumption, structural temperature, and fuel volume interacted. Testing one favorable parameter at a time could have hidden the mission-level failure. Early experiments should reproduce the coupling that determines whether the system works in its actual operating envelope.

### An honest stop is a delivered result

Cancellation protects future option value when the evidence is decisive. The leader's job is to distinguish a failed hypothesis from a failed team, salvage transferable learning, and close the project without disguising the reason. Returning money can be more valuable than producing a politically convenient artifact.

## Worked Example (Study)

Rich's half-scale model did not answer every design question, but it exposed the mismatch between the attractive concept and the aircraft's likely range. The project then ended with most funds returned and useful equipment redirected. This is a model for staged investment: each gate should be allowed to stop the next, larger commitment.

## Distinctions and Limitations

The fire story is a retrospective anecdote and should not be copied as a safety procedure. Modern high-energy or cryogenic work requires formal hazard analysis, independent safety authority, and regulated facilities. Rich's account also emphasizes Kelly's judgment, while the underlying engineering record would be needed to assess every numerical claim.

## Practical Use

Before scaling a breakthrough, list the assumptions that multiply rather than add: energy, weight, heat, range, throughput, or latency. Build a representative test with a kill criterion tied to the mission. If the criterion fails, record what remains reusable, close the work cleanly, and make the decision visible to the sponsor.

## Key Takeaways

- Large ambitions need small, representative physics tests.
- Safety and performance learning must happen together.
- A canceled platform can still yield valuable equipment and knowledge.
- Returning resources is a form of technical integrity.
