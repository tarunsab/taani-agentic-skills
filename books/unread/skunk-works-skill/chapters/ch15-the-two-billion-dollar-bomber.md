# Chapter 15 — The Two-Billion-Dollar Bomber

## Core Idea

The stealth bomber story shows the Skunk Works model colliding with national scale, political distribution, and a much larger industrial ecosystem. Rich refused a fantastical hypersonic aircraft that he believed did not make engineering or economic sense, then proposed a stealth bomber with a concrete survivability and payload mission. Lockheed produced a technically competitive design, but Northrop won the competition on the final evaluation. The resulting B-2 became dramatically more expensive and administratively heavy as quantities fell and the program expanded, illustrating why a lean development culture is hard to preserve at scale.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Reject an impossible requirement instead of building a prestige concept.
- Define the mission advantage before choosing size and range.
- Compare technical merit with political and industrial constraints explicitly.
- Keep early design teams small while the concept is still uncertain.
- Treat scale, quantity, and oversight as variables that can change the economics.

## Key Concepts

Rich's first major choice is negative: he declines the Reagan-era “Orient Express” vision of a Mach 12 aircraft because the distinction between a rocketship and an airplane made the requirement unrealistic. He then works with Perry, Fubini, and Strategic Air Command pilots on a stealth bomber concept intended to survive defended airspace and carry a meaningful payload over long range. The early project, nicknamed Peggy, benefited from operator input and a focused design team.

The competition pitted a smaller Lockheed design with better radar results against a larger Northrop design with stronger range and payload characteristics. A 1981 radar shootout established technical differences, but the final decision involved more than RCS: fewer large bombers could require more sorties, while political leaders also wanted to preserve industrial capability across companies. The memoir treats this as a genuine tradeoff rather than a simple technical defeat.

Once the bomber was selected, quantities dropped and unit costs rose sharply in the narrative. The program moved from a projected fleet of more than a hundred aircraft to a much smaller force at a far higher price, supported by a sprawling network of suppliers, auditors, and production sites. Rich contrasts that structure with the few hundred people involved in the F-117 fighter's development. Scale did not merely add labor; it changed the decision speed, documentation load, and relationship between technical teams and the customer.

## Mental Models

### The unit of innovation is not the unit of production

A small team may be ideal for proving a concept, while a large production system may be necessary to manufacture and sustain it. The mistake is assuming the second structure can replace the first before the decisive uncertainty is resolved. Preserve a protected advanced-development cell even when production expands.

### Technical merit is one decision input

The bomber competition required a trade among observability, payload, range, sortie count, jobs, suppliers, and national industrial policy. Rich's account favors the Lockheed result, but the mechanism is general: make the political, economic, and technical criteria visible so a “technical” decision does not conceal a political one.

## Worked Example (Study)

The bomber program can be modeled as two gates. Gate one asks which design best proves the mission advantage; gate two asks whether a national production and support system can afford and sustain it. The narrative shows what happens when the second gate changes quantity and cost after the technical work is largely complete.

## Distinctions and Limitations

The B-2 cost figures and comparisons are Rich's selected account and should not be treated as a budget audit. Political motives are interpreted from inside the Lockheed experience, so other stakeholders may assign different weight to jobs, industrial base, and fleet size. The chapter also cannot fully separate technical causes of cost from changing quantities and requirements.

## Practical Use

Separate prototype, low-rate production, and full-scale acquisition decisions. At each gate, re-estimate cost, quantity, staffing, oversight, and mission value rather than carrying forward an early unit price. Protect a small technical cell that can keep experimenting even as the production organization grows.

## Key Takeaways

- Refusing an impossible requirement can be more valuable than winning a visible contract.
- User input improves a concept before politics and scale dominate it.
- Acquisition scale changes cost and decision structure, not just volume.
- Keep advanced exploration distinct from production governance.
