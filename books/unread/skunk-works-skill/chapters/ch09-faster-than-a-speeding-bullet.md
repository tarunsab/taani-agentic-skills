# Chapter 9 — Faster Than a Speeding Bullet

## Core Idea

After the hydrogen aircraft failed, Kelly sought a successor to the U-2 that could outrun the improving Soviet defense network. The A-12/Oxcart and later Blackbird family emerged from a sequence of fast design iterations, a close CIA relationship, extraordinary propulsion work, and a manufacturing system built around titanium. The chapter makes speed look less like a single specification than a chain of coupled innovations: chines reduced radar return, inlet spikes made the engines viable at Mach 3, and suppliers had to learn a new material and process discipline. The team moved quickly because it kept the group small and concentrated bespoke effort on the hardest interfaces.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Iterate the concept before institutional commitments harden.
- Match the vehicle to the adversary's changing countermeasure.
- Treat propulsion, structure, signature, and production as one problem.
- Use a close supplier partnership for a novel material or process.
- Accept functional adequacy when the last increment of perfection is disproportionately expensive.

## Key Concepts

The A-1, A-10, and A-11 studies preceded the A-12, whose chine geometry was credited with reducing radar return while also contributing to high-speed aerodynamics. The final design was created in an unusually compressed collaboration between Kelly and the CIA customer. Richard Bissell required ingenuity and tight security without a large early commitment, which gave the team room to prove the concept before the program grew.

The J-58 engine, originally associated with a canceled Navy fighter, was adapted for sustained Mach 3 operation. The inlet spike and bypass behavior were not accessories; they supplied much of the propulsion system's efficiency at speed. Wind-tunnel testing at NASA Ames generated a huge measurement set, but the team still had to solve the transition from test data to a reliable full aircraft.

Titanium created a parallel production challenge. Much of the material was rejected, traceability had to be maintained, ordinary inks and tools could contaminate it, and welding and forming required new training. Lockheed built a large workforce around a process that produced only about one aircraft a month in the narrative, using large half-shell structures and carefully controlled suppliers. The team also press-tested fuel tanks with improvised Ping-Pong-ball indicators and moved aircraft by road under disguises, revealing a culture that combined high science with practical resourcefulness.

## Mental Models

### The dominant constraint moves

The U-2's limit was survivability against improving defenses. The Blackbird addressed that with altitude and speed, but its solution moved difficulty into engines, inlets, heat, titanium, fuel sealing, navigation, and pilot training. A design process should repeatedly ask which constraint becomes dominant after the first one is relieved.

### Reserve invention for the system bottleneck

The team reused an available engine core and many conventional manufacturing ideas, but invented where the mission required it. This is not anti-innovation; it is selective innovation. Every bespoke feature creates a supplier, test, maintenance, and schedule burden, so the novel surface area should be intentional.

## Worked Example (Study)

The Blackbird's titanium production line demonstrates a complete innovation loop: identify a material requirement, discover contamination and tooling problems, create traceability and training, then redesign the manufacturing process around those findings. The aircraft's performance was therefore a property of the factory as much as of the aerodynamic drawing.

## Distinctions and Limitations

Rich compresses a highly classified, multi-organization program into memorable engineering episodes. Exact cost, speed, efficiency, and production figures should be checked against primary records before use. The book also makes the Blackbird appear nearly inevitable after the U-2, while the design studies show that multiple assumptions had to be discarded first.

## Practical Use

For a successor system, identify the threat or constraint that will invalidate today's solution and design the first experiments around it. Separate the reusable platform elements from the genuinely new bottleneck. Include suppliers and production engineers in concept reviews whenever the new material, process, or tolerance could dominate schedule or reliability.

## Key Takeaways

- A successor should answer the new dominant threat, not merely improve the old platform.
- Propulsion and production bottlenecks can matter as much as the headline airframe.
- Supplier learning is part of the invention when the material is unfamiliar.
- Selective novelty makes high-risk programs more tractable.
