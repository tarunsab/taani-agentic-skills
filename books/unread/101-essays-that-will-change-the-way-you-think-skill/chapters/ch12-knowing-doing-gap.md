# Essay 12: The Knowing-Doing Gap: Why We Avoid Doing What’s Best for Us, and How to Conquer Resistance for Good

## Core Idea

The knowing-doing gap is the experience of knowing the best action but doing something else; the Greeks called it akrasia, Zen calls it resistance, and ordinary language calls it procrastination. Wiest says the central problem is not learning what is good but understanding why comfort repeatedly wins over writing, eating well, waking early, speaking honestly, or connecting genuinely. Human beings are wired to seek comfort as a survival cue, while modern culture reinforces the idea that a good life should be pain-free and secure. The practical reframe is to compare the discomfort of acting with the much larger discomfort of never having the desired work, relationship, or life. Small action breaks the spell because acting creates evidence and momentum that thinking alone cannot provide.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Knowing-doing gap**
  - When to use: You can state the right action but keep choosing the easier alternative.
  - How: Identify the comfort being protected, compare both futures, and take one small step immediately.
- **Alternative-cost framing**
  - When to use: Resistance makes short-term avoidance feel rational.
  - How: Ask what an average day repeated for a year would produce, including wasted hours and missed relationships.
- **Action before readiness**
  - When to use: You are waiting to feel prepared, confident, or unafraid.
  - How: Act while uncomfortable; let direct experience teach the mind that the task is possible and rewarding.

## Key Concepts

- **Akrasia**: Acting against one’s better judgment.
- **Resistance**: The felt opposition to an action that would serve a larger aim.
- **Comfort bias**: The tendency to treat immediate ease as the safest or best option.
- **Knowing-doing gap**: The distance between intellectual knowledge and behavior.
- **Idle-hour anxiety**: Fear and rumination that grow when meaningful action is continually deferred.
- **Momentum**: The increasing ease and evidence created by a first small action.
- **Readiness illusion**: The belief that important work should begin only after fear disappears.

## Mental Models

- Compare two discomforts: the discomfort of doing the thing and the discomfort of living without its result.
- If the same average day repeated, its pattern—not its intention—would become your life.
- Use one small action as a reality test; do not ask thought to prove what action alone can show.
- Comfort is not the absence of pain; it can be the long-term peace of doing what matters.

## Anti-patterns

- **Waiting for readiness**: Meaningful opportunities rarely arrive after fear has politely left.
- **Confusing reflection with progress**: Thinking about work can soothe anxiety while leaving the work untouched.
- **Overestimating the task**: Avoidance enlarges difficulty; small steps reveal that many desired actions are also enjoyable.
- **Protecting today at the expense of the year**: Immediate relief can accumulate into lost work, connection, and identity.

## Worked Example

Wiest asks the reader to measure a year by what would happen if today—or an average day—were repeated indefinitely. The questions include what would be accomplished, how many hours would be wasted, which relationships would be missed, and whether one would regret not beginning a creative project or a vulnerable relationship. The exercise turns an abstract “later” into a concrete comparison between action and avoidance.

## Key Takeaways

1. Name the comfort that resistance is protecting.
2. Calculate the life produced by repeating the current day.
3. Act in a small way before you feel ready.
4. Let action generate a new way of thinking.
5. Choose the discomfort that serves the larger life.
