# Essay 19: The Little Things You Don’t Realize Are Affecting How You Feel About Your Body

## Core Idea

Body image is built through accumulated social inputs rather than through the body alone. Wiest names parental comments, edited media, early intimate experiences, friends’ habits, cultural food judgments, catcalls, celebrity scrutiny, and the words used to insult other people as forces that teach a person what is “normal,” desirable, or shameful. A genuine relationship and genuine friendships can shift attention away from appearance as the price of love or belonging. She also distinguishes health and function from genetic build and appearance, warning that comparison creates an unfinishable standard. The practical reorientation is to treat the body as the thing that lets one laugh, play, jump, hug, love, and live, while holding oneself to a personal standard rather than to commercial or social surveillance.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Body image as social learning**
  - When to use: A body judgment feels like an objective fact about your worth.
  - How: Trace the family, media, peer, romantic, cultural, and online sources that taught the standard.
- **Function over display**
  - When to use: Fitness or food choices are being used only to reach a different appearance.
  - How: Reconnect movement, food, sunlight, rest, and embodiment with health and what the body enables.
- **Relational de-commodification**
  - When to use: You believe love or attention depends on a particular shape.
  - How: Notice whether relationships value the whole person rather than treating the body as a commodity.

## Influences to Audit

1. Parents’ body talk and judgments.
2. Photoshop and edited images that distort “normal.”
3. Early partners’ appreciation or criticism.
4. The physical insults you reach for against others.
5. Friends’ actions and collective body habits.
6. Books, magazines, sites, and shows that define the ideal.
7. Heritage, hometown, food culture, and relatives’ comments.
8. Relationships that are—or are not—more than skin-deep.
9. Fitness framed as a means to another body.
10. Friendships maintained for convenience and exterior acceptability.
11. Street comments that reduce a body to a commodity.
12. Confusion between health, genetics, weight, and subjective size.
13. “Good” and “bad” food labels tied to appearance or moral worth.
14. Lack of outdoor time, warmth, and sunlight.
15. Having no more meaningful basis for self-worth.
16. Unrequited love and celebrity-body surveillance.
17. Forgetting that bodies evolved for living, not for chiseled display.

## Key Concepts

- **Body image**: A socially shaped interpretation of one’s body.
- **Social norm**: The collective standard absorbed from family, peers, media, or culture.
- **Body function**: What the body enables—movement, perception, connection, play, and love.
- **Genetic build**: The inherited variation that comparison cannot standardize.
- **Commodification**: Treating a person’s body as an object for consumption or judgment.

## Anti-patterns

- **Using comparison as a finish line**: A shifting standard guarantees insufficiency.
- **Moralizing food**: Good/bad labels can displace attention from health and lived nourishment.
- **Making fitness cosmetic only**: It turns a holistic practice into punishment for appearance.
- **Treating rejection as bodily proof**: Someone’s lack of interest does not establish a physical defect.
- **Borrowing celebrity surveillance**: Another person’s professionally scrutinized body is not a personal standard.

## Key Takeaways

1. Trace where a body standard came from before obeying it.
2. Judge health and function separately from appearance.
3. Choose relationships that recognize more than the body.
4. Spend less attention on comparison and more on what the body lets you do.
