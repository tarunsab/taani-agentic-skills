# Essay 9: 20 Signs You’re Doing Better Than You Think You Are

## Core Idea

This checklist redefines progress through ordinary evidence of independence, safety, connection, curiosity, growth, and resilience rather than through a dramatic success story. Paying bills, having food, clothing, a job, a small pleasure, or a space of one’s own are treated as real achievements when they represent survival and responsibility. Questioning yourself, sensing that something is wrong, changing from a year ago, or not being rigid about the future are signs of openness rather than failure. Close friends matter more than a large social tally, and losing a relationship can show that someone refused to settle. The final measure is not that life became easy but that experience made you more capable of reassuring, caring for, and directing yourself.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Ordinary evidence of progress**
  - When to use: Your life seems unimpressive because it does not match an exceptional ideal.
  - How: Inventory the concrete capacities and freedoms that make daily life safer, richer, or more self-directed.
- **Growth through questioning**
  - When to use: Doubt or miserable days feel like proof that you are regressing.
  - How: Read self-questioning as evidence that you remain open to another way.
- **Flexible future**
  - When to use: You feel behind because the future is not fixed exactly as planned.
  - How: Keep goals while allowing the ability to make the present situation into an ideal.

## Key Concepts

- **Independence**: Meeting basic needs and taking responsibility for yourself.
- **Material safety**: Food, clothing, transport, shelter, and spare capacity beyond bare survival.
- **Tribe**: A small number of close relationships that provide intimacy and acceptance.
- **Self-awareness**: The ability to sense that something is wrong before knowing the complete solution.
- **Self-care knowledge**: Knowing what sleep, support, pleasure, and recovery you need.
- **Flexible commitment**: Working toward a dream without making one exact outcome the condition of a good life.
- **Earned reassurance**: Using past survival and learning to reassure the younger self.

## Progress Inventory

1. Bills are paid, perhaps with room for a nonessential.
2. You question yourself and remain open to another way.
3. You have work that supports food, shelter, and independence.
4. You have some time for enjoyment.
5. Your next meal is not a source of fear.
6. You can eat for pleasure, not only survival.
7. One or two close friends know and accept you.
8. A small daily convenience is affordable.
9. You are different from a year ago and can name how.
10. You have time or means beyond the bare minimum.
11. You have clothing for varied weather and circumstances.
12. You can sense what is not right even before knowing the fix.
13. You could tell your younger self that you survived.
14. You have some private space you govern.
15. You have lost relationships rather than settling indefinitely.
16. Something still interests you enough to explore.
17. You know how to care for yourself when tired, heartbroken, or unwell.
18. You are working toward a goal, even if it is vague or distant.
19. You are not rigidly committed to one exact future.
20. You have survived difficult experiences and become more capable.

## Anti-patterns

- **Measuring only by status**: It hides the progress represented by safety, care, and responsibility.
- **Counting relationships**: Quantity cannot substitute for intimacy or acceptance.
- **Treating doubt as failure**: Self-questioning can be the mechanism of growth.
- **Demanding a fixed future**: Rigidity makes it harder to turn the actual present into a good life.

## Key Takeaways

1. Count capacities, not only milestones.
2. Give ordinary survival and self-care their full evidentiary weight.
3. Let curiosity, change, and questioning register as progress.
4. Use your past resilience as proof that you can meet the next difficulty.
