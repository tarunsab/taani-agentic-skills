# Essay 16: 8 Cognitive Biases that Are Creating the Way You Experience Your Life

## Core Idea

Wiest begins with the claim that confidence in a belief reflects the coherence of the mind’s story more than the quality of its evidence. Because people experience the world through their senses and psyches, they mistake subjective output for objective reality—a form of naïve realism. Cognitive biases then select, magnify, or organize information before it becomes a felt “truth.” Recognizing the bias does not provide access to a perfectly objective world; it creates enough distance to question the story and make a more deliberate choice. The eight examples are projection, extrapolation, anchoring, negativity, conservatism, clustering illusion, confirmation, and choice-supportive bias.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Naïve realism**
  - When to use: Your interpretation feels like the only reasonable description of reality.
  - How: Remember that you experience the mind’s output, compare perspectives, and treat certainty as a story property rather than proof.
- **Projection**: Assume others think as you do and read the world through your own preferences.
- **Extrapolation**: Turn a present condition into a forecast of the whole life.
- **Anchoring**: Let the first information set the range of what seems possible.
- **Negativity bias**: Give bad news and dramatic destruction disproportionate attention.
- **Conservatism**: Prefer an old belief to newer information because it was accepted first.
- **Clustering illusion**: See meaningful patterns in random events because attention is seeking confirmation.
- **Confirmation bias**: Select evidence that supports an existing belief and ignore what challenges it.
- **Choice-supportive bias**: Judge what you chose more favorably and discount its flaws.

## Key Concepts

- **Subjective reality**: The experienced output of perception, memory, desire, and interpretation.
- **Naïve realism**: The assumption that one’s perception is an objective representation of the world.
- **Anchor**: The first reference point that narrows later judgment.
- **Confirmation**: Selective attention to evidence supporting a preconception.
- **Choice-supportive evaluation**: Positive reinterpretation of an option because it was selected.
- **Pattern detection**: The mind’s tendency to organize random events into meaningful clusters.

## Mental Models

- Ask what information arrived first and whether it is still deserving of authority.
- When a present moment feels permanent, label the leap from condition to life story.
- Seek disconfirming evidence before treating agreement as truth.
- Separate “I chose it” from “it has no flaws.”

## Anti-patterns

- **Mistaking confidence for evidence**: A coherent story can be wrong and still feel certain.
- **Treating attention as neutral**: Repeated exposure to negative or dramatic material reshapes perceived importance.
- **Overreading coincidence**: A pattern can be created by selective attention rather than the world.
- **Defending a choice at all costs**: Autonomy does not require denying the selected option’s weaknesses.

## Key Takeaways

1. Treat perception as an output to inspect, not reality in final form.
2. Name the bias shaping a judgment before acting on it.
3. Look for information that would change your mind.
4. Do not let a first frame or present mood define the possible future.
