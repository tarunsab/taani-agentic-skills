# Essay 26: The One Question to Ask Yourself If You’re Tired of Fighting for Someone’s Love

## Core Idea

Wiest says culture devotes enormous attention to getting love—landing dates, eliciting commitment, and manipulating how someone sees us—but too little to giving love. A person cannot convince someone else to love them, and trying to change an unwilling person keeps the heart closed in denial. Love is not an external object that another person owns and grants; it is an experience requiring equal, honest, willing contribution. The key question when self-pity arises over receiving too little is: how much are you giving? Giving does not mean remaining devoted to an unavailable or disrespectful person; it can mean loving honestly, walking away, respecting yourself, and letting many forms of love teach you how to love better.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Getting love versus giving love**
  - When to use: Your attention is fixed on earning another person’s affection or commitment.
  - How: Ask what love you are offering, what exchange is actually mutual, and whether leaving is the more honest act.
- **No-convincing rule**
  - When to use: Someone has shown they do not want to express affection, respect, or commitment.
  - How: Stop trying to manipulate the fact; protect your openness and move forward.
- **Love as experience**
  - When to use: Rejection has become proof that you are unlovable.
  - How: Separate another person’s willingness from your capacity to experience and give love.

## Key Concepts

- **Love acquisition**: The cultural project of trying to secure another person’s affection.
- **Love contribution**: The active, honest, and willing offering one brings to an exchange.
- **Closed heart**: The pain of resisting love or manipulating someone into changing.
- **Equal exchange**: Mutual contribution required for a healthy relationship.
- **Love story**: A relationship or encounter that teaches how to give, receive, choose, and leave.
- **Graceful departure**: Walking away without turning rejection into a verdict on worth.

## Mental Models

- Love is not a prize to earn from someone else; it is a way of relating that requires reciprocity.
- If you must manipulate someone into affection, the relationship is already denying the needed exchange.
- Ask what the relationship is teaching rather than whether it can promise forever.
- Giving love includes giving yourself honesty and the freedom to leave.

## Anti-patterns

- **Trying to manufacture affection**: It produces denial and keeps you stuck.
- **Treating withdrawal as proof of unlovability**: Another person’s choice is not a complete account of you.
- **Confusing devotion with strength**: Staying in an unequal exchange is not automatically loving.

## Worked Example

Wiest contrasts two responses to unreturned affection: keep trying to change the person, or continue to give love in a way that lets you move on. In her broader image, a life can contain a series of small love stories—from a brief encounter to a long relationship—that each teach self-knowledge, intuition, generosity, and graceful departure. The example preserves love’s value without making permanence the test.

## Key Takeaways

1. Ask “How much am I giving?” when self-pity about love appears.
2. Do not negotiate someone into wanting a mutual exchange.
3. Keep rejection separate from worth.
4. Treat leaving honestly as one form of love.
