# Chapter 20: How Every Ending Is a Beautiful Beginning

## Core Idea

The final chapter asks when a relationship problem can be accepted and when it reveals incompatibility or a deal breaker. Robbins’s standard for a lasting partnership is mutual: both people want the relationship to work and are willing to work on it, and the issues do not require either person to abandon a dream or compromise a core value. The first question is whether you love the person as they are now or only the person they were or might become; if the trait is tolerable, Let Them be and stop punishing them with resentment. The ABC(DE) Loop adds a decision phase after influence: give the issue time without negative energy, Decide whether it is a Deal Breaker, then End the bitching or End the relationship. The London-versus-Atlanta and children-versus-no-children examples show that commitment can coexist with genuine incompatibility when a choice touches a life dream. Robbins cites the Gottmans’ research that 69 percent of relationship problems are not resolvable and often reflect enduring differences or unfulfilled dreams, so accepting a partner requires honest clarity about what you can compromise. If the relationship ends, heartbreak is grief woven through the nervous system and daily routines; Let Them and Let Me do not erase it, but no-contact, environmental changes, support, a full calendar, and a self-chosen challenge can help the process. The chapter closes by redefining self-love as responsibility and choice: you are the one person you spend your entire life with, and care for that relationship sets the standard for every other one.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **ABC(DE) Loop**:
  - **A — Apologize and Ask**: Own pressure and ask open questions about the issue.
  - **B — Back off and observe Behavior**: Stop punishing, model positive change, and watch what happens.
  - **C — Celebrate and model Change**: Reinforce sincere progress without making it a condition.
  - **D — Decide the Deal Breaker**: Ask whether you can live with the issue if it never changes.
  - **E — End the bitching or End the relationship**: Choose acceptance with kindness or leave honestly.
- **Love the person, not their potential**: Assume the current person may remain current.
- **Breakup reset**: Let grief happen while reducing triggers and deliberately rebuilding daily life.

## Key Concepts

- **deal breaker**: An issue you cannot live with for the rest of your life.
- **compatibility**: Fit between two people’s enduring values, dreams, and desired way of living.
- **commitment**: Willingness to choose and work on a relationship, which does not guarantee compatibility.
- **unfulfilled dream**: A deeper hope underneath recurring gridlock or conflict.
- **resolvable problem**: An issue that can be addressed through mutual work without abandoning core values.
- **heartbreak as grief**: The loss of a shared future and the routines encoded in the nervous system.
- **no contact**: The chapter’s therapist-derived 30-day rule to reduce reactivation of old relationship patterns.
- **self as the love of your life**: The responsibility to treat yourself with respect, care, and choice across every relationship status.

## Mental Models

- Ask “Could I love and choose this person if nothing changed?” before demanding more influence.
- Distinguish surface annoyance from a core dream or value that cannot be traded away.
- After an ending, time alone does not heal; the actions taken during that time help rebuild a life.
- Water the relationship you choose, but do not call a different garden greener when the issue is incompatibility.

## Anti-patterns

- **Silent resentment**: Staying while complaining, hinting, or punishing is not accepting the partner.
- **Confusing commitment with compatibility**: Love and effort cannot solve every conflict over children, home, or life direction.
- **Staying for potential**: Treating a future change as present reality prolongs the standoff.
- **Revenge self-improvement**: Exercising or changing appearance to win back an ex keeps the ex at the center.
- **Rushing grief**: “Loving yourself” as a slogan can dismiss the actual grief and nervous-system disruption of a breakup.

## Worked Example

Chris’s organization and punctuality contrast with Robbins’s ADHD-related messes, lost keys, and lateness. He has asked for change and she has tried, but the pattern remains. The chapter says Chris can either accept her as she is and stop using the behavior as a daily indictment, or recognize that he cannot live with it and choose the relationship accordingly. A different kind of impasse appears when one partner must move to London and the other must remain near family: both may love each other, but neither should quietly abandon a core dream.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use ABC before making a permanent judgment about a changeable relationship issue.
2. If the person never changes, either love the actual person without chronic complaint or leave.
3. Treat dreams about location, children, values, and life direction as compatibility questions.
4. In heartbreak, reduce triggers, ask for support, fill the calendar, and choose a challenge for yourself.
5. Do not make self-care a performance designed to influence an ex.
