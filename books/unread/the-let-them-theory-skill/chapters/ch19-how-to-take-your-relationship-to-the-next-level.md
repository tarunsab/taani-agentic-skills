# Chapter 19: How to Take Your Relationship to the Next Level

## Core Idea

This chapter handles two different problems: repeatedly choosing unavailable people and wanting a particular person to commit. If the pattern is constant, Robbins says the relationship may be happening largely as a fantasy of potential, shaped by earlier experiences; she recommends being single for a period and addressing the root with therapy rather than using a new relationship as the cure. When the issue is one current relationship, the solution is an honest commitment conversation focused on the value of your time and the future you want, not on persuading or guilting the other person. Matthew Hussey’s story of Audrey is used to show the power of a calm, complimentary, expectation-free statement that she would invest further only if their visions matched. Let Them gives the other person freedom to say no; their answer is valuable information, not a challenge to overcome. Let Me then chooses whether to leave rather than accept “table scraps” of attention, sex, or companionship. The chapter’s hard boundary is that a clear refusal to commit should be believed, and staying after a repeated pattern may signal a deeper issue to explore.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Pattern audit**: Identify whether you repeatedly chase unavailable, controlling, unfaithful, or rescuing relationships.
  - When to use: The same commitment failure appears with different people.
  - How: Name the pattern, pause dating if needed, and seek the underlying issue rather than changing partners.
- **Commitment conversation**: Ask whether the other person shares your vision while valuing your own time.
  - When to use: You want exclusivity, a label, cohabitation, marriage, children, or another next step.
  - How: Talk in person without time pressure; describe what you want, invite the truth, and accept either answer.
- **Table-scraps test**: Decide whether partial access is enough when the desired commitment is unavailable.

## Key Concepts

- **unavailable partner**: Someone emotionally, practically, or explicitly unwilling to make the commitment you want.
- **relationship pattern**: A repeated choice and dynamic that recurs across partners.
- **commitment conversation**: A direct clarification of shared vision, timing, and investment.
- **value of time**: The principle that your finite time and energy deserve a relationship aligned with your aims.
- **expectation-free clarity**: Asking for truth without treating the desired answer as owed.
- **table scraps**: The chapter’s image for accepting less than the mutual commitment you actually want.

## Mental Models

- A clear no is a gift of information; it ends the need to interpret.
- Focus on what you are choosing with your time, not on how to win the other person.
- If a pattern repeats, changing partners without changing yourself preserves the pattern.

## Anti-patterns

- **Hinting instead of asking**: Indirectness protects ambiguity and prolongs anxiety.
- **Making the conversation a sales pitch**: Explaining why you are lovable does not create the other person’s readiness.
- **Treating yourself as the exception**: Repeated evidence should outweigh the fantasy that this partner will suddenly be different.
- **Accepting indefinite partial commitment**: Staying can be a choice, but the chapter asks you to own its cost.

## Worked Example

Audrey tells Matthew that she enjoys their relationship but values her time and wants commitment; she is willing to continue investing only if they share that direction. The statement compliments the connection, makes no threat, and leaves the door open to a no. Matthew is forced to answer the actual question rather than receive more persuasion. The reader is then asked to let the answer determine the next choice.

## Key Takeaways

1. Audit repeated attraction to unavailable or unhealthy partners.
2. Ask directly for the commitment you want without trying to control the answer.
3. Treat a refusal as data and do not accept partial access as a substitute for mutuality.
4. Use time single, therapy, and self-reflection to change a recurring relationship pattern.
