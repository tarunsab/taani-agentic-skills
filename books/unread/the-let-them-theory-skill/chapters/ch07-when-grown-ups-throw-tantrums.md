# Chapter 7: When Grown-Ups Throw Tantrums

## Core Idea

The chapter argues that other adults’ guilt trips, silent treatment, rage, pouting, and passive aggression often drive decisions because the reader is trying to regulate someone else’s emotions. Therapist Anne Davin gives Robbins the image of an eight-year-old inside an adult body: many adults have never learned to understand or communicate feelings directly, so they use childlike strategies while appearing more sophisticated. The image is intended to create compassion and clarity, not contempt or an excuse to tolerate abuse. The book distinguishes children, whose physical and emotional regulation is an adult’s responsibility, from adults, who own their emotions and the consequences of how they express them. It also treats sadness and disappointment after a loss, firing, or breakup as normal responses, while chronic avoidance and suppression can build distress. Emotions are described as fast chemical bursts that rise and fall; the practical move is Let Them rise, then Let Me refrain from the impulsive text, drink, snack, or confrontation until the wave passes. The same work applies when the reader is the immature one: notice the emotion, process it without acting it out, and keep practicing rather than demanding perfection.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Adult childlike behavior**: Recognize adult avoidance, sulking, silence, or rage as emotional-regulation failures rather than commands you must obey.
  - When to use: Someone’s reaction makes you feel responsible for soothing or fixing them.
  - How: Let Them react, visualize the younger emotional state, and choose boundaries or distance.
- **Let Them rise / Let Me not react**: Allow your own emotion to be felt without immediately turning it into behavior.
  - When to use: Anger, hurt, grief, or disappointment is rising.
  - How: Notice physical sensations, do not reach for an escape or send a message, and wait for the wave to fall.

## Key Concepts

- **emotional maturity**: A learned capacity to understand, communicate, and regulate feelings.
- **silent treatment**: Withdrawing communication to make another person pursue, soothe, or guess what is wrong.
- **emotional contagion**: Another person’s tone, energy, or mood triggering a similar state in your body.
- **emotion wave**: The book’s image for an emotion that rises and, if not fed by reaction, falls.
- **normal disappointment**: Sadness or frustration that fits a loss or blocked desire and is not itself evidence of mental illness.
- **boundary**: A choice about what behavior you will participate in or remain around.

## Mental Models

- Compassion can replace fear of someone’s outburst, but compassion does not require continued access to you.
- Ask whether the feeling fits the circumstance and whether the behavior is a chosen expression or an attempt to control you.
- When you are the problem, treat self-recognition as the beginning of skill-building, not a reason for shame.

## Anti-patterns

- **Soothing every adult tantrum**: Giving in teaches the other person that dysregulation controls the interaction.
- **Suppressing every uncomfortable emotion**: Avoidance and numbing may make the wave last longer and can create new problems.
- **Diagnosing or excusing from a metaphor**: “Eight-year-old” is a perspective-taking tool, not a clinical label.

## Worked Example

Robbins recalls a high-school friend who stopped speaking to her without explaining why. Robbins called, apologized for unknown offenses, and felt relief when the friend resumed contact, so the pattern continued. She later interprets the silence as the friend’s inability to process and communicate emotion, not proof that Robbins had done something wrong. The Let Them response is to stop chasing the silence, choose whether to address it, and protect herself from a relationship organized around guessing and soothing.

## Key Takeaways

1. Another adult’s emotions are not yours to manage.
2. Let Them react while you decide whether to communicate, set a limit, or leave.
3. Let your own emotions be felt without giving them an immediate behavioral outlet.
4. Practice emotional maturity as a lifelong skill, including compassion toward your own lapses.
