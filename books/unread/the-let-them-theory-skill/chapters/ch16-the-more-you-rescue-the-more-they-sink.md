# Chapter 16: The More You Rescue, The More They Sink

## Core Idea

This chapter adds an urgent boundary to Let Them: if an adult is dangerous or in immediate crisis, step in, take the keys, call for help, contact emergency services, or do what is necessary to make the situation safe. Outside immediate safety intervention, however, rescuing can keep a struggling person from facing the work and consequences that recovery requires. People who are depressed, grieving, addicted, ashamed, or in denial may not be ready to get better, and a supporter cannot want sobriety or healing more than the person who must do it. The book distinguishes support from enabling: money, excuses, and taking over can shield an adult from reality, prolong dependence, and make lying easier. It uses a ball metaphor in which support throws the person the means to act, while enabling grabs the ball and runs whenever they drop it. Dr. Robert Waldinger’s principle is to let people learn from life rather than shielding them from every consequence, though the response must account for children and young adults who still require guidance. The chapter’s most compassionate stance is to believe in another person’s capacity while refusing to do their healing for them.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Safety exception**: Do not simply let dangerous or immediately self-destructive behavior continue.
  - When to use: There is risk of driving impaired, crisis, serious harm, or another urgent threat.
  - How: Intervene, get professional help, and prioritize safety before applying the adult-responsibility principle.
- **Support versus enabling**: Offer tools and care without removing the person’s responsibility to use them.
  - When to use: Your help repeatedly prevents natural consequences or substitutes for treatment.
  - How: Stop covering, funding, or solving; remain available for responsible support.
- **Ball metaphor for healing**: Throwing the ball is support; running the field for someone is rescue.

## Key Concepts

- **rescue**: Taking over another person’s problem to relieve their pain or your own distress.
- **enabling**: Shielding an adult from consequences through money, excuses, or concealment.
- **natural consequence**: A real result of a person’s choice that may teach what avoidance hides.
- **readiness**: The struggling person’s own willingness to face the work of recovery.
- **survival mode**: A state in which depression, addiction, grief, or crisis narrows capacity for healthy choice.
- **avoidance coping**: Escaping a painful task or feeling in a way that strengthens the fear or problem.

## Mental Models

- Ask whether your action makes the hard thing possible or makes avoidance easier.
- Love can mean refusing the shortcut that keeps a person dependent.
- For children, guidance is a duty; for adults, care must not erase responsibility.

## Anti-patterns

- **Confusing money with support**: Funding an adult’s harmful pattern can prolong rather than solve it.
- **Lying to make the person look okay**: Concealment protects the problem from consequences and accountability.
- **Treating every distress as an emergency**: Immediate safety action and long-term rescue are not the same.
- **Abandoning someone in the name of Let Them**: The book explicitly says support does not mean leaving a struggling person alone.

## Worked Example

When Robbins’s daughter is anxious at night, Robbins initially lets her sleep on the floor of the parents’ room for six months. This reduces distress in the moment but teaches avoidance and makes the anxiety spread to school, travel, and ordinary activities. With a therapist’s guidance, Robbins validates the fear, walks her daughter back to her own room, stays nearby, and repeats the process until the child learns she can face the feeling. The example is a parenting case: the parent remains responsible for the child while supporting strength rather than making avoidance permanent.

## Key Takeaways

1. Intervene when safety is at stake; do not confuse this with solving every adult problem.
2. Let adults face consequences that may create the motivation to change.
3. Offer love, tools, and presence without taking over the work.
4. Treat avoidance as a process that can grow stronger when repeatedly rewarded.
