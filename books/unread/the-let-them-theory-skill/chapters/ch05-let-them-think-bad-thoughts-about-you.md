# Chapter 5: Let Them Think Bad Thoughts about You

## Core Idea

This chapter addresses the fear that other people’s judgments will prevent you from living your “one wild and precious life.” Robbins argues that adults will have negative opinions regardless of how carefully you behave, so trying to control those opinions is both impossible and expensive. Anticipated judgment becomes a life map: it makes people overthink captions, hide their work, stay silent, procrastinate, and reject themselves before anyone else has done so. The proposed move is unusually direct: give people permission to think badly of you, then give yourself permission to act according to your values. Robbins’s speaking-business story shows the cost of refusing that permission; she knew the formula for building a career but spent two years drafting and deleting social posts because friends might think she was arrogant or phony. The chapter also distinguishes a negative thought from a negative relationship: loved ones can be annoyed, critical, or wrong about you and still love and respect you. Let Them therefore clears the noise of anticipated judgment, while Let Me makes the visible, imperfect, courageous move that your goals require.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Permission to be judged**: Assume other adults may think negative thoughts and stop treating that possibility as a veto.
  - When to use: You are hiding, editing, delaying, or seeking approval before acting.
  - How: Ask whose opinion you fear, let them have it, and take the next value-aligned step.
- **Formula before originality**: Use a proven pattern instead of avoiding action to look completely different.
  - When to use: You are launching work, a business, art, or public communication.
  - How: Follow the working structure, then make it yours through your execution and voice.
- **Two things can be true**: A person can think something critical and still love you.

## Key Concepts

- **fear of judgment**: Anticipation of negative opinion that changes behavior before any actual rejection.
- **self-rejection**: Censoring, deleting, hiding, or staying silent so others never get to see the real attempt.
- **opinion road map**: Choosing a path based on predicted reactions instead of values or goals.
- **perfectionism**: An overcontrolled attempt to remove every possible reason for criticism.
- **proven formula**: A repeatable structure that has worked for others and can be personalized.
- **proud-of-yourself standard**: Evaluating decisions by whether they align with your values, not universal approval.

## Mental Models

- You cannot manage another adult’s private thoughts, so do not make imagined approval a requirement.
- Visibility is a practice: repeated exposure to possible judgment is more useful than perfect preparation.
- The meaningful question is not “Will they approve?” but “Will I respect how I showed up?”

## Anti-patterns

- **“Stop caring” without a method**: Suppressing concern leaves the fear intact; permission gives it a concrete response.
- **Deleting the imperfect attempt**: Removing a post or idea to manage likes reinforces the cycle of self-rejection.
- **Reinventing the wheel to avoid looking derivative**: Fear of appearing copied can block a tested route to progress.

## Worked Example

Robbins is advised to build a simple speaker website, collect testimonials, and post consistently about speaking. She completes the site and testimonials but cannot post because her account is mostly family photos and she imagines friends asking who she thinks she is. She drafts hundreds of captions, checks reactions, and deletes posts that do not receive the approval she expected. The Let Them approach is to let friends think she is arrogant or copied, use the working marketing formula, and Let Me share the work that can produce the career she wants.

## Key Takeaways

1. Grant adults freedom to judge, doubt, disapprove, or misunderstand you.
2. Move by your values and goals rather than by a forecast of everyone’s reaction.
3. Remember that criticism is not automatically a withdrawal of love.
4. Use proven structures and make them distinctive through your own work.
