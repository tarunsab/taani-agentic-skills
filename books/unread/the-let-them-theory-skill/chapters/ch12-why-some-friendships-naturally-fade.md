# Chapter 12: Why Some Friendships Naturally Fade

## Core Idea

Robbins uses a painful personal story to show how friendship can change when proximity, timing, and energy shift. When a close friend moved across the street from two couples in Robbins’s neighborhood, Robbins expected the four families to become one group. The three households with children the same age naturally began seeing one another more often, and invitations to Robbins’s family became less frequent. She initially treated the change as theft, became jealous and bitter, and brought such negative energy to gatherings that she could understand in retrospect why people withdrew. Let Them would have allowed the other families to be friends; Let Me would have required Robbins to process her feelings and change how she showed up. The chapter then warns against using the theory to sever relationships too quickly: a friend who stops texting may be overwhelmed by caregiving, work, illness, an unhealthy relationship, or a new life chapter. Reach out because you care rather than to keep score, assume good intent where possible, and use the three pillars to decide whether the friendship needs patience, reconnection, or release.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Friendship-fade review**: Check proximity, timing, and energy before concluding a friendship is over.
  - When to use: Contact becomes one-sided or a group no longer includes you.
  - How: Ask what changed in each life, assume good intent, and observe the current pattern.
- **No-scorekeeping friendship**: Offer contact because you want to, not as a transaction that demands an equal reply.
  - When to use: A friend responds slowly or cannot make plans.
  - How: Reach out without using response speed as a measurement of care.
- **Flexible reconnection**: Leave room for a relationship to resume when circumstances change.

## Key Concepts

- **friendship red flag**: Robbins’s description of her own bitter, insecure, visibly negative behavior around the group.
- **one-sided effort**: A pattern in which one person always calls or plans and no effort appears when they stop.
- **assume good intent**: Interpret distance cautiously because unseen burdens may explain it.
- **loss of touch**: A period of distance that does not necessarily mean the relationship has become hostile.
- **relationship energy**: The emotional tone each person brings, which can strengthen or repel connection.
- **flexibility**: Allowing friendship access and intensity to change with life conditions.

## Mental Models

- A changed invitation pattern is data, not a verdict.
- Ask “what changed?” before asking “who is to blame?”
- A friend can be absent from your routine and still not be your enemy.

## Anti-patterns

- **Blaming people for taking your place**: Proximity can make new routines emerge without a plot.
- **Punishing distance with bitterness**: Negative energy can create the exclusion you fear.
- **Blowing up a friendship over slow replies**: Response speed often reflects capacity, not love.
- **Keeping a friendship ledger**: Tit-for-tat expectations turn care into an accounting system.

## Worked Example

Robbins’s neighborhood group once revolved around children, school, sports, and shared weekends. After another family moved across the street from two couples, everyday proximity created a new triangle, and Robbins reacted by pouting, pretending not to care, and venting to Chris. Later she recognizes that no one needed to have excluded her intentionally and that her energy had become difficult to bring into the group. A calmer response would have been to Let Them be close, process the hurt, and either reach out or accept the changed season.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use the three pillars to understand a fade before making it personal.
2. Assume good intent and remember that you rarely know another person’s burden.
3. Reach out because caring is your choice, not because a reply is owed.
4. Let distance exist without converting it into hatred or permanent closure.
