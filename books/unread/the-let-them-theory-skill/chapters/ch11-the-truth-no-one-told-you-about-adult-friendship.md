# Chapter 11: The Truth No One Told You about Adult Friendship

## Core Idea

Robbins argues that adult friendship feels difficult because childhood trains people to expect friendship to happen automatically. School, sports, parents, dorms, shared schedules, and repeated proximity supplied a ready-made group, so friendship functioned as a group sport. In the twenties and later, the **Great Scattering** removes that structure: people move, work different jobs, marry, have children, divorce, retire, or enter new chapters on different timelines. Loneliness then gets misread as personal rejection when it may simply reflect changed conditions. The chapter identifies three invisible pillars of adult friendship: proximity, timing, and energy. Proximity creates repeated contact, timing creates shared life context, and energy is the felt click that can shift as people grow; Robbins cites research in the book suggesting roughly 74 hours for a casual friendship and over 200 for a close one. Let Them makes room for friends to move, respond slowly, or change seasons, while Let Me adopts the flexible and proactive responsibility required to create connection again.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Great Scattering**: The transition from built-in childhood friendship structure to dispersed adult lives.
  - When to use: A friend group has scattered and you feel left behind or excluded.
  - How: Treat the change as normal, then rebuild connection deliberately.
- **Three Pillars of Friendship**:
  - **Proximity**: Physical closeness creates repeated opportunities to interact.
  - **Timing**: Shared life stage creates common context and capacity.
  - **Energy**: Mutual ease or click determines how naturally a connection develops.

## Key Concepts

- **group sport**: Childhood friendship supported by common institutions, schedules, and constant contact.
- **individual sport**: Adult friendship requiring personal initiative and calendar coordination.
- **Great Scattering**: The recurring dispersal of friends through geography and life transitions.
- **proximity**: How often people are physically near each other.
- **timing**: The life chapter or developmental stage each person occupies.
- **energy**: The felt fit between people, which can improve or weaken over time.
- **friendship season**: A period in which a person is present for a particular reason or stage rather than forever.

## Mental Models

- Before declaring a friendship personal, check which pillar changed.
- Expect friendship to evolve rather than requiring every relationship to remain a “best friend” bond.
- Adult friendship is a design problem: allocate time and create repeated opportunities.

## Anti-patterns

- **Expecting childhood structure to continue**: Adult life no longer guarantees invitations or daily contact.
- **Treating every shift as betrayal**: Distance can follow geography, schedules, stage, or energy rather than malice.
- **Gripping a forced friendship**: Trying to recreate an old fit can drain both people.

## Worked Example

Robbins contrasts high school, where friends shared buses, classes, teams, and milestones, with adult life, where friends scatter across cities and calendars. She also compares a beloved family friendship with older grandparents who live far away: love remains, but proximity and timing limit depth. The examples show that a friendship can be real without being equally available in every chapter.

## Key Takeaways

1. Name the Great Scattering before judging your adult friendships.
2. Check proximity, timing, and energy when a relationship feels different.
3. Let people come and go without turning ordinary change into an enemy story.
4. Accept that close friendship takes substantial repeated time and intentionality.
