# Patterns

## Let Them + Let Me
**When to use**: Another adult’s behavior, mood, opinion, or choice is taking over your attention.
**How**: Name what is outside your control; say Let Them; pause; say Let Me; choose the response that matches your values.
**Trade-offs**: It releases futile control but still leaves responsibility for boundaries, conversations, action, or exit.

The first phrase creates distance from the trigger; the second prevents that distance from becoming passivity. The book repeatedly uses the pair to move from reaction to choice. The sequence is useful for both small irritations and painful relationship facts. It is not a reason to ignore danger, discrimination, abuse, or a needed conversation.

## Stress Reset
**When to use**: You notice agitation, an urge to send a regrettable message, or a body-level stress response.
**How**: Say Let Them, pause, say Let Me, and take a slow breath before deciding whether to act.
**Trade-offs**: Breathing can restore deliberation, but it does not change the external stressor.

The book frames stress as a brain-and-body state in which the amygdala’s survival response can displace the prefrontal cortex’s planning. The pause is therefore a reset, not a demand to pretend the trigger is harmless. The example of the coughing passenger shows the choice between escalating and taking practical steps to protect oneself. The method is strongest when followed by a concrete, proportionate action or a conscious decision to disengage.

## Time-Horizon Test
**When to use**: You are unsure whether a frustrating event deserves more energy.
**How**: Ask whether it will bother you in an hour, a week, or a year; act when the issue will persist or matter, and release passing irritation.
**Trade-offs**: Letting a minor event pass protects energy, while persistent or values-relevant issues require action.

Mel uses the dog-on-the-trail example to show that Let Them is not one fixed response. Reporting the behavior, addressing it, cleaning up, or walking away can all be valid Let Me choices depending on energy, time, values, and consequences. The test helps distinguish present-moment annoyance from a recurring problem. It is a prioritization aid, not a rule against speaking up.

## Frame of Reference
**When to use**: Someone’s disapproval feels like a verdict on your worth or love.
**How**: Ask what history, fear, expectation, or lived experience could make their view coherent to them; then decide how to respond.
**Trade-offs**: Understanding can create compassion and connection, but it does not require agreement or continued access.

The book’s example is Mel’s mother reacting to a marriage that would take her daughter far from home. Seeing the mother’s history reframes apparent lack of support as fear and grief, while preserving Mel’s right to choose. The pattern makes room for two people to be right from different lenses. It should not be used to excuse bigotry or harmful conduct.

## 5 Whys
**When to use**: You are pressuring someone to change and need to find the real source of your frustration.
**How**: Ask yourself “Why does this bother me?” and repeat the question five times, writing each answer.
**Trade-offs**: It can expose fear, judgment, or loss of control, but the result is personal and need not be shared.

The book borrows the method associated with Sakichi Toyoda to move below the surface complaint. In the health example, annoyance becomes concern about modeling, attraction, loss, and fear of losing a spouse. Seeing the deeper fear helps the supporter apologize and release pressure. It prepares the more compassionate conversation in the ABC Loop.

## ABC Loop
**When to use**: You want to influence an adult’s change without creating a control standoff.
**How**: Apologize for judging and Ask open questions; Back off and observe Behavior; Celebrate progress while modeling Change.
**Trade-offs**: It can lower friction and support intrinsic motivation, but the person may not change.

The conversation is meant to be in person, without alcohol or time pressure, and with full listening rather than a debate. Open questions help the person hear the discrepancy between what they want and what they do; the supporter is not there to deliver a verdict. Space gives tension time to become motivation, while immediate positive acknowledgment links difficult action with pleasure. The book recommends patience, including six months or more for influence, and later uses three months as a relationship test.

## Support Without Rescue
**When to use**: Someone is struggling and your help may be removing the work or consequences they need to face.
**How**: Separate safety intervention from everyday rescue; offer love, practical support, boundaries, and conditions while letting the adult own recovery.
**Trade-offs**: Stepping back can feel cruel, but unconditional money or excuses can prolong the problem.

The book’s ball metaphor treats support as offering the means to act, while enabling means taking over whenever the person drops it. Conditional funding, environmental help, and steady presence can support independence without pretending the supporter can heal someone else. The response changes for children, whose physical and emotional needs remain the adult’s responsibility. Immediate danger is an explicit exception to simply letting someone continue.

## Go First and Build Weak Ties
**When to use**: You are lonely, new somewhere, or waiting for friendship to happen.
**How**: Say hello, learn names, show curiosity, invite a promising connection out of its setting, and repeat without demanding an instant friendship.
**Trade-offs**: Awkward moments are likely, but repeated low-pressure contact creates community.

The book presents the coffee-shop contact list, the doorstep visit to Mia, and recurring walking, ski, and class groups as examples of proactive friendship. Familiar strangers become social scaffolding before they become close friends. The pattern depends on patience and no scorekeeping. Its explicit time frame for a new place or life chapter is to give it a year.

## ABC(DE) Relationship Decision
**When to use**: You have communicated a need, modeled change, waited, and the issue remains.
**How**: Decide whether the unchanged issue is a deal breaker; then end the chronic complaining or end the relationship.
**Trade-offs**: Acceptance can preserve a good relationship, while leaving may protect a core dream or value.

The book asks whether you could stay if the person never changed. If yes, choose them as they are and stop punishing them for the trait. If no, continuing to bitch, hint, or resent is neither acceptance nor love. The London/Atlanta and children/no-children examples show that commitment does not erase incompatibility. The decision belongs to the reader, not to the partner’s potential.
