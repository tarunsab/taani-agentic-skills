# Glossary

**ASML** — Dutch equipment company that became the sole supplier of extreme ultraviolet lithography systems for leading-edge chips (Chs. 32, 39).

**Arm** — Processor instruction-set company whose licensable architecture enabled low-power mobile and embedded designs without requiring each customer to invent an instruction set (Ch. 24).

**CUDA** — Nvidia's software platform for programming its GPUs, an example of a software ecosystem that strengthens hardware advantage (Ch. 48).

**DARPA** — U.S. defense research agency whose funding helped create demand and technical support for advanced electronics (Chs. 5, 14).

**DRAM** — Dynamic random-access memory, a high-volume memory category whose economics are shaped by density, yield, and brutal commodity cycles (Chs. 18–20, 50).

**EDA** — Electronic-design automation: software used to design, simulate, verify, and prepare chip layouts for fabrication (Chs. 12, 36, 40).

**EUV** — Extreme ultraviolet lithography, the advanced patterning technology needed for the smallest leading-edge features (Ch. 39).

**Fab** — Semiconductor fabrication plant, an extremely expensive and process-intensive facility where wafers become chips (Chs. 4, 35, 37).

**Fabless** — A company that designs chips but outsources wafer fabrication to a foundry; the model lets design firms avoid owning fabs (Chs. 22, 36).

**FinFET** — Three-dimensional transistor structure that improved control of current as planar scaling became harder (Ch. 55).

**Foundry** — A company that manufactures chips designed by other companies; TSMC made the pure-play version strategically important (Chs. 21, 37).

**GPU** — Graphics processing unit, a parallel processor that became central to AI computation (Chs. 24, 48).

**Grand Alliance** — Morris Chang's ecosystem of customers, suppliers, engineers, investors, and governments around a trusted foundry model (Ch. 37).

**Integrated circuit** — A circuit containing many transistors and other components on one piece of semiconductor material (Ch. 3).

**Lithography** — The process of projecting patterns onto a wafer so that microscopic circuit features can be formed (Chs. 3, 32, 39).

**Logic chip** — A processor or other chip that performs computations, distinct from memory that stores data (Introduction, Ch. 55).

**Moore's Law** — The industry expectation, originating in Gordon Moore's observations, that transistor counts and computing capability would keep rising rapidly (Chs. 13, 55).

**NAND** — A nonvolatile flash-memory technology used for data storage; it competes on density, cost, and manufacturing scale (Ch. 19).

**Offset strategy** — A military approach that seeks an asymmetric advantage through technology such as precision, stealth, networking, or autonomy rather than matching an opponent's mass (Chs. 14, 26, 48).

**Paveway** — A family of laser-guided bombs used in the book as an example of low-cost precision enabled by electronics (Ch. 11).

**RISC** — Reduced instruction-set computing, an architecture approach that simplified processor instructions and opened opportunities for challengers to incumbent x86 designs (Ch. 22).

**RISC-V** — An open instruction-set architecture discussed as a possible route for reducing dependence on proprietary processor ecosystems (Ch. 52).

**Semiconductor** — A material whose electrical properties can be controlled to fabricate transistors and integrated circuits; colloquially, a chip (Introduction, Ch. 2).

**Silicon wafer** — A polished slice of silicon on which many integrated circuits are fabricated simultaneously (Chs. 3, 32).

**Supply-chain statecraft** — The use of production ties, investment, access, and dependency as instruments of national strategy (Ch. 12).

**TSMC** — Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, the leading pure-play foundry whose neutrality and process execution made Taiwan central to advanced logic production (Chs. 21, 37, 54).

**Transistor** — A controllable electronic switch; its miniaturization and mass production drive modern computing (Chs. 2–3).

**UMC** — United Microelectronics Corporation, a Taiwanese foundry involved in the Fujian Jinhua technology-transfer case (Ch. 50).

**Weaponized interdependence** — Strategic leverage gained when a state or firm occupies a central network position through which others must pass (Chs. 12, 49–51).

**Yield** — The share of manufactured chips that work; improving yield turns a technically possible process into an economically viable one (Chs. 4–5, 35, 37).

**x86** — Intel-originated processor architecture that dominated personal computers and servers before facing challenges from alternative architectures (Chs. 13, 22, 38).
