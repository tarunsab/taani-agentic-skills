# Chapter 17: “Shipping Junk”

## Core Idea

GCA Corporation’s fall shows that equipment suppliers can lose a technology race through management failure even when their invention is excellent. GCA pioneered the wafer stepper, which exposed each chip pattern step by step with greater precision than a scanner. Its CEO Milt Greenberg then overexpanded, mismanaged inventory, ignored the semiconductor cycle, and let customer service decay. Nikon, initially a lens supplier, listened to customers, reverse-engineered the stepper, and delivered equipment with better uptime and yields. GCA’s story was often told as an allegory of Japan defeating America, but Miller stresses the homegrown causes: unreliable machines, weak manufacturing, and arrogance. In precision industries, commercialization means making hundreds of dependable systems, not winning one technical demonstration.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Stepper model**: expose individual chip fields sequentially for greater lithographic accuracy.
  - When to use: when shrinking features makes whole-wafer scanning too imprecise.
- **Customer-uptime discipline**: treat serviceability, reliability, and user feedback as product features.
  - When to use: when a machine is embedded in a customer’s costly production line.

## Key Concepts

- **Photolithography**: using light, masks, lenses, and photoresist to pattern silicon.
- **Stepper**: lithography tool that moves field by field across a wafer.
- **GCA**: American lithography leader that lost to Nikon.
- **Yield**: working chips produced from a wafer.

## Mental Models

- Think of capital equipment as a service relationship, not a one-time sale.
- Ask whether an apparent national failure is actually a management failure.
- In cyclical markets, treat a boom as temporary unless demand evidence says otherwise.

## Anti-patterns

- **Spending ahead of the cycle without controls**: GCA entered the downturn overextended.
- **Monopolist behavior after the monopoly ends**: “buy what we build” drove customers to Nikon.

## Worked Example

GCA’s revenue fell by more than two-thirds when lithography sales dropped 40 percent from 1984 to 1986. Nikon then built its own stepper after GCA cut it out of the lens supply, and its machines achieved far longer continuous uptime for customers.

## Key Takeaways

1. Commercialize precision equipment through uptime and service.
2. Watch cycles and inventory as carefully as technical road maps.
3. Listen to customers before blaming foreign subsidies.
