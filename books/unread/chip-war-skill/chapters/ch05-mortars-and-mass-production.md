# Chapter 5: Mortars and Mass Production

## Core Idea

The chip industry’s decisive challenge was not only inventing transistors but producing them with consistent yields. TI’s Morris Chang and Fairchild’s manufacturing leaders learned that microscopic defects, impurities, chemical timing, and equipment variation could turn a promising design into scrap. Military orders gave firms the opportunity to learn, but commercial expansion required process control and cost reduction at much greater scale. The chapter also shows how managers such as Charlie Sporck translated engineering into factory routines, while entrepreneurs hunted for better materials, tools, and labor. Manufacturing became a source of competitive advantage in its own right. Moore’s Law therefore depended on organizational repetition as much as on clever circuit design.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Yield learning**: improve the proportion of fabricated chips that work by identifying and eliminating recurring process failures.
  - When to use: whenever a complex product is technically possible but too expensive to make reliably.
  - How: measure defects, standardize steps, improve equipment, and repeat at volume.
- **Process discipline**: convert tacit factory knowledge into repeatable routines.
  - When to use: when scale makes artisanal production too variable.

## Key Concepts

- **Yield**: the share of chips on a wafer that function correctly.
- **Wafer**: a disk of silicon on which many chips are fabricated.
- **Clean room**: a controlled environment minimizing particles and contamination.
- **Mass production**: repeatable fabrication that drives cost down through volume and learning.

## Mental Models

- Think of every defect as a tax on Moore’s Law.
- Use the “factory floor” as a source of innovation, not merely execution.
- Prefer cumulative process improvements over a single heroic breakthrough.

## Anti-patterns

- **Focusing only on transistor design**: production errors can erase the value of a better design.
- **Treating tacit knowledge as transferable by inspection**: the Soviet chapters show why copying a device does not reveal the recipe.

## Worked Example

TI’s work on guided electronics and missile systems forced engineers to confront the difference between a prototype and a dependable product. The firm’s ability to tune chemicals, materials, and assembly processes created the operational knowledge that made later mass markets possible.

## Key Takeaways

1. Measure yield and defects as strategic variables.
2. Build production learning into the innovation process.
3. Treat manufacturing managers as co-creators of technology.
