# Chapter 19: Death Spiral

## Core Idea

American semiconductor leaders feared that the country was entering a “death spiral” like Detroit, yet they disagreed on what government should do. Tax cuts and stronger chip intellectual-property protection helped the venture system, but neither solved the DRAM crisis. The 1986 U.S.-Japan trade deal raised prices and redistributed profits but could not restore most American memory producers. Sematech, created in 1987 with equal industry and Pentagon funding, tried to coordinate chipmakers and equipment suppliers and revive domestic lithography. It improved some technologies and practices, but failed to save GCA because a superior tool still lacked customers, trust, and a viable business model. Public coordination worked best where it strengthened a healthy ecosystem, not where it tried to rescue an exhausted incumbent.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Ecosystem coordination**: align chipmakers and tool suppliers around shared production schedules, reliability, and technical requirements.
  - When to use: when interdependent firms cannot invest effectively without visibility into one another’s road maps.
- **Capability versus company**: protect a national capability without assuming every existing firm deserves rescue.
  - When to use: when a strategic technology and its incumbent businesses are diverging.

## Key Concepts

- **Sematech**: U.S. semiconductor consortium funded by industry and the Pentagon.
- **Semiconductor Chip Protection Act**: U.S. law strengthening protection for chip layouts.
- **Dumping**: alleged below-cost foreign sales used to justify trade restrictions.
- **Lithography**: equipment-dependent process central to chip fabrication.

## Mental Models

- Use a capability test: what knowledge, tools, and suppliers must survive?
- Treat coordination as a network problem, not simply a subsidy problem.
- Distinguish temporary price relief from long-term competitiveness.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming tariffs rebuild know-how**: trade restrictions cannot substitute for manufacturing learning.
- **Saving a firm without a customer**: Sematech’s support could not make GCA’s market position credible.

## Worked Example

Sematech allocated 51 percent of its funding to American lithography because Noyce said lithography was “half the problem.” GCA’s new tools were later judged among the world’s best, yet the company lost $30 million between 1988 and 1992 and closed after Sematech withdrew support.

## Key Takeaways

1. Coordinate standards and schedules where interdependence is the bottleneck.
2. Use trade policy as one instrument, not the whole strategy.
3. Invest in viable capabilities and customers, not only distressed firms.
