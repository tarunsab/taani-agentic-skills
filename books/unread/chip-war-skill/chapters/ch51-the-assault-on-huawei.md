# Chapter 51: The Assault on Huawei

## Core Idea

The U.S. campaign against Huawei was an application of “weaponized interdependence”: use control over network nodes and semiconductor choke points to constrain a rival inside a global system. A first ban on U.S.-made goods was insufficient because Huawei designed chips with U.S. software and had them fabricated in Taiwan using U.S. equipment. The 2020 rule extended restrictions to foreign-made products produced with U.S. technology, effectively reaching TSMC and most advanced chipmaking. Huawei’s smartphone and server businesses then lost access to essential chips, while China chose not to retaliate fully. The chapter shows both the power and the danger of interdependence: networks can encourage cooperation, but their hubs can become coercive weapons.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Weaponized interdependence**: a state exploits central nodes in a network to impose costs on actors that depend on them.
  - When to use: when a supply chain has concentrated hubs and extraterritorial reach.
- **Rules plus intent**: apply export controls according to both their technical wording and the policy purpose behind them.
  - When to use: when a foreign firm can route around a direct product ban.

## Key Concepts

- **Huawei**: Chinese telecom and smartphone company targeted by U.S. restrictions.
- **Weaponized interdependence**: political use of network dependence.
- **Extraterrestrial reach**: controls applying to foreign products made with U.S. technology.
- **ASML**: sole EUV producer whose access to U.S. technology supports the choke.

## Mental Models

- Follow the recipe of a product backward to find the jurisdictional lever.
- Treat software, tools, and foundries as connected control points.
- Ask whether a sanction removes capability or merely shifts suppliers.

## Anti-patterns

- **Targeting only the final product** when the production process is the real lever.
- **Assuming interdependence automatically moderates conflict**: network hubs can intensify it.

## Worked Example

In May 2020, Washington restricted chips made for Huawei with U.S. technology, not only chips physically made in America. Because TSMC relied on U.S. equipment and Huawei relied on U.S. design software, the rule cut Huawei off from most advanced chip production.

## Key Takeaways

1. Map network centrality before designing sanctions.
2. Coordinate controls across software, equipment, and manufacturing.
3. Expect coercive leverage to alter commercial ecosystems.
