# Chapter 1: From Steel to Silicon

## Core Idea

The chapter contrasts the industrial logic of World War II with the semiconductor revolution that followed it. The United States won the war through mass production of steel, ships, planes, and ammunition, but the next contest would be shaped by technologies that made military systems more precise and information-rich. The childhood experiences of Akio Morita, Morris Chang, and Andy Grove place the future chip industry inside the destruction, migration, and anti-Communist politics of the postwar world. Silicon Valley emerged because scientific talent, defense funding, venture finance, and an unusually open labor market reinforced one another. Its later international reach was built through Asian partners that gained capability while remaining dependent on U.S. tools, software, customers, and capital.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **From steel to silicon**: strategic advantage moves from the volume of physical platforms to the quality of the information controlling them.
  - When to use: when a sector appears small relative to heavy industry but may determine the performance of many systems.
  - How: ask whether miniaturization, sensing, and computation change what existing weapons or products can do.
- **Innovation ecosystem**: scientific expertise, manufacturing know-how, capital, customers, and talent mobility must reinforce one another.
  - When to use: when explaining why an invention becomes an industry in one place and not another.

## Key Concepts

- **Silicon Valley**: a regional network combining research, startups, venture capital, and semiconductor manufacturing expertise.
- **Industrial attrition**: a war logic based on producing more physical goods than an opponent.
- **Talent mobility**: the movement of skilled people across firms and borders.
- **Defense procurement**: an early source of demand and funding for chips.

## Mental Models

- Use an ecosystem lens when a single inventor cannot explain scale.
- Think of migration as a technology-transfer mechanism, not merely a demographic fact.
- Distinguish the first customer that funds a technology from the larger market that ultimately sustains it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating invention as sufficient**: Miller emphasizes that manufacturing, markets, and managers made the technology consequential.
- **Assuming national capability is self-contained**: the U.S. advantage depended on foreign talent and later on allied production networks.

## Worked Example

The chapter follows Morita, Chang, and Grove from wartime displacement into the chip world: Morita from Japan’s ruined cities, Chang through repeated flight across China and Hong Kong, and Grove through Nazi and Soviet occupation of Hungary. Their later roles show how the industry’s global character was present in its leadership from the beginning.

## Key Takeaways

1. Look for the shift from mass to precision when assessing a new strategic technology.
2. Map the institutions that turn research into repeatable production.
3. Treat migration and openness as part of the productive infrastructure.
