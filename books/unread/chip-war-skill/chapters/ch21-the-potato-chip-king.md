# Chapter 21: The Potato Chip King

## Core Idea

Jack Simplot, an Idaho potato entrepreneur, recognized that DRAM had become a commodity precisely when Silicon Valley’s experts were abandoning it. His investment in Micron was a contrarian bet based on commodity-cycle experience: buy when prices are depressed and others are liquidating. Micron survived by designing smaller chips, reducing process steps, modifying equipment, loading more wafers per furnace, and enforcing severe cost discipline. The company’s advantage was not a glamorous breakthrough but a relentless ability to make a working memory chip more cheaply. Micron shows that a late entrant can win by making the economics of production its central technology.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Commodity-cycle contrarianism**: enter a commodity business when price pressure drives capable competitors out, provided costs can be structurally lowered.
  - When to use: when the market is depressed but the product remains necessary.
- **Lowest-cost producer of the highest-quality product**: combine cost reduction with enough performance and reliability to win customers.
  - When to use: in brutal price competitions where premium branding cannot protect margins.

## Key Concepts

- **Micron**: Idaho DRAM company backed by Jack Simplot.
- **Cost curve**: relative production cost that determines survival in a commodity market.
- **DRAM**: temporary memory whose production requires scale and constant investment.
- **Process simplification**: removing manufacturing steps to reduce time and defects.

## Mental Models

- Think like a farmer: prices and capacity cycles can reveal the best entry point.
- Look for physical design changes that improve factory economics, not only chip density.
- Treat survival cash as a technical constraint.

## Anti-patterns

- **Leaving a necessary commodity because prices are low**: low prices can be the moment to buy capability.
- **Chasing prestige over cost**: Micron survived through unglamorous process work.

## Worked Example

Micron shrank the chip itself so more chips fit on a wafer, used fewer process steps, modified lithography tools, and increased furnace loads from 150 to 250 wafers. In 1981 it had only two weeks of payroll cash, yet later turned Simplot’s first $1 million into a billion-dollar stake.

## Key Takeaways

1. Study cycles before declaring a market permanently lost.
2. Make cost reduction a design and process discipline.
3. Preserve liquidity long enough for the cycle to turn.
