# Chapter 22: Disrupting Intel

## Core Idea

Andy Grove recognized that Intel’s DRAM business had been disrupted and that the company had to disrupt itself or fail. He and Gordon Moore reluctantly abandoned the memory identity they had built when IBM’s personal computer revealed a growing market for Intel microprocessors. Grove then imposed “constructive confrontation,” layoffs, factory discipline, and a “copy exactly” manufacturing method derived from Japanese practice. The approach replaced local improvisation with standardized processes and raised yields. Intel’s recovery also benefited from the rise of PC clones, Microsoft’s software, and the x86 ecosystem. The chapter’s enduring lesson is that self-disruption is emotionally hardest when the old business still defines the company.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Only the paranoid survive**: fear of competition, bankruptcy, error, and loss can motivate decisive adaptation.
  - When to use: when evidence says the current model cannot recover.
- **Disrupt yourself**: abandon an identity and resource base before a rival makes the decision for you.
  - When to use: when the old market is structurally lost and an adjacent market is emerging.
- **Copy exactly**: replicate a proven manufacturing process across facilities instead of locally optimizing it.
  - When to use: when consistency and yield matter more than individual plant experimentation.

## Key Concepts

- **Andy Grove**: Intel leader who forced the DRAM-to-microprocessor pivot.
- **Microprocessor**: general-purpose processor central to PCs and servers.
- **x86**: instruction set that became the PC standard.
- **Constructive confrontation**: Grove’s method of resolving disagreement through direct challenge.

## Mental Models

- Ask what a new CEO would do if the board replaced you; Grove used this to expose denial.
- Separate identity from the capability worth preserving.
- Standardize proven process knowledge before optimizing local variations.

## Anti-patterns

- **Waiting for a cyclical recovery when structural disruption has occurred**.
- **Protecting a profitable identity at the expense of the next market**.

## Worked Example

Grove and Moore stared toward the Great America Ferris wheel in 1985, hoping DRAM would recover. Grove finally asked what a replacement CEO would do; Moore admitted that person would exit memories. Intel did so, adopted “copy exactly,” and won the PC processor market as IBM clones spread.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use fear as a signal to test the business model, not as a reason to panic.
2. Make the hard strategic exit while resources remain.
3. Standardize the factory once the process is known to work.
