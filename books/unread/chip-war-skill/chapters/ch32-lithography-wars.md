# Chapter 32: Lithography Wars

## Core Idea

The semiconductor industry faced three simultaneous lithography contests: which light source could pattern smaller features, which company could mass-produce the tools, and which countries would control access to the technology. Intel funded extreme ultraviolet research because existing 193-nanometer methods could not sustain transistor shrinkage indefinitely. ASML, a small Dutch spinoff, won by assembling components from a global supplier network rather than making everything in-house. Its neutrality, Philips connection to TSMC, and ability to integrate many suppliers made it a credible alternative to Japanese Canon and Nikon. U.S. policymakers treated the technology as commercial during the post–Cold War “unipolar moment,” but manufacturing control became monopolized even as scientific knowledge globalized.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Three lithography wars**: engineering, commercial concentration, and geopolitical control interact in a strategic tool market.
  - When to use: when a frontier technology depends on both technical breakthroughs and supply-chain ownership.
- **Integrator advantage**: orchestrate specialized suppliers rather than internalize every component.
  - When to use: when no single firm can build the full system efficiently.

## Key Concepts

- **EUV**: extreme ultraviolet lithography at a 13.5-nanometer wavelength.
- **ASML**: Dutch lithography company that became the sole EUV producer.
- **Canon/Nikon**: Japanese lithography competitors.
- **193nm lithography**: deep-ultraviolet method extended by optical tricks.

## Mental Models

- Analyze frontier tools through technical, commercial, and geopolitical layers.
- Distinguish global scientific collaboration from monopolized manufacturing.
- Prefer an ecosystem integrator when component specialization is extreme.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming borderless science implies distributed production**: ASML’s toolchain became a monopoly.
- **Treating frontier tools as ordinary trade goods**: access can determine every downstream chip.

## Worked Example

Intel gave Carruthers $200 million for EUV research in 1992 and later invested $4 billion directly in ASML. ASML integrated thousands of components and absorbed SVG, America’s remaining major lithography firm, while the U.S. government allowed the technology to move abroad.

## Key Takeaways

1. Track who controls the tool that enables the next node.
2. Invest early when a technology has no plausible substitute.
3. Reassess geopolitical implications when a “global” project creates one supplier.
