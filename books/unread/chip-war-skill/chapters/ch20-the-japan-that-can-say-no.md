# Chapter 20: The Japan That Can Say No

## Core Idea

Akio Morita’s confidence in Japanese industrial superiority culminated in The Japan That Can Say No, coauthored with nationalist politician Shintaro Ishihara. The book argued that Japan’s engineers, managers, and semiconductor producers had earned a position equal to or above the United States. Its geopolitical force came from the claim that Japanese chips were essential to American military accuracy and could therefore be used as leverage. Yet Japan’s semiconductor dominance rested on overinvestment in DRAMs, cheap bank credit, and an inability to pivot toward microprocessors or new memory categories. The chapter turns semiconductor market share into a warning about how economic capability can create political ambition—and how quickly that capability can erode.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **High tech is foreign policy**: industrial leadership in critical technology changes diplomatic and military power.
  - When to use: when commercial dependence is large enough to constrain state choices.
- **Overinvestment trap**: cheap capital and status incentives can keep firms expanding an unprofitable commodity business.
  - When to use: when all competitors invest to avoid losing share, even as returns deteriorate.

## Key Concepts

- **The Japan That Can Say No**: Morita and Ishihara’s 1989 nationalist argument.
- **Pax Niponica**: forecast of an Asia led by Japan.
- **DRAM**: memory segment in which Japan briefly dominated.
- **Flash/NAND**: nonvolatile memory Toshiba discovered but Intel commercialized.

## Mental Models

- Treat industrial concentration as potential geopolitical leverage, but test its durability.
- Compare cheap financing’s growth benefits with its ability to postpone exit.
- Look for the adjacent market a dominant incumbent is failing to enter.

## Anti-patterns

- **Equating market share with durable power**: Japan’s DRAM position collapsed when the market shifted.
- **Defending the old category too long**: Japanese firms missed microprocessors and flash memory.

## Worked Example

Japan’s DRAM share fell from about 90 percent in the late 1980s to 20 percent by 1998, while South Korea overtook it. Toshiba’s Fujio Masuoka had developed flash memory in 1981, but Intel—not Toshiba—brought it to market.

## Key Takeaways

1. Treat high-tech leadership as strategically consequential.
2. Monitor overinvestment and adjacent-market neglect.
3. Recheck geopolitical claims against the durability of the underlying business.
