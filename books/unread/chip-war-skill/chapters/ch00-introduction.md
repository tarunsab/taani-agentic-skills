# Introduction: Chip War

## Core Idea

Miller frames semiconductors as the hidden infrastructure of modern economic, military, and geopolitical power. The industry’s achievement is not only scientific: it depends on manufacturing discipline, supply-chain specialization, entrepreneurial finance, and markets large enough to pay for relentless research. The contemporary system is extraordinarily efficient but concentrated in choke points, especially Taiwanese fabrication, Korean memory production, U.S. design software and equipment, and Dutch EUV lithography. That concentration makes a disruption in Taiwan or a clash between China and the United States an economic event of global scale.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Silicon over steel**: computing, sensors, and precision guidance can substitute information advantage for mass in military competition.
  - When to use: when assessing a technology’s strategic value beyond its commercial price.
  - How: trace the entire chain from design and materials to fabrication, assembly, and the systems that consume the chip.
- **Choke-point analysis**: power follows irreplaceable inputs controlled by very few firms or countries.
  - When to use: when evaluating resilience, sanctions, or geopolitical leverage.
  - How: identify the steps that cannot be quickly substituted and the dependencies behind them.

## Key Concepts

- **Semiconductor**: a material platform on which microscopic transistors are fabricated.
- **Moore’s Law**: the observed expectation that transistor density and computing power keep rising rapidly.
- **Foundry**: a manufacturer that fabricates chips designed by other companies.
- **Supply-chain statecraft**: using investment and production ties to bind countries politically.
- **Logic chip**: a processor that computes.
- **Memory chip**: a chip that stores data.

## Mental Models

- Think of the chip industry as a network of bottlenecks, not a single national factory.
- Use “who supplies the next indispensable step?” to connect technical capability to political leverage.
- Treat cost reduction and manufacturing yield as innovations, not merely operational details.

## Anti-patterns

- **Equating “tech” with software or consumer brands**: this hides the physical dependencies that make digital services possible.
- **Assuming globalization means diffusion**: the book’s central warning is that production can globalize while control concentrates.

## Worked Example

The introduction traces a representative chip: an Arm-based design from California and Israel, U.S. design software, Japanese wafers and gases, Taiwanese fabrication using Dutch, Japanese, and Californian tools, Southeast Asian testing, and Chinese final assembly. A break at any one point threatens the flow of new computing power. TSMC’s share of advanced production and ASML’s monopoly on EUV make the network’s political geography visible.

## Key Takeaways

1. Study semiconductors as both technology and political economy.
2. Separate design, tools, materials, fabrication, packaging, and assembly when assessing dependence.
3. Efficiency and vulnerability can be produced by the same concentration.
