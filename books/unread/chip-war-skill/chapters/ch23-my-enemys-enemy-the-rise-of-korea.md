# Chapter 23: “My Enemy’s Enemy”: The Rise of Korea

## Core Idea

Samsung entered semiconductors through a combination of Korean state support, conglomerate financing, and American technology. Lee Byung-Chul saw that Korea could use lower costs to challenge Japan, while U.S. firms viewed Korean DRAM makers as allies against their Japanese rivals. Samsung licensed Micron technology, recruited American-trained engineers, and invested through downturns when competitors could not. Trade restrictions on Japanese dumping unintentionally opened market space for Korean firms. The strategy was geopolitical as well as commercial: “my enemy’s enemy is my friend” made Korea a useful counterweight. Samsung’s eventual leadership shows that a partner supported for strategic reasons can become a durable competitor.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Enemy’s-enemy partnership**: support a second competitor to prevent one rival from controlling a strategic market.
  - When to use: when no domestic firm can immediately regain leadership.
- **Sustained investment game**: in DRAM, stopping capital expenditure even briefly can surrender the next technology generation.
  - When to use: when scale and learning compound across successive nodes.

## Key Concepts

- **Samsung**: Korean conglomerate that became a DRAM leader.
- **Chaebol**: Korean conglomerate structure with political and banking ties.
- **DRAM**: memory market in which Samsung competed with Japan and Micron.
- **Technology licensing**: legal acquisition of production knowledge from a rival or partner.

## Mental Models

- Use alliance logic to explain why a firm may help create its future competitor.
- Compare rivals by who can sustain investment through a downturn.
- Treat licenses, talent, and bank credit as a combined capability package.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming the sponsored partner remains dependent**: Samsung learned and accumulated its own capability.
- **Stopping investment at the wrong node**: memory leadership rewards continuous spending.

## Worked Example

Samsung licensed Micron’s 64K DRAM technology and “whatever we did, Samsung did,” Micron founder Ward Parkinson recalled. Intel also entered joint ventures with Samsung because Korean costs could undermine Japan’s strategy of flooding the market.

## Key Takeaways

1. Strategic partnerships can reshape industry structure.
2. Make technology transfer feed local learning, not just assembly.
3. In scale races, financial endurance is part of engineering.
