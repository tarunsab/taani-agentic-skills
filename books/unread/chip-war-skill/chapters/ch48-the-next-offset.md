# Chapter 48: The Next Offset

## Core Idea

China is trying to turn the Pentagon’s 1970s offset against the United States by using precision missiles, air defenses, cyberwar, anti-satellite weapons, and electronic warfare. The next offset depends on “intelligentized” warfare: AI, autonomy, distributed sensors, and connected machines. Georgetown researchers describe AI’s required triad as data, algorithms, and computing power; China is competitive in the first two but still depends heavily on foreign chips for the third. The U.S. is responding with autonomous platforms, human-machine teaming, distributed computing, and DARPA’s Electronics Resurgence Initiative. Yet the Pentagon buys too small a share of the market to control the ecosystem and must rely on foreign foundries. The strategic problem is that the U.S. wants a technological offset while China is embedded in the same supply chain.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **AI triad**: data, algorithms, and computing power must all be present for AI capability.
  - When to use: when evaluating an AI race beyond model quality.
- **Next offset**: use AI, autonomy, sensors, and networks to compensate for China’s numerical military advantages.
  - When to use: when platform-for-platform competition is unfavorable.
- **Zero trust in microelectronics**: assume chips may be compromised and verify their integrity.
  - When to use: when defense systems use commercial or foreign-manufactured components.

## Key Concepts

- **Intelligentized warfare**: Chinese term for AI-enabled military systems.
- **Human-machine teaming**: people and autonomous systems sharing sensing and decision work.
- **Electromagnetic spectrum**: contested medium for radar, communications, and jamming.
- **Trusted foundry**: supplier judged suitable for sensitive military chips.

## Mental Models

- Evaluate AI by the full triad, not by algorithms alone.
- Treat computing access as a military resource like fuel or ammunition.
- Prefer distributed systems that can keep operating when links or GPS are disrupted.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming autonomy is only a software problem**: it requires chips, sensors, power, communications, and manufacturing.
- **Using off-the-shelf components without verification** in systems where tampering or flaws are consequential.

## Worked Example

DARPA’s imagined human-machine team pairs a piloted fighter with autonomous drones that provide additional eyes and ears. The drones require chips for AI, radio-frequency processing, navigation, and communication, while U.S. officials worry that many of these chips are fabricated in Taiwan.

## Key Takeaways

1. Analyze AI capability as data, algorithms, and compute.
2. Build resilient distributed systems and verify their hardware.
3. Align military ambitions with actual industrial access.
