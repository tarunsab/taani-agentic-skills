# Chapter 25: The KGB’s Directorate T

## Core Idea

Directorate T institutionalized Soviet efforts to acquire Western equipment and technology. The KGB stole chips, bought them through intermediaries, recruited insiders, and even acquired large quantities of manufacturing machinery. These operations produced impressive intelligence but weak industrial results because a fab needs a complete, maintained system of equipment, spare parts, materials, process knowledge, and trained people. Vladimir Vetrov’s “Farewell” disclosures revealed the scale of the bureaucracy, while Operation Exodus tightened Western controls. Soviet replicas of Intel and Motorola chips appeared with clockwork regularity, but were always about half a decade behind. Espionage could shorten information gaps; it could not replace the learning system behind mass production.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Acquisition without assimilation**: obtain designs and machines faster than an organization can understand, maintain, and improve them.
  - When to use: when judging whether espionage or procurement will close a manufacturing gap.
- **Complete-system dependency**: advanced production depends on the whole toolchain, including spares and service.
  - When to use: when a single stolen artifact appears to solve a complex industrial problem.

## Key Concepts

- **Directorate T**: KGB division for acquiring Western technology.
- **Vladimir Vetrov/Farewell**: KGB source who exposed Directorate T.
- **Operation Exodus**: Reagan-era effort to restrict illicit technology transfers.
- **Industrial espionage**: clandestine acquisition of commercial or technical secrets.

## Mental Models

- Map dependencies behind a device before estimating the value of stealing it.
- Measure lag in production cycles, not only access to information.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming a machine is self-sufficient**: without spares and process integration, it becomes an isolated artifact.
- **Celebrating replica output as parity**: the Soviet system reproduced old designs while the frontier moved on.

## Worked Example

The KGB reportedly acquired hundreds of machines for materials, lithography, etching, doping, packaging, and testing, yet Soviet factories still struggled with defects and maintenance. A stolen TI-like semiconductor found in a North Atlantic listening buoy demonstrated access to designs, not the ability to compete at scale.

## Key Takeaways

1. Audit the system around a technology, not just the technology.
2. Treat service, spares, and tacit knowledge as strategic assets.
3. Expect copying to produce lag when the target keeps improving.
