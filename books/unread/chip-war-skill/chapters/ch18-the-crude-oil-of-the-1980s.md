# Chapter 18: The Crude Oil of the 1980s

## Core Idea

When Jerry Sanders called semiconductors the “crude oil of the 1980s,” he was arguing that chips had become an essential input into computers, weapons, cars, and consumer electronics. The analogy gained force because the U.S. had experienced the economic pain of foreign oil embargoes and feared similar dependence on Japanese memory chips and lithography tools. The Pentagon confirmed the strategic stakes: electronics absorbed an increasing share of military spending, and semiconductors were the foundation of electronic superiority. Bob Noyce, Jack Kilby, and other industry leaders therefore returned to Washington seeking coordinated action. The shift marked Silicon Valley’s reluctant admission that markets and national security could not be separated.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Strategic commodity**: an input is strategic when its absence can disable a much larger economy or military system.
  - When to use: when deciding whether supply concentration warrants public action.
  - How: trace downstream dependence, substitutability, time to rebuild, and military consequences.
- **Industry-government compact**: private firms seek public help when foreign competition threatens the industrial base.
  - When to use: when an industry’s commercial market is also a national-security foundation.

## Key Concepts

- **Semiconductor Industry Association**: industry group formed to lobby Washington.
- **Strategic commodity**: input whose control affects economic and military power.
- **Electronics intensity**: rising share of military expenditure devoted to electronics.
- **Foreign dependence**: reliance on external producers for critical technology.

## Mental Models

- Compare chips to oil through substitutability and choke points, not rhetoric.
- Ask whether the government is protecting a capability or simply incumbent firms.

## Anti-patterns

- **Using “strategic” as a free pass**: the label must be connected to concrete system dependence.
- **Assuming a prior lead persists**: Perry’s offset assumed Silicon Valley would remain ahead.

## Worked Example

The Pentagon task force summarized the chain: U.S. forces rely on technological superiority; electronics are the most leveraged technology; semiconductors lead electronics; and U.S. defense would soon depend on foreign sources for state-of-the-art chips.

## Key Takeaways

1. Define strategic value through downstream consequences.
2. Reassess industrial assumptions when supply shares shift quickly.
3. Align public support with capability, not nostalgia.
