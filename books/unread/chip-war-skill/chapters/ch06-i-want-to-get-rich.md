# Chapter 6: “I… WANT… TO… GET… RICH”

## Core Idea

Fairchild Semiconductor created a culture in which technical talent, stock options, job mobility, and the prospect of personal wealth accelerated experimentation. Its founders had escaped William Shockley’s domineering startup and then left Fairchild when the parent company’s East Coast ownership limited autonomy and refused meaningful equity. The “traitorous eight” and their successors made entrepreneurship contagious: employees could leave, recruit colleagues, and start another firm nearby. Venture capital and public markets supplied the money to repeat the cycle. Miller’s point is not that greed alone caused innovation, but that incentives and mobility let technical knowledge multiply across firms.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Spinout engine**: a successful firm becomes a platform for new companies when employees can leave with knowledge and ambition.
  - When to use: when evaluating whether an ecosystem can renew itself.
  - How: combine talent mobility, equity ownership, capital access, and nearby customers.
- **Market expansion through price cuts**: lower prices can enlarge demand enough to outweigh lost margin per unit.
  - When to use: when a technology’s adoption is limited by cost.

## Key Concepts

- **Fairchild Semiconductor**: the company that industrialized silicon transistors and seeded Silicon Valley.
- **Traitorous eight**: engineers who left Shockley to found Fairchild.
- **Stock option**: equity incentive tying employees to future firm value.
- **Venture capital**: risk capital for startups with uncertain but scalable growth.

## Mental Models

- Think of talent as a compounding asset when people can carry know-how into new firms.
- Use “margin versus market size” to judge whether price reduction can unlock a new category.

## Anti-patterns

- **Centralizing ownership and credit**: Shockley’s control and Fairchild’s distant parent weakened retention.
- **Assuming a startup ecosystem is created by money alone**: mobility, trust, and technical density also mattered.

## Worked Example

Fairchild cut some integrated-circuit prices from about $20 to $2, sometimes selling below cost, and won large civilian computer orders as a result. The company’s refusal to grant equity helped motivate employees to leave and form new firms, including Intel.

## Key Takeaways

1. Design incentives that let contributors share in long-term value.
2. Expect spinouts when talent and capital are geographically concentrated.
3. Use price as a market-creation lever when volume can compound.
