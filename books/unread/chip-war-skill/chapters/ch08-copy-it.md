# Chapter 8: “Copy It”

## Core Idea

Alexander Shokin’s order to “copy it” captured the Soviet Union’s strategy for catching up in semiconductors. Copying a circuit could reproduce its visible design, but not the hidden sequence of chemical, optical, materials, and quality-control decisions that made it reliable at scale. Western firms were advancing rapidly under Moore’s Law, so a copied design was obsolete by the time Soviet factories could reproduce it. COCOM restrictions made advanced equipment and pure materials harder to obtain, while the Soviet economy lacked complementary suppliers and civilian demand. The result was a system that was always several years behind, even when its scientists understood the theory.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Recipe versus artifact**: a finished product reveals less than the process knowledge required to manufacture it.
  - When to use: when deciding whether reverse engineering can close a capability gap.
  - How: identify tacit know-how, supplier networks, equipment maintenance, and the pace of rival improvement.
- **Moving-target trap**: catching up fails when the target keeps improving faster than the follower can copy.
  - When to use: in sectors with short technology cycles.

## Key Concepts

- **COCOM**: Western coordination restricting technology transfers to Communist countries.
- **Tacit knowledge**: practical know-how not fully captured in documents.
- **Reliability**: consistent operation across large volumes and harsh conditions.
- **Backwardness**: a widening gap produced by slow learning, not merely a lack of information.

## Mental Models

- Think of reverse engineering as copying a cake without the recipe, oven settings, or trained baker.
- Use a moving-target test: compare the follower’s cycle time with the leader’s improvement cadence.

## Anti-patterns

- **Equating stolen design files with capability**: production equipment and process control remain missing.
- **Using bureaucracy to manage a fast-moving technology**: centralized approvals slowed adaptation.

## Worked Example

Soviet engineers were given a Texas Instruments SN-51 integrated circuit and three months to reproduce it exactly. They copied the design and even used inches in some machinery, but the broader process yielded fewer working chips because equipment and materials were inferior.

## Key Takeaways

1. Audit the complete production recipe, not just the product artifact.
2. In fast cycles, copying yesterday’s design can institutionalize backwardness.
3. Complementary suppliers and civilian markets are part of the capability.
