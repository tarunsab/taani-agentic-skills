# Chapter 53: Shortages and Supply Chains

## Core Idea

The COVID-era chip shortage made semiconductor dependence visible, but Miller argues that it was mainly a demand shock rather than proof that global chip supply chains had failed. PC and data-center demand surged, carmakers canceled orders, then returned when capacity had been reassigned; just one missing chip could stop a vehicle. The world produced more chips than ever in 2021, while auto manufacturers lost millions of units because just-in-time purchasing left little margin. The deeper lesson is about profits and power: choke points work because a few firms control them, and governments now compete to attract fabrication capacity. The U.S., Taiwan, South Korea, Japan, Europe, and Singapore all want more share, but advanced capacity cannot expand everywhere at once.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Demand-shock diagnosis**: distinguish a change in orders from a physical failure of the production network.
  - When to use: when shortages appear after a sudden market reversal.
  - How: compare total output, capacity allocation, cancellations, and inventory before blaming supply.
- **Strategic diversification**: add capacity in alternative locations without assuming every fab can match the frontier.
  - When to use: when concentration creates rare but catastrophic risks.

## Key Concepts

- **COVID chip shortage**: disruption caused largely by demand swings and just-in-time auto purchasing.
- **Just-in-time**: inventory model with little buffer against demand or supply variance.
- **Choke point**: concentrated input that can constrain a whole system.
- **Industrial policy**: state support for domestic production and R&D.

## Mental Models

- Diagnose shortages with supply, demand, and allocation separately.
- Diversify the most consequential bottlenecks first.
- Remember that allies compete for the same fabrication investment.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating every shortage as a broken global supply chain**: output grew during the shortage.
- **Assuming allied interests are identical**: countries want different shares of design, tools, memory, and fabrication.

## Worked Example

Auto firms produced an estimated 7.7 million fewer cars in 2021, a $210 billion revenue loss, after canceling chip orders early in the pandemic and then failing to regain capacity. Semiconductor production nevertheless rose 13 percent to more than 1.1 trillion devices.

## Key Takeaways

1. Separate demand allocation from supply failure.
2. Add buffers where one missing component stops a system.
3. Treat capacity-building as geopolitical bargaining among allies.
