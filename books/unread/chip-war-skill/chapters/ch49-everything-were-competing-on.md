# Chapter 49: “Everything We’re Competing On”

## Core Idea

By the late 2010s, U.S. officials increasingly understood that semiconductors underpinned every major area of competition with China. The chip industry itself was caught in a bind: China was its largest customer while also trying to replace it with state-supported firms. Washington’s inherited policies of globalization and “running faster” delayed action because leaders assumed technology would diffuse and that U.S. innovation would remain ahead. National-security officials instead saw semiconductor choke points as leverage and began treating export controls as strategic tools. The ZTE episode showed that access to U.S. chips could threaten a major Chinese firm’s survival, but it also exposed the risks of mixing trade tactics with technology strategy.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Semiconductor cornerstone**: the foundation beneath many economic, technological, and military contests.
  - When to use: when prioritizing industrial policy or security controls.
- **Run faster versus control diffusion**: innovation can preserve a lead only if rivals cannot compel or acquire the underlying capability.
  - When to use: when a competitor benefits from the same global ecosystem.

## Key Concepts

- **China hawks**: U.S. officials who framed technology competition as strategic and zero-sum.
- **Export control**: restriction on the sale or transfer of technology.
- **ZTE**: Chinese telecom firm temporarily cut off from U.S. components.
- **Technology diffusion**: spread of capabilities across countries and firms.

## Mental Models

- Identify whether an industry’s largest customer is also its strategic competitor.
- Treat “globalization” as a claim to test against actual ownership and control.
- Use choke points when they are concentrated, enforceable, and tied to allied suppliers.

## Anti-patterns

- **Repeating “run faster” without measuring the lead**.
- **Reducing technology policy to tariffs** when the real leverage sits in software, tools, and IP.

## Worked Example

When the Commerce Department restored restrictions on ZTE in 2018, the company faced collapse because it depended on U.S. chips. President Trump later traded relief for a fine, while national-security officials saw that the same supply-chain dependency could be a far more powerful strategic weapon.

## Key Takeaways

1. Make semiconductor mastery explicit in competitive strategy.
2. Measure technology diffusion and control, not only trade flows.
3. Separate short-term bargaining from durable export-control policy.
