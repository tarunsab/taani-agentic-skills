# Chapter 9: The Transistor Salesman

## Core Idea

Japan’s postwar semiconductor rise was deliberately linked to the American-led order. The U.S. chose a strong Japan as a better strategic risk than a weak one, while Japanese officials and businesses used access to American technology and markets to rebuild prosperity. Akio Morita’s Sony illustrates the difference between licensing and copying: Sony licensed the transistor, found consumer uses, priced products aggressively, and marketed them globally. Japanese firms initially relied on U.S. chip designs and intellectual property while mastering product integration and manufacturing. The relationship was symbiotic but political: trade and investment tied Japan to Washington even as they built a future competitor.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **License it**: acquire a technology legally, then create value through product design, market selection, and execution.
  - When to use: when the underlying component is available but customer demand is not yet obvious.
- **Strategic interdependence**: use commercial ties to strengthen an alliance while accepting that the partner will gain capability.
  - When to use: when economic development and geopolitical alignment reinforce each other.

## Key Concepts

- **Akio Morita**: Sony cofounder who championed consumer uses for transistors.
- **Sony**: Japanese electronics firm that turned licensed technology into global products.
- **MITI**: Japan’s powerful Ministry of International Trade and Industry.
- **Consumer market**: high-volume demand that eventually funded chip expansion.

## Mental Models

- Distinguish component leadership from product and market leadership.
- Treat alliance tradeoffs dynamically: a partner can become both customer and competitor.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming a licensee remains permanently subordinate**: product innovation can move value downstream.
- **Using protectionism without a strategic goal**: U.S. tolerance of Japanese growth was part of alliance strategy, not simple laissez-faire.

## Worked Example

Sony licensed transistor technology from AT&T, then ignored the prediction that transistors were useful mainly for hearing aids. It built transistor radios and later consumer calculators, while TI—despite having the technology—failed to market its own products effectively.

## Key Takeaways

1. Look for value in application and marketing, not only invention.
2. Technology transfer can strengthen an alliance and create a rival.
3. Let customers and product design reveal markets that component makers miss.
