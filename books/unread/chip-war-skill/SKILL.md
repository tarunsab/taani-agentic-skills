# Chip War: The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology

**Author**: Chris Miller  
**Source**: *Chip War: The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology* (Scribner, 2022)

## Purpose

Use this skill to reason about semiconductors as technical systems, industrial ecosystems, and instruments of geopolitical power. It is especially useful for analyzing dependencies across design, electronic-design automation, equipment, materials, fabrication, packaging, assembly, and end systems. The book is a historical narrative, so the chapter notes preserve its examples while extracting reusable mechanisms and decision patterns. For claims that depend on events after the book's 2022 publication, verify current facts separately.

## Core frameworks

### 1. Silicon over steel

The book's strategic shift is from mass and metal toward computation, sensing, communications, and precision. A smaller force with better chips can detect, target, coordinate, and guide more effectively than a larger force using older systems. The same logic applies commercially: better computing changes what products can do and lowers the cost of many kinds of information processing. Use the framework by tracing the chip-dependent functions inside a system rather than treating “technology” as an abstract sector.

### 2. Moore's Law as an ecosystem

The continuing fall in the cost of computing is not a single inventor's achievement. It requires coordinated progress in device physics, lithography, process control, materials, design software, finance, customers, and manufacturing yield. Each generation is expensive, so firms need large markets and credible launch customers to fund the next one. Use this framework when assessing whether an apparent technical lead can be reproduced, scaled, and paid for.

### 3. Chokepoints and supply-chain statecraft

Semiconductor production is globally distributed but not evenly replaceable. A small number of firms control especially difficult steps such as advanced lithography, leading-edge fabrication, design software, high-end processor design, and some memory categories. Dependence on those nodes can become leverage in trade, alliance management, and export controls. Map the chain, identify the least substitutable step, and distinguish commercial exposure from actual control over the bottleneck.

### 4. The offset strategy

When an opponent has more mass or lower costs, a state can seek an asymmetric advantage in sensing, precision, networking, stealth, or automation. The U.S. defense establishment repeatedly used this logic to turn semiconductor advances into military leverage. An offset works only when the technology is fielded reliably and integrated into doctrine, logistics, and training. Use it to test whether an innovation changes the contest or merely improves a component.

### 5. Copying versus capability

The Soviet and Chinese cases show that a blueprint, sample, or stolen design is not the same as a working industrial base. Reproduction depends on tacit process knowledge, equipment, materials, maintenance, yield learning, and a market that rewards iteration. Copying can accelerate catch-up, but it rarely removes the need to build the surrounding ecosystem. Use this distinction when evaluating imitation, technology transfer, reverse engineering, or industrial policy.

### 6. The pure-play foundry and the Grand Alliance

Morris Chang's foundry model separated chip design from fabrication and let many designers share the enormous cost of a fab. TSMC's success depended on trusted neutrality, process discipline, customer service, and a dense network of equipment and materials suppliers. This “Grand Alliance” made specialization productive without making any participant self-sufficient. Use the framework when deciding whether a platform should own production, specialize, or coordinate a network.

### 7. Disruption, margins, and organizational renewal

The most dangerous incumbent is often the one that protects its existing profit pool. Intel repeatedly faced the temptation to optimize its dominant franchise while new markets grew elsewhere; Apple and Nvidia illustrate the value of designing around a strategic bottleneck instead. Ask what a replacement leader would stop funding, what customer or architecture is being neglected, and which capability is becoming a constraint. Use this framework for technology transitions where the old business is still profitable.

### 8. Globalization, Taiwanization, and selective decoupling

Globalization spread production across borders, but specialization concentrated control in a few locations and firms. Taiwan became central because it combined engineering talent, government support, manufacturing density, and TSMC's trusted foundry model. The resulting efficiency is also strategic vulnerability: a blockade, conflict, or export-control cascade would affect the entire world. The book therefore points toward selective resilience—protect critical chokepoints and allies—rather than assuming either complete autarky or frictionless globalization.

1. Start with the Introduction for the system map and with `patterns.md` for reusable decision patterns.

3. When making a current claim, separate the book's historical account from post-2022 facts and verify the latter.
4. Preserve the book's distinctions: design is not fabrication, copying is not capability, efficiency is not resilience, and a national customer base is not the same as national control.
