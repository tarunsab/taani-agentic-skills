# Patterns

These patterns translate the book's historical cases into reusable questions. They are analytical aids, not claims that every industry should copy the semiconductor industry.

## 1. Buy the launch customer

**When to use**: When a new technology is too expensive or unfamiliar for a normal market to adopt.

**How**: Find a customer with an urgent mission, fund an early product around that mission, and use its volume and feedback to improve the process. Defense procurement helped make early integrated circuits reliable enough for broader markets (Chs. 5, 11, 14).

**Trade-offs**: A protected customer can finance learning, but it can also pull the product toward narrow requirements or make the supplier dependent on political budgets.

## 2. Separate the recipe from the artifact

**When to use**: When evaluating copying, licensing, espionage, or technology transfer.

**How**: Ask for the entire production recipe: equipment, materials, process sequence, maintenance, operators, test data, and yield-learning loop. The Soviet experience and the Fujian Jinhua case show that a copied design or stolen files do not automatically create competitive production (Chs. 7–8, 25, 50).

**Trade-offs**: Reverse engineering can shorten the path to competence, but it can consume capital while leaving tacit know-how and supplier relationships missing.

## 3. Treat yield as a product feature

**When to use**: When comparing a laboratory breakthrough with a manufacturable technology.

**How**: Track working units, defect sources, process variation, cycle time, and learning per production run—not just feature size or theoretical performance. Fairchild, Intel, Samsung, and TSMC gained advantage by turning process discipline into predictable cost (Chs. 4–5, 13, 23, 37).

**Trade-offs**: Yield work is slow and unglamorous, yet skipping it makes scale uneconomic and creates false confidence in demonstrations.

## 4. Specialize the expensive layer

**When to use**: When a system has one layer with huge fixed costs and many potential users.

**How**: Let specialized designers share a neutral manufacturing platform instead of forcing every designer to own a fab. TSMC's pure-play foundry model expanded the market for advanced chip design while concentrating process expertise (Chs. 21, 35–37).

**Trade-offs**: Specialization lowers duplication but increases dependence on the platform and makes its location a strategic concern.

## 5. Build the Grand Alliance

**When to use**: When a capability depends on many complementary suppliers and skills.

**How**: Align customers, equipment makers, materials suppliers, universities, investors, engineers, and government around a learning roadmap. The value comes from synchronized iteration, not from any single subsidy or firm (Chs. 29–32, 37, 39).

**Trade-offs**: Dense ecosystems are productive but difficult to replicate quickly and vulnerable when one partner becomes a chokepoint.

## 6. Use an offset, not a mirror

**When to use**: When matching an opponent's scale or cost directly is unattractive.

**How**: Identify the rival's structural advantage, then target a different dimension—precision, sensing, communications, stealth, software, or autonomy. U.S. military planners used chips to make accuracy and information substitute for mass (Chs. 11, 14, 26–28, 48).

**Trade-offs**: An offset can change the contest, but only if it survives procurement, integration, training, and countermeasures.

## 7. Copy exactly before improving

**When to use**: When a process is complex and the organization is tempted to “improve” it before it understands it.

**How**: Reproduce the complete process, document deviations, and change one variable at a time. The book uses copying to show that small undocumented differences in equipment, materials, or maintenance can destroy yield (Ch. 8).

**Trade-offs**: Exact copying accelerates learning but can preserve obsolete assumptions; improvement should follow understanding, not replace it.

## 8. Disrupt yourself before the market does

**When to use**: When an incumbent's current product is profitable but a new architecture is eroding its future.

**How**: Ask what a replacement CEO would stop protecting, which customer is being underserved, and which capability is becoming the bottleneck. Intel's missed transitions and Apple's move to custom silicon illustrate the cost of defending the old franchise (Chs. 33, 38, 41).

**Trade-offs**: Cannibalizing a profitable product is painful, and the replacement may fail; refusing to try transfers the decision to competitors.

## 9. Read commodity cycles contrarianly

**When to use**: When a mature component market looks unattractive because prices have collapsed.

**How**: Separate temporary oversupply from permanent demand destruction, then invest in cost, yield, and scale when weaker rivals are exiting. Japanese and Korean memory firms used different versions of this logic across the cycle (Chs. 18–23).

**Trade-offs**: The strategy requires cash and patience; timing can be wrong and the next downturn can arrive before learning pays off.

## 10. Map the choke point before imposing controls

**When to use**: When using export controls, sanctions, procurement restrictions, or alliance leverage.

**How**: Locate the indispensable node, identify allied ownership and substitutes, model spillovers, and define an enforceable rule. U.S. actions involving ZTE, Fujian Jinhua, and Huawei worked because they targeted dependencies in chips, equipment, software, or access to foundries (Chs. 49–51).

**Trade-offs**: Controls can slow a rival and buy time, but overuse encourages substitution, harms domestic firms, and may fragment the ecosystem.

## 11. Separate demand shocks from supply shocks

**When to use**: When a shortage or price spike is attributed to one simple cause.

**How**: Decompose changes in end demand, inventory, cancellations, capacity allocation, logistics, and manufacturing constraints. Miller's COVID account shows how auto cancellations followed by an electronics boom interacted with just-in-time supply chains (Ch. 53).

**Trade-offs**: Detailed diagnosis takes longer than a slogan, but it prevents expensive capacity decisions based on a temporary signal.

## 12. Protect the network, not just the national factory

**When to use**: When designing resilience or industrial policy for a globally specialized industry.

**How**: Secure the full chain—design tools, equipment, materials, fabs, packaging, logistics, talent, and allied access—while prioritizing nodes that cannot be replaced quickly. The Taiwan dilemma demonstrates why a domestic fab alone cannot recreate the ecosystem (Chs. 40, 54–55).

**Trade-offs**: Redundancy costs money and may reduce efficiency; selective resilience is more achievable than complete autarky.
