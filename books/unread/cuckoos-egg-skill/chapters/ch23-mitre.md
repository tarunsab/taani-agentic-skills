# Chapter 23: Mitre

## Core Idea

Stoll warns Dennis Hall and the other agencies that the trace points toward Mitre, a defense contractor near McLean and the CIA. Dan Stanford independently follows a related hacker toward the same area, while Mike Gibbons explains that the U.S. computer-fraud statute can apply even when prosecution and extradition are difficult. A November session defeats the first attempt to trace Mitre's internal packet network, so Stoll tests the “Mitre as stepping-stone” theory rather than relying on geography. The chapter combines corroboration from another investigator with a reminder that legal jurisdiction and technical origin remain separate questions.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Independent corroboration
- Gateway-versus-origin distinction
- Statutory framing

## Key Concepts

- Mitre
- Packet network
- Computer Fraud and Abuse Act
- McLean
- Dan Stanford

## Mental Models

- Two observers, one pattern
- A network can hide its own internal route

## Anti-patterns

- Assume two similar traces are automatically the same case
- Treat a statute as a technical trace

## Worked Example

Stanford's work makes the McLean lead more interesting, but not conclusive, because the two intruders may still be distinct. Mitre's internal network hides the last hop, demonstrating that a visible modem can be only the front door of a larger system. Stoll responds by obtaining bills and looking for correlated destinations, a slower method that can expose the stepping-stone's role.

## Key Takeaways

- Seek independent observations before merging cases.
- Model internal packet routing as an uncertainty source.
- Use law to guide cooperation, not to substitute for technical evidence.
