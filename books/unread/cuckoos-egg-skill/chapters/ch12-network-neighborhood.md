# Chapter 12: The Network Neighborhood

## Core Idea

Stoll maps the emerging network neighborhood: Milnet, White Sands, military gateways, and the systems that let an intruder move between institutions. The hacker searches for military and Strategic Defense Initiative material, tries common account names, and sometimes reaches a system without immediately obtaining useful data. Stoll must explain the case to the FBI, DCA, AFOSI, DOE, and other groups whose responsibilities overlap but do not align. Martha challenges his cooperation with the military, forcing him to distinguish the defensive purpose of the investigation from any political endorsement of the institutions being protected.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Network topology as context
- Mission-versus-means distinction
- Cross-agency coordination

## Key Concepts

- Milnet
- White Sands
- NIC
- Default credentials
- Bailiwick

## Mental Models

- The network neighborhood
- Responsibility without ownership

## Anti-patterns

- Treat every reachable host as equally important
- Confuse protecting a system with endorsing its mission

## Worked Example

The intruder's searches are more revealing than the occasional failed login: he repeatedly chooses military keywords and gateways, suggesting a consistent target interest. Yet Stoll cannot infer the final motive from search terms alone. He therefore shares technical facts, keeps watching, and accepts that the investigation itself will cross political boundaries that his own job description never anticipated.

## Key Takeaways

- Map routes and targets separately.
- State your defensive objective in terms others can evaluate.
- Coordinate facts across organizations without assuming coordination will happen automatically.
