# Chapter 21: Responsible

## Core Idea

When DOE and other organizations say the incident is not their bailiwick, Stoll confronts the difference between formal responsibility and practical responsibility. The network resembles a shared utility: an intrusion at one node can affect people who never agreed to participate, while no single institution owns the whole route. Stoll decides that caring about the problem is itself a choice, even though he cannot guarantee a successful arrest or protect every system. The chapter is therefore less about a new tool than about accepting stewardship when a boundary failure has wider consequences.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Responsibility beyond mandate
- Shared-infrastructure model
- Stewardship under uncertainty

## Key Concepts

- Bailiwick
- DOE
- Network utility
- System manager
- Trust damage

## Mental Models

- The network as public utility
- Responsibility as a decision

## Anti-patterns

- Wait for a formal owner before warning others
- Assume technical reach implies authority to fix every node

## Worked Example

Stoll keeps receiving requests for information from agencies that do not offer to run the investigation. He could close his own system and declare victory, but that would leave the attacker free to move elsewhere. The source does not romanticize this choice: it costs time and creates personal strain, yet the decision follows from the shared nature of the network.

## Key Takeaways

- Define the affected community before defining the responsible office.
- Warn downstream owners even when the case is inconvenient.
- Acknowledge the limits of voluntary stewardship while still acting on it.
