# Chapter 40: Bait

## Core Idea

Martha turns the problem of a short, badly timed session into a design problem: create enough plausible material to keep the hacker connected for two hours, the time a German technician may need to trace a manual telephone exchange. Stoll and Claudia build the SDINET decoy from modified bureaucratic documents, invented correspondence, a fictional secretary, and carefully restricted files. The documents avoid sensational language and instead imitate the repetitive, low-level paperwork that makes a large defense project believable. The bait is a measurement instrument: it is useful only if it produces a long, observable session without exposing real secrets.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Time-extension bait
- Plausible decoy design
- Controlled exposure

## Key Concepts

- Fictitious documents
- SDINET
- Barbara Sherwin
- Manual exchange
- Restricted file

## Mental Models

- Boring detail as camouflage
- A decoy as an instrument

## Anti-patterns

- Put real classified data in the trap
- Make the bait obviously theatrical or overly informative

## Worked Example

The team invents a project office, creates ordinary-looking requests and memos, and limits access so that the decoy will be invisible to normal users. A form letter may invite a response, but the main experiment does not depend on the hacker writing back. The source emphasizes that plausibility, restraint, and access control are what make the bait informative rather than reckless.

## Key Takeaways

- Design the bait around the observation window you need.
- Use synthetic or safely altered material.
- Make the decoy believable through ordinary operational detail.
