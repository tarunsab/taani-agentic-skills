# Chapter 38: The Medical Computer

## Core Idea

The Air Force blocks the hacker's stolen account, but he keeps returning to LBL and trying to regain the same path, showing that an error message can be a deliberate containment device rather than a public warning. Stoll's Bevatron computer then appears in the monitor, and its system manager confirms that an intruder is creating accounts with super-user authority. Because the Bevatron controls particle beams used both for physics and cancer treatment, an operating-system compromise could have consequences far beyond lost data. The chapter makes Stoll choose continued observation over simply closing LBL, while the local FBI argues that unclassified, nonmonetary damage is not enough to justify a major case.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Containment by controlled failure
- Safety-critical context
- Impact beyond data loss

## Key Concepts

- Bevatron
- Cancer treatment
- Super-user account
- Air Force lockout
- FBI threshold

## Mental Models

- A computer as part of a physical process
- The locked door that still reveals pursuit

## Anti-patterns

- Describe a safety-critical system as an ordinary host
- Wait for classified loss before treating compromise as dangerous

## Worked Example

Chuck McParland verifies that no authorized administrator created the Bevatron accounts, then changes the passwords and closes the intruder's access. Stoll realizes that the hacker may not understand what the machine controls, which is precisely why labels and monitoring matter. Fred Wyniken's skepticism shows the gap between technical danger and the legal threshold used to allocate investigative resources.

## Key Takeaways

- Identify physical consequences of privileged access.
- Protect safety-critical systems even when the intruder's intent is unknown.
- Do not use monetary loss as the only measure of security impact.
