# Chapter 46: Political Boundaries

## Core Idea

At home, Laurie challenges Stoll's cooperation with military agencies, asking whether pursuing a spy makes him an instrument of policies he may oppose. Stoll tests the decoy with political, financial, games, and SDI material, and the hacker's selective attention to SDI files suggests a target preference rather than random curiosity. A later route through Caltech and LBL reaches Tinker and Optimis-related systems, including a medical computer, while rumors begin circulating and Stoll must tell lab staff enough to protect them without exposing the investigation. The chapter keeps ethics, technical evidence, and personal strain in the same frame: motives matter, but the immediate responsibility is to reduce harm and understand what is happening.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Ethical boundary setting
- Controlled-interest experiment
- Need-to-know disclosure

## Key Concepts

- Decoy categories
- Caltech
- Tinker
- Optimis
- Lab secrecy

## Mental Models

- Means versus ends
- A controlled decoy as an ethical probe

## Anti-patterns

- Assume defensive cooperation is politically neutral
- Reveal sensitive details simply to relieve pressure

## Worked Example

The fake files let Stoll compare the hacker's attention across topics without sacrificing real secrets. The repeated focus on SDI material strengthens the inference that the intruder has a particular objective, though it does not explain who commissioned it. When the case becomes a workplace rumor, Stoll shifts from private investigator to team steward and communicates the operational risk directly.

## Key Takeaways

- State the boundary between protecting systems and endorsing missions.
- Use controlled content to test interest, not to bait with real secrets.
- Share only what colleagues need to act safely.
