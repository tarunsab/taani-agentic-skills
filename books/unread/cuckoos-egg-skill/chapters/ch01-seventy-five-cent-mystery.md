# Chapter 1: The Seventy-Five-Cent Mystery

## Core Idea

Stoll enters Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory's computer center as an astronomer who has been moved into systems work, and the first clue is an apparently trivial accounting discrepancy. Two overlapping accounting systems disagree by seventy-five cents, and the missing charge belongs to a user called Hunter with no billing address. When Stoll removes that account, a login attempt appears under the name of Joe Sventek, a former LBL Unix expert now in England, and the timestamps do not agree cleanly because the machines' clocks drift. The incident teaches the book's first investigative rule: a small, reproducible anomaly can be more valuable than a dramatic suspicion.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Anomaly as lead
- Reproducible observation
- Timestamp skepticism

## Key Concepts

- Accounting mismatch
- Hunter account
- Clock drift
- Sventek identity
- LBL computer center

## Mental Models

- The small discrepancy
- The ledger as sensor

## Anti-patterns

- Dismiss a small unexplained discrepancy as harmless
- Assume a login name identifies the person at the keyboard

## Worked Example

The seventy-five-cent charge is not treated as proof of espionage. Stoll checks the account, notices the missing billing information, and watches what happens after he removes it. The resulting Sventek login turns a bookkeeping irregularity into a security question, but the connection remains provisional because clock drift and reused credentials can distort the first story.

## Key Takeaways

- Preserve the original observation before changing the system.
- Treat identity, time, and accounting data as hypotheses to test.
- Start with the smallest fact that can be independently checked.
