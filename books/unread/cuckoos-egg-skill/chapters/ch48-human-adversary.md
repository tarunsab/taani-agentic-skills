# Chapter 48: The Human Adversary

## Core Idea

Stoll visits NSA, Justice, NTISSIC, and CIA contacts to explain the case and ask how a government should respond to a network intruder who is not a conventional military opponent. The organizations describe computer-security testing, compartmentalization, and isolated systems, but they also reveal uncertainty about who owns a live incident and how much responsibility can be delegated. Stoll reframes the attacker as a human adversary whose choices, errors, and persistence can be studied, while refusing to turn technical skill into a moral verdict. A story about a contractor who wiretapped a terminal switch reinforces the danger of insiders and of systems whose physical trust assumptions are invisible to users.

## Mental Models

- The adversary inside the abstraction
- Security as a social system

## Anti-patterns

- Reduce the attacker to a clever machine
- Assume isolation eliminates insider or boundary risk

## Worked Example

The meetings give Stoll a clearer view of how national-security organizations think about computer security, but they do not hand him a ready-made response. The contractor story shows that a trusted technical position can be abused without a dramatic break-in. Stoll leaves with stronger arguments for continued observation and for a policy that treats computer security as a human and institutional problem.

## Key Takeaways

- Model people, trust, and incentives alongside software.
- Use compartmentalization to limit consequences, not to deny incidents.
- Turn unusual cases into policy evidence without generalizing beyond the data.
