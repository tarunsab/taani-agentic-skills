# Chapter 16: Research, Not Revenge

## Core Idea

The intruder reaches a Livermore Cray-related system and uses a hidden path through the lab's network, but Livermore closes its account and declines to support a trace. Stoll's meeting with Luis Alvarez changes his framing: instead of pursuing revenge, he should treat the case as research by gathering observations, testing methods, and recording results. That shift does not make the work emotionally neutral, but it gives him a durable reason to continue when agencies or system managers refuse cooperation. The chapter also shows that shutting down individual paths can redirect the intruder to another institution, so local closure is not the same as campaign containment.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Research framing
- System boundary mapping
- Local closure versus campaign persistence

## Key Concepts

- Livermore
- Cray

## Mental Models

- The incident as an experiment
- A closed door moves the experiment elsewhere

## Anti-patterns

- Pursue the intruder as a personal contest
- Treat one site's account closure as a solved campaign

## Worked Example

Stoll records the Livermore route because it demonstrates reach and trust relationships, even though the account is soon disabled. Alvarez's advice helps him see the case as a body of evidence that can be analyzed and communicated. When the hacker later touches MIT and SRI, the new targets confirm that the campaign is broader than any one lab.

## Key Takeaways

- Use a research question to resist emotional drift.
- Record negative results and blocked cooperation.
- Ask how a local defensive change changes the attacker's route.
