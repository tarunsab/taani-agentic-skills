# Chapter 14: Calling the Spooks

## Core Idea

The hacker's searches point toward CIA-related routes, so Stoll calls the agency rather than keeping the clue to himself. The call introduces Ed Manning and a style of information gathering that is less cinematic than Stoll expected: the agency wants technical details, but its legal and operational authority differs from the FBI's. Stoll learns that calling the “spooks” does not solve the case; it adds another perspective and another boundary. The chapter's value is procedural—when a clue crosses domains, contact the organization that can interpret it, while keeping the claim narrower than the suspicion.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Cross-domain escalation
- Clue-to-contact mapping
- Scope control

## Key Concepts

- CIA
- FBI
- Computer route
- Information request
- Operational boundary

## Mental Models

- The clue as a bridge
- Every agency sees a different slice

## Anti-patterns

- Keep a relevant agency uninformed to preserve control
- Treat agency interest as proof of the theory

## Worked Example

Stoll's call is prompted by observed network behavior, not by a desire to recruit a secret ally. The CIA asks questions and helps interpret the significance of systems, but it does not take over the technical monitoring or the legal trace. That division of labor preserves both usefulness and uncertainty.

## Key Takeaways

- Escalate clues to the domain owner.
- Separate interpretation from operational control.
- Do not mistake a recipient's interest for confirmation.
