# Chapter 32: The Profile

## Core Idea

Stoll reports the German route and military activity to the FBI, AFOSI, CIA, NSA, and other contacts, while Bob Orr connects the case to reports from Toronto, Fermilab, and the Chaos computer community. He then drafts a behavioral profile: the hacker is persistent, methodical, technically capable across Unix and VMS, interested in military and defense-contractor systems, and likely a person because of typing mistakes. Zeke challenges the assumptions, asking whether a program could perform the same routine and whether the operator must be in Germany, which forces Stoll to state the limits of his inference. A false beeper alarm at the end turns caution into a concrete rule: verify before broadcasting.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Behavioral profiling
- Adversarial questioning
- Verification before escalation

## Key Concepts

- Methodical behavior
- Typing errors
- Unix/VMS fluency
- Chaos
- False alarm

## Mental Models

- The profile as a hypothesis table
- A beeper alarm has a confidence level

## Anti-patterns

- Present guesses as facts
- Escalate an unverified alarm to every agency

## Worked Example

The profile is built from repeated observations rather than from a psychological story. Stoll can support expertise, schedule, targets, and habits, but age, occupation, and location remain estimates. When the beeper triggers because of an electrical event, he sees how quickly a false positive could damage hard-won credibility, so the monitoring loop gains a confirmation step.

## Key Takeaways

- Separate direct observations from inferred traits.
- Invite skeptical questions that could falsify the profile.
- Confirm alerts before widening the audience.
