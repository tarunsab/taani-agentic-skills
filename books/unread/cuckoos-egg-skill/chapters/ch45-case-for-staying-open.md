# Chapter 45: The Case for Staying Open

## Core Idea

At a Bolling Air Force Base meeting, roughly twenty agencies debate whether Stoll should close the monitored system or continue collecting evidence. The FBI and Justice Department emphasize weak criminal consequences, no obvious classified loss, and extradition difficulty, while DOE and other technical groups emphasize the damage to trust and the possibility of future harm. Bob Morris takes Stoll to NSA, where Harry Daniels treats the logbook and accumulated traces as a documented case that exposes a policy gap. The chapter turns the operational choice into a public-interest argument: an open network needs a way to respond before every consequence is classified or catastrophic.

## Mental Models

- The case as a policy experiment
- Trust damage as an impact measure

## Anti-patterns

- Measure harm only in dollars or classified labels
- Let a jurisdictional obstacle decide the technical risk

## Worked Example

The meeting shows that different agencies use different thresholds for action, so agreement on the facts does not guarantee agreement on the response. Morris's support matters because it connects Stoll's local records to national computer-security policy. Staying open remains a controlled, contested choice rather than a default permission to expose every system.

## Key Takeaways

- Put competing thresholds on the table.
- Preserve a documented case that policy makers can evaluate.
- Include trust and future exposure in impact assessment.
