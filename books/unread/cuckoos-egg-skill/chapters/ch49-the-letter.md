# Chapter 49: The Letter

## Core Idea

The fictitious SDINET files produce the first direct human response: a letter from Pittsburgh signed by Laszlo J. Balogh asks for the promised material. Stoll preserves the envelope and compares the request with the one fake file the hacker actually read, allowing the FBI to connect the physical letter to the network behavior without claiming that the signature alone proves the case. The Hungarian name, misspellings, and three-month delay become clues that the agencies can use while German and U.S. authorities keep working. Claudia receives credit for the form-letter idea, illustrating that good investigation often comes from collaborative, ordinary suggestions rather than a single heroic insight.

## Mental Models

- The canary that writes back

## Anti-patterns

- Alter or discard the returned evidence
- Treat a matching name as a complete identity

## Worked Example

The letter matters because it is an independent channel that echoes the bait's fictional details. Stoll sends it to the FBI and keeps the original materials intact, while investigators interpret the writing and timing alongside the logs. The case shifts from “someone read our decoy” to “someone may be trying to use the decoy's promised path,” which is a sharper but still bounded claim.

## Key Takeaways

- Plant canaries that create observable, low-risk callbacks.
- Preserve physical and digital evidence together.
- Use cross-channel agreement to strengthen a hypothesis without erasing uncertainty.
