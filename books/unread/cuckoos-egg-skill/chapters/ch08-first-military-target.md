# Chapter 8: The First Military Target

## Core Idea

The intruder moves through Berkeley into Milnet and reaches an Army computer at Anniston, turning a local accounting puzzle into a national security concern. Chuck McNatt has already seen the name Hunter, while phone recorders fail to capture the network's actual entry point, showing the limits of conventional telephone evidence. Stoll reports the activity to the Air Force, the National Computer Security Center, the FBI, and other agencies, but encounters questions about jurisdiction, classification, and whether the work belongs to civilian science or military counterintelligence. The chapter preserves uncertainty: Anniston proves a target and a route, not the attacker's nationality or motive.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Escalation by impact
- Network-versus-telephone visibility
- Jurisdictional boundary

## Key Concepts

- Milnet
- Anniston Army Depot

## Mental Models

- The network as a new geography
- Impact outruns jurisdiction

## Anti-patterns

- Infer foreign espionage from the first military target
- Assume one agency owns a cross-boundary incident

## Worked Example

The path into Anniston is compelling enough to prompt military attention, yet no one can simply point to a single phone call and name the intruder. Stoll therefore shares the observations widely while continuing to monitor the Berkeley host. Martha's warning to watch his step captures the double risk: the technical case is growing, but his informal cooperation with military organizations could change how others interpret his motives.

## Key Takeaways

- Separate target, route, identity, and motive.
- Escalate when the consequence changes, even if attribution remains incomplete.
- Expect cross-organizational incidents to create ownership gaps.
