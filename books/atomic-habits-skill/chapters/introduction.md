# Introduction — My Story

## Central idea

Lasting change is usually less about a dramatic breakthrough than about a system that makes small improvements repeatable. A tiny behavior can look insignificant in isolation yet become consequential when repeated and allowed to compound. The delay between an action and its visible result makes early progress easy to underestimate. This is why a workable routine, a reliable cue, and a way to notice repetition matter more than a short burst of enthusiasm. The framework treats behavior change as a design problem: shape the conditions around the action so that progress is easier to repeat.

## Apply it

When a user brings an ambitious goal, translate it into the smallest behavior that would still count as showing up. Then design the surrounding cue, environment, and reward. The minimum should be meaningful enough to reinforce the direction but small enough to survive a difficult day. Keep the focus on the next repetition while preserving a longer review horizon, because the daily action and the eventual outcome move at different speeds. Use the early repetitions to learn which part of the system needs adjustment rather than treating them as a final verdict on the person's ability.

## Coaching prompt

“What result matters, what repeated behavior would move it forward, and what is the smallest version you could do on a difficult day?”

This prompt separates the desired result from the behavior that can actually be scheduled. It also exposes whether the proposed habit depends on ideal energy, perfect timing, or an unexamined assumption about motivation. Ask for an observable action and a realistic cue before adding tracking or accountability. The answer should leave the user with a next repetition they could perform today.

## Guardrail

Compounding is a useful model, not a promise. Results also depend on the quality of the behavior, time, health, resources, and external conditions. A small action can compound in an unhelpful direction if the system reinforces the wrong behavior or measures the wrong outcome. Avoid presenting the framework as a substitute for medical, financial, or other qualified advice when those domains are involved. Use it to improve the design of repeated actions while remaining honest about uncertainty and constraints.
