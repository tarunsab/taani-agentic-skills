# Chapter 14 — How to Make Good Habits Inevitable and Bad Habits Impossible

## Core lesson

A commitment device is a present choice that makes a future behavior more likely. It changes the future choice architecture so that the desired response is easier or the unwanted response is harder when the moment arrives. Automation and one-time decisions can lock in beneficial defaults and reduce the need for daily willpower. This is especially useful for repeated decisions whose long-term value is clear but whose immediate temptation is strong. A good commitment is proportionate, understood in advance, and revisable when the user's circumstances or aims genuinely change.

## Use it

Automate recurring actions where appropriate: schedule transfers, use defaults, pre-commit time, or remove access to a tempting option. For an unwanted habit, invert the law by making the response difficult or impossible in the target context. Prefer one-time setup when it eliminates many future choices without creating a hidden cost. Check that the default is still aligned with what the user actually values, because automation can scale a bad decision as efficiently as a good one. Build an escape or review point when the commitment affects health, money, safety, or important relationships.

## Playbook

Look for a decision that recurs often enough to justify a one-time intervention. Decide whether the best commitment changes the default, removes access, schedules the action, or adds a meaningful delay. Estimate the downside if the choice becomes wrong, and prefer a reversible version when uncertainty is high. Explain the commitment to the future self who will encounter it so that the consequence is not surprising or punitive. Review whether the device is still serving the identity and outcome that motivated it.

1. Identify a repeated decision that can be made once.
2. Choose a reversible, proportionate commitment where possible.
3. Automate or pre-arrange the desired default.
4. Test that the commitment does not create harmful rigidity.

## Pitfall

Automating a poorly chosen behavior can multiply its consequences while making the mistake less visible. A commitment that is unsafe, financially damaging, or impossible to revise is not disciplined design; it is an avoidable loss of flexibility. Another problem is choosing a device that works only by creating fear, shame, or excessive punishment. Use commitment to support a considered choice, not to remove the user's ability to respond to new information. Build review and reversibility into the system whenever the stakes are meaningful.
