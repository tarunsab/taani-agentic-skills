# Chapter 12 — The Law of Least Effort

## Core lesson

People tend to choose the option requiring less work. A small amount of friction can determine which of two otherwise similar actions happens, especially when the user is tired, rushed, or undecided. Friction includes physical effort, number of steps, waiting, setup, and the need to remember what comes next. This makes convenience a design variable rather than a sign that the person cares less. Reduce friction for the behavior that serves the chosen aim, while adding enough friction to the competing behavior that the default can change.

## Use it

Prime the environment in advance. Prepare materials, reduce clicks, pre-commit choices, and place the desired behavior in the path of least resistance. Add steps, distance, or delay to a competing behavior, but keep the added barrier proportionate so it does not create an unreasonable burden. Look for one-time changes that improve many future repetitions, such as preparing the workspace or changing a default. After implementation, observe where the user still hesitates; the remaining friction is often more informative than the original plan.

## Playbook

Friction shaping begins with a map of the actual path from cue to response. Count the steps and decisions required for both the desired and competing behaviors, including steps that feel too small to mention. Remove the highest-cost step first, because reducing every detail at once can create a complicated redesign that is hard to maintain. Add a modest barrier to the unwanted option and test whether it changes the first choice rather than merely slowing the final action. Keep the design reversible when the consequences are uncertain.

1. Map every step between cue and response.
2. Remove or combine steps for the desired action.
3. Prepare the environment at the end of the previous routine.
4. Add friction to the unwanted action.

## Pitfall

Optimizing a low-value behavior simply because it is easy can create a highly efficient distraction. Ease should serve the user's chosen aim, so first confirm that the target behavior is worth making convenient. Excessive friction removal can also eliminate useful reflection, safety checks, or quality control. Adding barriers to a bad habit may fail when the user has no alternative that meets the same need. Shape effort after clarifying value, and review whether the easier default is producing the result that matters.
