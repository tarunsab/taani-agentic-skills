# Appendix — Applying the Four Laws

## Compact checklist

Use the checklist as a sequence of design questions rather than as four obligations to maximize simultaneously. The first law solves visibility, the second solves attraction, the third solves effort, and the fourth solves repetition. Start with the law that matches the observed bottleneck and add another only when it removes a real obstacle. A small number of well-chosen changes is easier to evaluate than a complete lifestyle redesign. Keep the checklist connected to the user's actual context and desired identity.

### Make it obvious

- Score the current habits.
- Specify time and location.
- Stack the new behavior after a current habit.
- Prepare the environment so the cue is visible.

These techniques create a reliable opportunity to notice the behavior. A cue works best when it is stable, close to the response, and not competing with a stronger signal. The Scorecard is useful before redesign because it reveals the sequence that is already operating. If a cue is visible but the behavior still does not start, move to attractiveness or ease rather than making the cue more elaborate.

### Make it attractive

- Bundle a needed action with an enjoyable one.
- Join a group where the behavior is normal.
- Build a brief positive ritual before starting.

Attractiveness concerns the expected feeling before action, not only the eventual benefit. Pairing, social norms, and rituals can make the first step more inviting without requiring constant excitement. Choose a reward that is available at the cue and compatible with the larger aim. If the reward becomes more compelling than the target behavior, simplify or rebalance the bundle.

### Make it easy

- Reduce friction.
- Prime the environment.
- Protect the decisive opening moment.
- Use a two-minute gateway.
- Automate one-time choices where appropriate.

Ease is about reducing the number of steps between the cue and the response. Prepare the environment before the opportunity arrives, then shrink the gateway until starting no longer requires a debate. Automation is especially useful when one decision can improve many future repetitions, but it should be reviewed when the stakes are high. Do not optimize an action merely because it is convenient; confirm that it is the right action to make convenient.

### Make it satisfying

- Reinforce completion immediately.
- Track a meaningful repetition.
- Use a recovery rule.
- Add consensual accountability when useful.

Immediate satisfaction supplies feedback when the meaningful outcome is delayed. Tracking can make progress visible, while accountability can make a commitment socially real. The recovery rule matters because a missed repetition is information, not a reason to abandon the identity. Keep rewards, measures, and consequences proportionate so that the system supports honest repetition rather than fear of failure.

## Applying the framework to teams and business

Shape defaults, onboarding, interfaces, incentives, and social norms so the desired customer or employee behavior is obvious, attractive, easy, and satisfying. Measure behavior that leads to the outcome, and watch for incentives that create a harmful shortcut. A team should examine the path a person actually follows, including ambiguous cues, approval signals, handoffs, and unnecessary effort. A metric can improve while the underlying outcome worsens if the system rewards a proxy too aggressively. Use the Four Laws to design the default, then review whether the default still produces quality and aligns with the organization's purpose.

## Applying it to parenting

Design the environment and routines around the child's current ability. Model the behavior, make the first step small, reinforce effort and completion, and avoid turning a temporary behavior into a fixed identity. The adult's behavior and the surrounding norms often provide stronger cues than verbal instructions. Consequences should teach the relationship between action and result without relying on humiliation or fear. Adapt the system as the child grows, because a routine that was appropriately easy can later become either boring or unnecessarily controlling.

## Final diagnostic

If a habit plan fails, do not jump straight to a motivation explanation. Check the cue, the predicted reward, the effort required, the immediate feedback, and the fit between the behavior and the person's current context. Ask which link failed first and change that link before adding more complexity. If the same failure returns, inspect whether the underlying need, environment, or identity has been misunderstood. The best diagnosis produces a smaller, clearer experiment for the next repetition.
