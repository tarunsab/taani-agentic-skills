# Chapter 10 — How to Find and Fix the Causes of Your Bad Habits

## Core lesson

An unwanted behavior has a surface craving and a deeper motive. The cue creates a prediction about the feeling the behavior will produce, and the prediction can be more influential than the actual outcome. Someone may seek the behavior for relief, stimulation, connection, certainty, or rest even when the behavior later creates a cost. Change becomes easier when the motive is understood because a replacement can address the same need instead of merely blocking the visible response. Reframing the behavior makes the full trade-off more salient: the immediate benefit is no longer the only part of the prediction. The goal is not to deny desire, but to update the story that makes the response attractive.

## Use it

Ask: “What state am I trying to change?” The answer may be relief from stress, stimulation, connection, certainty, or rest. Record the feeling before the behavior and compare it with the actual feeling afterward, because the gap often reveals the misleading part of the prediction. Then design a replacement that addresses the same need with less downstream cost. Make the benefit of the replacement visible and the cost of the unwanted behavior specific rather than relying on a vague warning. If the replacement does not satisfy the real motive, the old loop will remain attractive even when the user understands it intellectually.

## Playbook

Treat the unwanted habit as a solution that has become too costly or too automatic. Map the cue, the expected change in state, the response, and the immediate payoff before deciding what to remove. Ask whether the main lever is cue exposure, the meaning attached to the behavior, the effort required for an alternative, or the lack of an immediate consequence. Reframe the response so that avoiding it has a visible benefit, then make a replacement available at the same moment. Test the replacement against the actual motive rather than against an idealized version of what the user “should” want.

1. Record the cue and the feeling before the behavior.
2. State the expected benefit and the actual after-effect.
3. Reframe the unwanted behavior around its cost or the benefit of not doing it.
4. Pair the replacement with a positive feeling or ritual.

## Pitfall

Fighting the visible behavior while leaving the underlying need untouched often produces substitution or rebound. Reframing is not denial; it is making the full trade-off easier to see before the response is chosen. Another failure is to exaggerate the cost until the user stops believing the message, which makes the intervention less credible. A replacement that is technically healthy but emotionally irrelevant will not compete at the decisive moment. Keep the explanation accurate, the alternative accessible, and the test focused on whether the underlying state can be changed in a better way.
