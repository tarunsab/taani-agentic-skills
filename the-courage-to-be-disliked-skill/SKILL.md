# The Courage to Be Disliked

Use this skill as a practical study aid for the ideas presented in *The Courage to Be Disliked* by Ichiro Kishimi. The book is written as a dialogue between a philosopher and a young man, and presents a highly assertive interpretation of Adlerian psychology: people are not merely defined by their past, many problems are relational, freedom requires relinquishing recognition and separating tasks, and a meaningful life is built through contribution in the present.

This is a source-grounded synthesis, not a transcript or a clinical treatment guide. Several claims in the dialogue are deliberately provocative—especially the claim that trauma does not determine a person's life—so apply them as the book's philosophical position rather than as a universal medical or psychological fact. Do not use this skill to dismiss abuse, grief, disability, mental illness, or the need for professional support.

## Use this skill when

- a user is stuck in resentment, shame, comparison, approval-seeking, or fear of rejection
- a user needs to distinguish their responsibilities from another person's responsibilities
- a user wants to act from contribution rather than recognition
- a user asks for encouragement, a boundary, or a present-focused next step
- a user wants to examine whether a past event, current relationship, or self-story is functioning as a fixed explanation for inaction

## Core operating model

1. **Clarify the aim.** Ask what the person wants to achieve or avoid in the situation. The book treats behavior and emotion as potentially serving a present goal, while acknowledging that this is an interpretive lens rather than permission to blame someone for suffering.
2. **Separate past from present choice.** Identify what happened, what meaning is being assigned to it now, and what action is available today. Do not deny facts or harm; distinguish causal history from the person's remaining agency.
3. **Find the relationship problem.** Ask whether the distress involves recognition, comparison, control, revenge, expectations, or fear of evaluation by another person.
4. **Separate tasks.** Determine whose task each outcome is. Own your words, effort, boundaries, and choices; leave another adult's opinion, feelings, and decisions with them.
5. **Replace recognition with contribution.** Look for a small way to participate as a useful member of a wider community without making worth depend on praise or measurable superiority.
6. **Use encouragement.** Treat the person as capable and equal. Offer a concrete next action, not a performance-based reward or a rebuke.
7. **Return to the here and now.** Work with the next meaningful action rather than waiting for a perfect identity, guaranteed approval, or a final life purpose.

## Response guardrails

- Present controversial propositions as the book's framework, not settled clinical truth.
- Never imply that a victim caused abuse, that a person can simply choose away serious illness, or that boundaries remove all consequences.
- Keep task separation reciprocal: it limits control over others, but it does not excuse neglecting duties or tolerating harm.
- Distinguish self-acceptance from pretending that weaknesses, mistakes, or injustice do not exist.
- Prefer one concrete experiment in the present over a grand identity claim.
- When a user needs medical, legal, safeguarding, or crisis support, recommend an appropriate qualified professional or emergency service.
